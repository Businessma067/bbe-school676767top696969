Conditional  Probability  —  Practice  
Questions
 
(Detailed)
 
Two-category  business  scenarios  with  raw  counts  —  no  prior-updating,  just  reading  and  combining  a  table  
These  questions  are  not  Bayes'  Theorem  questions  —  there's  no  'cause'  being  inferred  from  an  'effect.'  Instead,  each  question  
gives
 
you
 
raw
 
counts
 
that
 
form
 
a
 
simple
 
table
 
(a
 
category,
 
split
 
by
 
whether
 
some
 
condition
 
held),
 
and
 
every
 
statement
 
is
 
answered
 
by
 
reading

combining
 
values
 
directly
 
from
 
that
 
table:
 
P(A|B),
 
P(B|A),
 
unions,
 
complements,
 
and
 
comparisons
 
between
 
two
 
conditional
 
probabilities.
 
As
 
in
 
the
 
supply-counts
 
sets,
 
the
 
number
 
of
 
TRUE
 
statements
 
varies
 
from
 
question
 
to
 
question.
 Notation  reminder:  ●  P(A)  =  (count  in  category  A)  /  (grand  total).  ●  P(A  ∩  B)  =  (count  where  both  A  and  B  hold)  /  (grand  total).  ●  P(B|A)  =  (count  where  both  A  and  B  hold)  /  (count  in  category  A)  —  divide  by  A's  own  total,  not  the  grand  total.  ●  P(A|B)  =  (count  where  both  A  and  B  hold)  /  (count  in  category  B)  —  the  same  joint  count,  but  divided  by  B's  
total
 
instead.
 ●  P(A|B)  and  P(B|A)  are  generally  different  numbers  —  don't  assume  one  equals  the  other.

Step  1  —  Organize  the  counts.  Laptops  sold:  800,  of  which  60  had  a  claim  (so  740  did  not).  Tablets  sold:  500,  of  which  55  had  a  claim  (so  445  did  not).

A  TRUE  P(claim)  =  115/1300  ≈  8.846%,  which  is  greater  than  8%.  This  is  a  straightforward  marginal  probability  —  just  add  the  two  claim  counts  and  divide  by  the  grand  total.  Formula:  P(claim)  =  (claims_laptop  +  claims_tablet)  /  grand  total  
B  TRUE  P(claim|laptop)  =  60/800  =  7.5%.  P(claim|tablet)  =  55/500  =  11%.  11%  is  greater  than  7.5%,  so  the  statement  is  true  —  tablets  have  the  higher  claim  rate,  even  though  they  sold  in  smaller  
numbers
 
overall.
 Notice  that  each  rate  is  computed  by  dividing  by  that  category's  own  total  (800  for  laptops,  500  for  tablets),  not  by  the  grand  
total
 
of
 
1,300.
 Formula:  Compare  (claims_tablet)/(tablets  sold)  to  (claims_laptop)/(laptops  sold)  
C  FALSE  Now  we  flip  the  conditioning  direction:  given  a  claim  happened,  what's  the  chance  the  product  was  a  laptop?  Divide  the

Question  2 
Step  1  —  Organize  the  counts.  Small  drinks  sold:  1,400,  of  which  210  had  oat  milk.  Large  drinks  sold:  900,  of  which  270  had  oat  milk.  Grand  total:  1,400+900  =  2,300  drinks.  Total  oat-milk  drinks:  210+270  =  480.  
A  TRUE  P(oat  milk)  =  480/2300  ≈  20.870%,  which  is  greater  than  20%.  Formula:  P(oat  milk)  =  (oat_small  +  oat_large)  /  grand  total  
B  TRUE  P(oat  milk|large)  =  270/900  =  30%.  P(oat  milk|small)  =  210/1400  =  15%.  30%  is  greater  than  15%  —  large-drink  customers  are  exactly  twice  as  likely  to  add  oat  milk  as  small-drink  customers,  even  though  small  drinks  sell  in  greater  volume  overall  (1,400  vs.  900).  Formula:  Compare  (oat_large)/(large  drinks)  to  (oat_small)/(small  drinks)  
C  TRUE  Flipping  direction:  given  oat  milk  was  added,  what's  the  chance  the  drink  was  large?  Divide  the  large-oat  count  by  the  total  oat-milk  count.  P(large|oat  milk)  =  270/480  =  56.25%,  which  is  greater  than  50%.  So  more  than  half  of  all  oat-milk  drinks  are  large,  even  though  large  drinks  make  up  less  than  half  of  all  drinks  sold  overall  (900  out  of  2,300  ≈  39%)  —  the  much  higher  oat-milk  rate  among  large  drinks  pulls  this  posterior  above  50%.  Formula:  P(large|oat  milk)  =  (oat_large)  /  (total  oat-milk  drinks)  
D  TRUE  Every  drink  is  either  small  or  large,  so  given  oat  milk  was  added,  'small'  and  'large'  are  complementary  outcomes.  This  makes  the  statement  true  by  the  complement  rule.  Formula:  P(small|oat  milk)  =  1  −  P(large|oat  milk)  
E  TRUE  P(no  oat  milk)  =  1  −  P(oat  milk)  =  1  −  20.870%  ≈  79.130%,  which  is  greater  than  75%.  Formula:  P(no  oat  milk)  =  1  −  P(oat  milk)  
All  five  statements  are  true  in  this  question  —  large  drinks  have  double  the  oat-milk  rate  of  small  drinks,  and  that  gap  is  large  enough  to  make  every  comparison  built  around  it  come  out  in  the  expected  direction.   
Question  3 
Step  1  —  Organize  the  counts.  Standard  packages  shipped:  2,200,  of  which  1,980  arrived  on  time,  so  2200−1980  =  220  arrived  late.  Express  packages  shipped:  800,  of  which  760  arrived  on  time,  so  800−760  =  40  arrived  late.  Grand  total:  2,200+800  =  3,000  packages.  Total  late  packages:  220+40  =  260.  
A  FALSE  P(late)  =  260/3000  ≈  8.667%,  which  is  not  greater  than  9%.  Formula:  P(late)  =  (late_Standard  +  late_Express)  /  grand  total  
B  FALSE  P(late|Express)  =  40/800  =  5%.  P(late|Standard)  =  220/2200  =  10%.

5%  is  not  greater  than  10%  —  it's  smaller.  Express  shipping  is  actually  more  reliable  here,  with  half  the  late  rate  of  Standard  shipping,  which  makes  sense  given  it's  the  premium  option.  Formula:  Compare  (late_Express)/(Express  total)  to  (late_Standard)/(Standard  total)  
C  FALSE  Given  a  package  is  late,  what's  the  chance  it  was  Express?  Divide  the  Express-late  count  by  the  total  late  count  (260,  from  Step  1).  P(Express|late)  =  40/260  ≈  15.385%,  which  is  not  greater  than  20%.  Express  packages  are  both  a  smaller  share  of  all  shipments  and  less  likely  to  be  late,  so  unsurprisingly  they  make  up  a  small  share  of  the  late  packages  specifically.  Formula:  P(Express|late)  =  (late_Express)  /  (total  late  packages)  
D  FALSE  P(Standard|late)  =  220/260  ≈  84.615%.  P(Express|late)  =  40/260  ≈  15.385%,  from  statement  C.  84.615%  is  not  less  than  15.385%  —  it's  far  greater.  The  overwhelming  majority  of  late  packages  are  Standard,  both  because  Standard  ships  in  greater  volume  and  because  it  has  the  higher  late  rate.  Formula:  Compare  (late_Standard)/(total  late)  to  (late_Express)/(total  late)  
E  TRUE  P(on  time)  =  1  −  P(late)  =  1  −  8.667%  ≈  91.333%,  which  is  greater  than  90%.  Formula:  P(on  time)  =  1  −  P(late)  
Only  statement  E  is  true.  This  question  flips  the  framing  from  the  previous  two  —  here,  the  smaller-volume  category  (Express)  is  actually  the  more  reliable  one,  so  every  comparison  that  might  intuitively  favor  Express  (statements  B,  C,  D)  turns  out  false.   
Question  4 
Step  1  —  Organize  the  counts.  Thrill-ride  records:  3,500,  of  which  980  had  a  long  wait.  Family-ride  records:  4,500,  of  which  630  had  a  long  wait.  Grand  total:  3,500+4,500  =  8,000  recorded  rides.  Total  long-wait  records:  980+630  =  1,610.  
A  TRUE  P(long  wait)  =  1610/8000  ≈  20.125%,  which  is  greater  than  18%.  Formula:  P(long  wait)  =  (longwait_Thrill  +  longwait_Family)  /  grand  total  
B  TRUE  P(long  wait|Thrill)  =  980/3500  =  28%.  P(long  wait|Family)  =  630/4500  =  14%.  28%  is  exactly  double  14%,  so  the  statement  is  true  —  this  is  a  clean,  exact  relationship  rather  than  an  approximate  one,  worth  double-checking  with  the  raw  division  rather  than  rounding  early.  Formula:  Check  whether  (longwait_Thrill)/(Thrill  total)  =  2  ×  (longwait_Family)/(Family  total)  
C  TRUE  Given  a  long  wait  occurred,  what's  the  chance  it  was  a  Thrill  ride?  Divide  the  Thrill  long-wait  count  by  the  total  long-wait  count.  P(Thrill|long  wait)  =  980/1610  ≈  60.870%,  which  is  greater  than  55%.  Even  though  Thrill  rides  account  for  fewer  total  recorded  rides  than  Family  rides  (3,500  vs.  4,500),  their  double  long-wait  rate  is  enough  to  make  them  the  majority  source  of  long  waits.  Formula:  P(Thrill|long  wait)  =  (longwait_Thrill)  /  (total  long-wait  records)  
D  TRUE  Every  ride  is  either  Thrill  or  Family,  so  given  a  long  wait,  these  two  categories  are  complementary.

The  statement  is  true  by  the  complement  rule.  Formula:  P(Family|long  wait)  =  1  −  P(Thrill|long  wait)  
E  FALSE  P(no  long  wait)  =  1  −  P(long  wait)  =  1  −  20.125%  ≈  79.875%,  which  is  not  greater  than  85%.  Formula:  P(no  long  wait)  =  1  −  P(long  wait)

These  exact  relationships  are  easy  to  mistake  for  approximations  if  you  round  too  early.   
Question  5 
Step  1  —  Organize  the  counts  (three  categories  this  time,  not  two).  Billing  calls:  1,200,  of  which  900  were  resolved  on  the  first  call,  so  1200−900  =  300  were  not.  Technical  calls:  1,800,  of  which  990  were  resolved,  so  1800−990  =  810  were  not.  General  calls:  1,000,  of  which  850  were  resolved,  so  1000−850  =  150  were  not.  Grand  total:  1,200+1,800+1,000  =  4,000  calls.  Total  unresolved  calls:  300+810+150  =  1,260.  
A  FALSE  P(not  resolved)  =  1260/4000  =  31.5%,  which  is  not  greater  than  35%.  Formula:  P(not  resolved)  =  (unresolved_Billing  +  unresolved_Technical  +  unresolved_General)  /  grand  total  
B  FALSE  Given  a  call  was  not  resolved,  what's  the  chance  it  was  Technical?  Divide  the  Technical-unresolved  count  by  the  total  unresolved  count.  P(Technical|not  resolved)  =  810/1260  ≈  64.286%,  which  is  not  greater  than  70%.  Technical  calls  do  make  up  the  clear  majority  of  unresolved  calls  —  both  because  they're  the  largest  call  category  and  because  they  have  the  highest  unresolved  rate  —  but  that  majority  falls  short  of  70%.  Formula:  P(Technical|not  resolved)  =  (unresolved_Technical)  /  (total  unresolved  calls)  
C  TRUE  This  compares  two  different  conditional  probabilities,  both  conditioning  forward  on  call  type  rather  than  backward  on  the  outcome:  the  unresolved  rate  within  Billing  calls,  versus  the  unresolved  rate  within  General  calls.  P(not  resolved|Billing)  =  300/1200  =  25%.  P(not  resolved|General)  =  150/1000  =  15%.  25%  is  greater  than  15%,  so  the  statement  is  true  —  Billing  calls  are  proportionally  harder  to  resolve  on  the  first  attempt  than  General  calls,  even  though  Technical  calls  (not  part  of  this  comparison)  have  the  highest  rate  of  all  at  45%.  Formula:  Compare  (unresolved_Billing)/(Billing  total)  to  (unresolved_General)/(General  total)  
D  FALSE  Now  condition  the  other  direction:  given  a  call  was  unresolved,  compare  the  chance  it  was  Billing  to  the  chance  it  was  General.  P(Billing|not  resolved)  =  300/1260  ≈  23.810%.  P(General|not  resolved)  =  150/1260  ≈  11.905%.  23.810%  is  not  less  than  11.905%  —  it's  roughly  double.  Don't  let  statement  C's  direction  (Billing  has  a  higher  unresolved  rate  than  General)  trick  you  into  assuming  the  same  ordering  holds  when  you  flip  which  variable  you're  conditioning  on  —  here  it  happens  to  hold  in  the  same  direction,  but  as  a  greater-than,  not  a  less-than,  so  the  statement  as  written  ('is  less  than')  is  false.  Formula:  Compare  (unresolved_Billing)/(total  unresolved)  to  (unresolved_General)/(total  unresolved)  
E  TRUE  'Resolved  on  the  first  call'  and  'not  resolved  on  the  first  call'  are  the  only  two  outcomes  for  any  call,  so  they  are  complementary  events.  This  statement  is  true  by  the  complement  rule,  regardless  of  the  specific  counts.

Formula:  P(resolved)  =  1  −  P(not  resolved)  
Only  statements  C  and  E  are  true.  This  question  adds  a  third  category  specifically  to  test  whether  you  keep  track  of  which  total  you're  dividing  by  —  Billing's  unresolved  rate  (25%)  is  the  second-highest  of  the  three  categories,  and  Technical  dominates  the  raw  unresolved  count,  but  neither  of  those  facts  alone  tells  you  the  answer  to  a  specific  comparison  like  D  without  doing  the  actual  division.   Tip:  the  single  most  common  error  in  conditional-probability  questions  is  dividing  by  the  wrong  total  —  P(A|B)  divides  by  B's  total,  while  P(B|A)  divides  by  A's  total,  and  they  are  almost  never  equal.  Before  doing  any  division,  say  out  loud  (or  write  down)  exactly  which  total  you're  using  as  the  denominator,  especially  in  three-category  questions  like  Question  5  where  it's  easy  to  accidentally  use  the  grand  total  instead  of  a  single  category's  total.

A  continuation  of  Part  5,  keeping  the  same  statement  style:  no  statement  compares  two  unknown  probabilities  to  each  other,  and  every  statement  instead  compares  one  computed  probability  —  an  overall  rate,  a  rate  within  one  category,  a  share  of  an  outcome  traced  back  to  one  category,  or  a  combined  share  across  two  categories  —  to  a  single  fixed  percentage.  Five  new  scenarios  are  used,  and  the  wording  of  the  statements  is  varied  on  purpose  ("exceeds,"  "is  above,"  "is  at  least,"  "is  under,"  "is  below,"  alongside  the  plainer  "is  greater  than")  so  the  questions  don't  all  read  identically.  Two  of  the  five  questions  use  three  categories.  As  before,  the  number  of  TRUE  statements  is  not  forced  into  a  predictable  pattern  —  this  set  ranges  all  the  way  from  a  question  where  only  one  statement  is  true  up  to  a  question  where  every  statement  is  true.

Whenever  P(event)  itself  is  a  clean  number  (4%,  5%,  10%),  its  complement  will  be  equally  clean  (96%,  95%,  90%)  —  which  makes  it  easy  to  write  a  'greater  than'  statement  that  looks  safely  true  at  a  glance  but  is  actually  testing  whether  you  caught  the  exact  match.
