CONTEXT_DE Beim Pokerabend wird aus einem standardmäßigen 52-Karten-Deck eine zufällige 5-Karten-Hand an Amanda ausgeteilt.
STATEMENTS_DE:
  A. Die Gesamtzahl verschiedener 5-Karten-Hände ist größer als 2,5 Millionen.
  B. Die Wahrscheinlichkeit für ein Full House ist mehr als 10-mal so groß wie die für einen Vierling.
  C. Die Wahrscheinlichkeit für einen Flush (ohne Straight Flush) ist kleiner als 1 zu 500.
  D. Die Wahrscheinlichkeit, dass Amandas Hand mindestens ein Paar enthält, ist größer als 50%.

=== EXPL 0 A ===
**A.** → True

This statement asks for the total number of distinct 5-card hands that can be dealt. This is the size of the sample space and is calculated by finding the number of combinations of 5 cards chosen from 52, since the order of the cards in a hand does not matter. The general formula for combinations is $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$.

Applying this formula to select 5 cards from 52:

$$
N_{\text{total}} = \binom{52}{5}
$$

$$
\binom{52}{5} = \dfrac{52!}{5!(52-5)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{52!}{5!(47)!} = \dfrac{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49 \cdot 48}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5}
$$

$$
52 \cdot 51 = 2652
$$

$$
2652 \cdot 50 = 132600
$$

$$
132600 \cdot 49 = 6497400
$$

$$
6497400 \cdot 48 = 311875200
$$

$$
\dfrac{311875200}{120} = 2598960
$$

$$
\binom{52}{5} = 2598960
$$

$$
N_{\text{total}} = \frac{52 \times 51 \times 50 \times 49 \times 48}{5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1}
$$

$$
N_{\text{total}} = 2,598,960
$$

This calculation shows there are $2,598,960$ distinct 5-card hands possible. The claim states that this number is greater than $2.5$ million, which is $2,500,000$.

So the statement is True.


=== EXPL 1 B ===
**B.** → False

This statement compares the probability of being dealt a full house to the probability of being dealt four of a kind. To do this, we count the number of hands for each type. The probability of each hand type is its count divided by the total number of hands, $N_{\text{total}}$, so comparing the counts is sufficient.

First, we calculate the number of ways to be dealt a full house. A full house consists of three cards of one rank and two cards of another rank. We choose 1 rank for the three cards (out of 13), then 3 suits for these 3 cards (out of 4). We then choose 1 different rank for the two cards (out of the remaining 12 ranks), and 2 suits for these 2 cards (out of 4):

$$
N_{\text{full house}} = \binom{13}{1} \binom{4}{3} \times \binom{12}{1} \binom{4}{2}
$$

$$
\binom{13}{1} = \dfrac{13!}{1!(13-1)!}
$$

$$
\binom{13}{1} = 13
$$

$$
\binom{4}{3} = \dfrac{4!}{3!(4-3)!}
$$

$$
\binom{4}{3} = 4
$$

$$
\binom{12}{1} = \dfrac{12!}{1!(12-1)!}
$$

$$
\binom{12}{1} = 12
$$

$$
\binom{4}{2} = \dfrac{4!}{2!(4-2)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{4!}{2!(2)!} = \dfrac{4 \cdot 3}{1 \cdot 2}
$$

$$
4 \times 3 = 12
$$

$$
\dfrac{12}{2} = 6
$$

$$
\binom{4}{2} = 6
$$

$$
N_{\text{full house}} = 13 \times 4 \times 12 \times 6
$$

$$
13 \times 4 = 52
$$

$$
52 \times 12 = 624
$$

$$
624 \times 6 = 3744
$$

$$
N_{\text{full house}} = 3,744
$$

Next, we calculate the number of ways to be dealt four of a kind. Four of a kind consists of four cards of one rank and one card of any other rank. We choose 1 rank for the four cards (out of 13), then all 4 suits for these 4 cards (out of 4). We then choose 1 card from the remaining $52 - 4 = 48$ cards, ensuring it is not of the same rank as the four of a kind:

$$
N_{\text{four of a kind}} = \binom{13}{1} \binom{4}{4} \times \binom{48}{1}
$$

$$
\binom{4}{4} = 1
$$

$$
\binom{48}{1} = \dfrac{48!}{1!(48-1)!}
$$

$$
\binom{48}{1} = 48
$$

$$
N_{\text{four of a kind}} = 13 \times 1 \times 48
$$

$$
13 \times 1 = 13
$$

$$
13 \times 48 = 624
$$

$$
N_{\text{four of a kind}} = 624
$$

To compare the probabilities, we find the ratio of these counts:

$$
\frac{P(\text{full house})}{P(\text{four of a kind})} = \frac{N_{\text{full house}}}{N_{\text{four of a kind}} = \frac{3,744}{624}}
$$

$$
\frac{P(\text{full house})}{P(\text{four of a kind})} = 6
$$

This calculation shows that the probability of being dealt a full house is $6$ times the probability of being dealt four of a kind. The claim states that it is more than $10$ times this probability.

So the statement is False.


=== EXPL 2 C ===
**C.** → True

This statement asks for the probability of being dealt a flush, excluding straight flushes, and compares it to $1$ in $500$. A flush consists of 5 cards of the same suit. To count these hands, we first count all hands with 5 cards of the same suit, then subtract the straight flushes.

To count hands with 5 cards of the same suit, we choose 1 suit out of 4, and then choose 5 cards from the 13 cards of that chosen suit:

$$
N_{\text{5 cards of same suit}} = \binom{4}{1} \times \binom{13}{5}
$$

$$
\binom{4}{1} = \dfrac{4!}{1!(4-1)!}
$$

$$
\binom{4}{1} = 4
$$

$$
\binom{13}{5} = \dfrac{13!}{5!(13-5)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{13!}{5!(8)!} = \dfrac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5}
$$

$$
13 \times 12 = 156
$$

$$
156 \times 11 = 1716
$$

$$
1716 \times 10 = 17160
$$

$$
17160 \times 9 = 154440
$$

$$
\dfrac{154440}{120} = 1287
$$

$$
\binom{13}{5} = 1287
$$

$$
N_{\text{5 cards of same suit}} = 4 \times 1,287
$$

$$
N_{\text{5 cards of same suit}} = 5,148
$$

From these hands, we must exclude straight flushes. A straight flush is 5 cards of the same suit in sequential rank (e.g., $A\clubsuit, 2\clubsuit, 3\clubsuit, 4\clubsuit, 5\clubsuit$). There are 10 possible rank sequences for straights (A-5, 2-6, ..., 10-A). Since there are 4 suits, there are $10 \times 4 = 40$ straight flushes.

So, the number of flushes (excluding straight flushes) is:

$$
N_{\text{flush (excl. straight flushes)}} = N_{\text{5 cards of same suit}} - N_{\text{straight flushes}}
$$

$$
N_{\text{flush (excl. straight flushes)}} = 5,148 - 40
$$

$$
N_{\text{flush (excl. straight flushes)}} = 5,108
$$

The probability of being dealt such a flush is this count divided by the total number of distinct hands, $N_{\text{total}} = 2,598,960$, as calculated in the overview:

$$
P_{\text{flush (excl. straight flushes)}} = \frac{5,108}{2,598,960}
$$

$$
P_{\text{flush (excl. straight flushes)}} \approx 0.0019653
$$

The claim states this probability is less than $1$ in $500$. We convert $1/500$ to a decimal: $1/500 = 0.002$.

So the statement is True.


=== EXPL 3 D ===
**D.** → False

This statement asks about the probability that Amanda's hand contains at least one pair, meaning it is not a 'nothing' or high-card hand. This is the complement of having a high-card hand (which has no pair, no straight, and no flush). The probability of at least one pair is $1 - P(\text{high-card hand})$.

First, we count the number of hands with 5 distinct ranks, ignoring suits for a moment. We choose 5 ranks from 13 available ranks ($\binom{13}{5}$). For each of these 5 chosen ranks, there are 4 possible suits, so $4^5$ suit combinations:

$$
N_{\text{5 distinct ranks}} = \binom{13}{5} \times 4^5
$$

$$
\binom{13}{5} = \dfrac{13!}{5!(13-5)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{13!}{5!(8)!} = \dfrac{13 \cdot 12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5}
$$

$$
13 \times 12 = 156
$$

$$
156 \times 11 = 1716
$$

$$
1716 \times 10 = 17160
$$

$$
17160 \times 9 = 154440
$$

$$
\dfrac{154440}{120} = 1287
$$

$$
\binom{13}{5} = 1287
$$

$$
N_{\text{5 distinct ranks}} = 1,287 \times 1,024
$$

$$
N_{\text{5 distinct ranks}} = 1,317,888
$$

From these hands, we must subtract the hands that are straights or flushes, as these also consist of 5 distinct ranks but are not classified as 'high-card' hands. We already know the number of straight flushes is 40. From statement C, the number of non-straight flushes is 5,108. From statement E, the number of non-flush straights is 10,200.

So, the number of high-card hands is:

$$
N_{\text{high-card}} = N_{\text{5 distinct ranks}} - N_{\text{non-flush straights}} - N_{\text{non-straight flushes}} - N_{\text{straight flushes}}
$$

$$
N_{\text{high-card}} = 1,317,888 - 10,200 - 5,108 - 40
$$

$$
N_{\text{high-card}} = 1,302,540
$$

The probability of a high-card hand is this count divided by $N_{\text{total}} = 2,598,960$:

$$
P_{\text{high-card}} = \frac{1,302,540}{2,598,960}
$$

$$
P_{\text{high-card}} \approx 0.501169
$$

Therefore, the probability of at least one pair is:

$$
P_{\text{at least one pair}} = 1 - P_{\text{high-card}}
$$

$$
P_{\text{at least one pair}} = 1 - 0.501169
$$

$$
1 - 0.501169 = 0.498831
$$

$$
P_{\text{at least one pair}} \approx 0.498831
$$

As a percentage, this is approximately $49.88\%$. The claim states this probability is greater than $50\%$.

So the statement is False.


=== EXPL 4 E ===
**E.** → False

This statement compares the probability of being dealt a straight (excluding straight flushes) with the probability of being dealt three of a kind. We determine the number of ways to form each hand, and then compare these counts.

First, we count the number of straights, excluding straight flushes. A straight consists of 5 cards of sequential rank. There are 10 possible sequential rank combinations (A2345 through 10JQKA). For each of these 10 sequences, there are $4^5$ ways to assign suits to the five cards. From these $4^5$ combinations, we must subtract the 4 combinations that form a straight flush (one for each suit).

$$
N_{\text{straight (excl. straight flushes)}} = 10 \times (4^5 - 4)
$$

$$
N_{\text{straight (excl. straight flushes)}} = 10 \times (1,024 - 4)
$$

$$
N_{\text{straight (excl. straight flushes)}} = 10 \times 1,020
$$

$$
N_{\text{straight (excl. straight flushes)}} = 10,200
$$

Next, we count the number of three of a kind hands. A three of a kind hand consists of three cards of one rank and two other cards of different ranks that do not form a pair or a full house. We choose 1 rank for the three cards (out of 13), then 3 suits for these 3 cards (out of 4). We then choose 2 ranks from the remaining 12 ranks for the other two cards (ensuring they are different from each other and the triplet's rank), and 1 suit for each of these two cards (out of 4):

$$
N_{\text{three of a kind}} = \binom{13}{1} \binom{4}{3} \times \binom{12}{2} \binom{4}{1} \binom{4}{1}
$$

$$
\binom{13}{1} = \dfrac{13!}{1!(13-1)!}
$$

$$
\binom{13}{1} = 13
$$

$$
\binom{4}{3} = \dfrac{4!}{3!(4-3)!}
$$

$$
\binom{4}{3} = 4
$$

$$
\binom{12}{2} = \dfrac{12!}{2!(12-2)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{12!}{2!(10)!} = \dfrac{12 \cdot 11}{1 \cdot 2}
$$

$$
12 \times 11 = 132
$$

$$
\dfrac{132}{2} = 66
$$

$$
\binom{12}{2} = 66
$$

$$
\binom{4}{1} = \dfrac{4!}{1!(4-1)!}
$$

$$
\binom{4}{1} = 4
$$

$$
N_{\text{three of a kind}} = 13 \times 4 \times 66 \times 4 \times 4
$$

$$
13 \times 4 = 52
$$

$$
52 \times 66 = 3432
$$

$$
3432 \times 4 = 13728
$$

$$
13728 \times 4 = 54912
$$

$$
N_{\text{three of a kind}} = 54,912
$$

Comparing the counts, $N_{\text{straight (excl. straight flushes)}} = 10,200$ and $N_{\text{three of a kind}} = 54,912$. Since $10,200 < 54,912$, the probability of being dealt a straight (excluding straight flushes) is less than the probability of being dealt three of a kind. The claim states that the probability of a straight is greater than that of three of a kind.

So the statement is False.
