The claim asks for the probability that the sample contains exactly 2 defective items. This is a hypergeometric probability calculation for $P(X=2)$. We need to choose 2 defective items from the $K=4$ available, and $n-k = 5-2=3$ non-defective items from the $N-K=16$ available.

The general formula is:

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k} \binom{N-K}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}
$$

Substituting the values $N=20$, $K=4$, $n=5$, and $k=2$:

$$
P(X = 2) = \frac{\binom{4}{2} \binom{16}{3}}{\binom{20}{5}}
$$

We calculate the combinations:

$$
\binom{4}{2} = \dfrac{4!}{2!(4-2)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{4!}{2!(2)!} = \dfrac{4 \cdot 3}{1 \cdot 2}
$$

$$
4 \times 3 = 12
$$

$$
\dfrac{12}{2} = 6
$$

$$
\binom{4}{2} = 6
$$

$$
\binom{16}{3} = \dfrac{16!}{3!(16-3)!}
$$

$$
\dfrac{16!}{3!(13)!} = \dfrac{16 \cdot 15 \cdot 14}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3}
$$

$$
16 \times 15 = 240
$$

$$
240 \times 14 = 3360
$$

$$
\dfrac{3360}{6} = 560
$$

$$
\binom{16}{3} = 560
$$

From the overview, the total number of samples is $\binom{20}{5} = 15\,504$. Now we can substitute these values into the probability formula:

$$
P(X = 2) = \frac{6 \times 560}{15\,504} = \frac{3\,360}{15\,504}
$$

$$
P(X = 2)
$$

$$
\approx 0.2167
$$

This probability is approximately $21.67\%$. The statement claims this probability is greater than $20\%$.

So the statement is True.
