Chapter 11.2 – Continuous Compounding (Complete Set: Tasks 1–20)
Tasks 1–20, sorted from easiest to hardest (difficulty 1/5 to 5/5). Key formulas: with continuous compounding of interest
at nominal annual rate r, an initial principal S0 grows after t years to S(t) = S0·ert; each year the principal is multiplied by
the same fixed factor er, so S(t+1) = S(t)·er. The effective interest rate with continuous compounding at annual rate r is
er − 1, and this is also the largest effective annual rate obtainable at nominal rate r under any compounding frequency
whatsoever, since (1 + r/n)n → er as n → ∞. Continuous compounding is therefore always at least as profitable for the
lender as any less-frequent compounding at the SAME nominal rate, and the gap between them grows larger as either
the interest rate or the number of years increases (and stays small for low rates over short periods) — though a higher
nominal rate under less-frequent compounding can still outperform a lower nominal rate compounded continuously,
since the ceiling result only compares schedules sharing one common rate. The identical exponential law, run with a
negative rate, describes continuous depreciation: a value shrinking continuously at annual rate δ follows v(t) = v0·e−δt.
Tasks 11–20 additionally combine these same results in multi-step ways — solving for an unknown rate or time from
observed growth or decay, chaining sequential compounding phases, and comparing several instruments or assets at
once — but every task relies exclusively on the formulas above. Round dollar amounts to 2 decimal places and rates to
2 decimal places unless otherwise noted.

Chapter 11.3 – Present Value (Complete Set: Tasks 1–20)
Tasks 1–20, sorted from easiest to hardest (difficulty 1/5 to 5/5). Key formulas: if the interest or discount rate is
p% per year and r = p/100, the present discounted value (PDV) of an amount K payable in t years is K(1 + r)−t
with annual interest payments, or Ke−rt with continuous compounding of interest. The (annual) discount factor is
(1 + r)−1, whose reciprocal, 1 + r, equals one plus the discount rate; continuous compounding always produces a
smaller present value than annual compounding at the same stated rate, since capital grows fastest under
continuous compounding. Several tasks additionally draw on the chapter's optimal-timing result for an asset
whose market value P(t) grows over time: the present value f(t) = P(t)e−rt is maximized at a time t* satisfying the
first-order condition P′(t*) = rP(t*), subject to the second-order condition P″(t*) − rP′(t*) < 0 for a genuine
maximum, with comparative-statics sensitivity dt*/dr = P(t*) / [P″(t*) − rP′(t*)]. All tasks below rely exclusively on
these results. Round dollar amounts to 2 decimal places and rates/times to 2 decimal places unless otherwise
noted.

Chapter 11.4 – Geometric Series (Complete Set: Tasks 1–20)
Tasks 1–20, sorted from easiest to hardest (difficulty 1/5 to 5/5). Key formulas: a finite geometric series with first term
a and quotient k ≠ 1 has sum sn = a + ak + ak2 + ··· + akn−1 = a·(kn − 1)/(k − 1); when k = 1, every term equals a and
sn = a·n. An infinite geometric series a + ak + ak2 + ··· converges if and only if |k| < 1, in which case its sum is a/(1 −
k); if |k| ≥ 1 the infinite series diverges and has no finite sum. Tasks 17 and 20 additionally draw on two related results
about general (not necessarily geometric) infinite series: convergence requires the terms an to tend to 0, though this
is necessary but not sufficient (the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ··· is the standard counterexample), and the
p-series rule ∑1/np converges if and only if p > 1. All tasks below rely exclusively on these results. Round dollar and
quantity amounts to 2 decimal places unless otherwise noted.

Chapter 11.5 – Present Value, Future Value, Annuities, Annuities Due & Perpetuities (Complete Set: Tasks
1–10)
Tasks 1–10, sorted from easiest to hardest (difficulty 1.0/5 to 3.0/5). Key formulas: the present value of a
single future payment of A due in n years, discounted at annual rate r, is x = A/(1+r)n; the future value of a
present deposit P after n years is F = P(1+r)n. For n successive equal payments of a per period (an ordinary
annuity, paid at the end of each period), the present value is Pn = (a/r)[1 − 1/(1+r)n], and the future value,
immediately after the last payment, is Fn = (a/r)[(1+r)n − 1]. As n tends to infinity, the annuity's present-value
formula reduces to the present value of a perpetuity, P = a/r. An annuity due shifts every payment to the
START of each period instead of the end, so its present and future values are simply the corresponding
ordinary-annuity values multiplied by (1+r): Pdue = Pn×(1+r) and Fdue = Fn×(1+r). Every task relies
exclusively on these formulas. Round dollar amounts to 2 decimal places and rates/percentages to 2
decimal places unless otherwise noted.

Chapter 11.6 – Mortgage Repayments
Tasks 1–13, sorted from easiest to hardest (difficulty 1/5 to 5/5, with Tasks 11–13 as extra harder capstone
comparisons). Key ideas: a loan of K dollars repaid in n equal instalments of a dollars per period, at periodic interest
rate r, satisfies K = (a/r)·[1−(1+r)−n], so the required payment is a = rK/[1−(1+r)−n]. Each payment splits into an interest
part (r times the outstanding balance from the previous period) and a principal part (the rest of the payment, which
reduces the balance). If the first payment is made immediately rather than at the end of the first period (an annuity
due), the first payment counts at full face value and only the remaining n−1 payments are discounted. When the
payment amount a is fixed in advance instead of solved for, the number of periods needed is the smallest integer n
with n ≥ [ln a − ln(a−rK)]/ln(1+r); unless this holds with equality, the final payment will be smaller than a. Finally, when
several deposits are made within a single interest period and the bank applies simple interest to money held for only
part of that period, each deposit is multiplied by (1+r·t), where t is the remaining fraction of the period after the deposit
is made. Tasks 11–13 additionally compare the present value of several complete payment schedules against each
other — including schedules that combine an upfront cash portion with a later series of equal payments — to
determine which schedule is cheapest, and how that conclusion can change at a different interest rate. Round dollar
amounts to 2 decimal places unless otherwise noted.
Task 1. Equipment Loan: Finding the Equal Annual Payment
Difficulty: 1/5
A small bakery-supply distributor borrows $60,000 at the beginning of the year to buy a delivery van and a
walk-in cooler. The loan is to be repaid in 6 equal instalments at the end of each year, with interest charged at
12% per year, compounding annually.
Statements (True / False):
a) The periodic interest rate to use in the payment formula is r = 0.12.
b) The required equal annual payment is approximately $14,593.54.
c) The interest portion of the first payment is exactly $7,200.00.
d) The principal-repayment portion of the first payment is more than half of the total payment amount.
e) The outstanding balance immediately after the second payment is approximately $45,000.00.
Solution – Task 1
Given:
• K (loan amount) = $60,000
• r (annual interest rate) = 0.12
• n = 6 equal annual payments
Formula(s):
• Equal-payment formula: K = (a/r)·[1−(1+r)−n], so a = rK/[1−(1+r)−n]
• Interest in a period = r × outstanding balance at the start of that period
