# Chapter 4 — Forms of business ownership and sources of finance

Once a business begins operating and trading goods or services with customers, an important legal question arises: who is the customer actually contracting with—the business as such, or the individuals who founded and manage it? The answer matters whenever contracts are signed, capital is invested, or disputes end up in court. Who holds ownership? Who exercises control? Who is the legal counterparty, and who can be held liable in litigation? Is the business a distinct legal person, or is it legally indistinguishable from its owner or owners?

The answer depends on the legal form chosen for the business. That choice shapes several fundamental issues:

- **Ownership:** Is there a single owner, several owners, or even another business as owner?

- **Management:** Do the owners also run the business, or is day-to-day management entrusted to different people?

- **Liability:** Who bears responsibility for the business’s debts and obligations—and is that liability capped or unlimited?

- **Finance:** How can the business obtain the money it needs to operate and grow?

These questions matter not only to customers, suppliers, and creditors, but also to founders and owners themselves. Some owners want to be actively involved in management—setting objectives, planning activities, organising resources, and monitoring performance. Others prefer to invest capital and hold an ownership stake while leaving operational decisions to professional managers.

Finance is equally central to every business venture. From the moment of establishment, money is required to cover initial outlays such as equipment, machinery, vehicles, and inventory, as well as recurring costs like rent, energy, and insurance. Depending on the chosen legal form, minimum capital requirements may apply before trading can begin. Once operations are under way, revenue from sales can fund day-to-day activities and, in favourable circumstances, support expansion. Yet sales income is seldom enough on its own, and it may not be available in the right amount or at the right time to meet every financial obligation. For these reasons, most businesses draw on additional sources of finance throughout their lifecycle.

The link between legal structure and finance is direct: incorporated forms such as corporations can issue shares and bonds, while unincorporated forms are largely restricted to personal savings, credit, and retained profits. Businesses that outgrow their initial structure often reorganise specifically to access broader funding channels. Although the names and detailed rules differ between countries, the underlying categories of business ownership are broadly comparable worldwide.

**Key ideas:** Legal form determines ownership, management, liability, and financing options. Founders must weigh operational involvement against investment goals, and recognise that structure and finance are closely linked.

## 4.1 Sole proprietorship / sole traders

A **sole proprietorship** is owned by a single individual who typically also manages and runs the business. It is straightforward to establish—particularly for small ventures—because no minimum capital is required and formal registration procedures are comparatively simple. The business is not a separate legal entity; legally and fiscally, the proprietor and the business are one and the same. Profits are reported on the owner’s personal income tax return, and the owner pays tax on business earnings accordingly. There is no separate corporate tax return for the business itself.

Management rests largely with the sole proprietor, who can make decisions independently without consulting co-owners or seeking approval from a board. That autonomy allows rapid responses to market changes but also concentrates risk in a single person. Continuity may be threatened if the proprietor retires, becomes seriously ill, or is otherwise unable to work, since there is no automatic succession mechanism built into the legal form. Employees can be hired for support, but the most important management decisions and the full weight of financial risk remain with the owner.

### Available financial funds for sole proprietors

Financing capacity depends primarily on the proprietor’s own financial position. Without adequate personal resources, starting the business becomes difficult. Most sole proprietors fund operations from personal savings. When more money is needed, they may seek funds from investors and/or banks. The owner’s contribution, together with money from external investors and creditors, constitutes **external** sources of finance.

Banks and other financial institutions offer **credit** on both short-term (under one year) and long-term bases. A creditor lends money for an agreed period; repayment follows the contract terms, usually including **interest** as compensation for the risk and the opportunity cost of lending. Creditors often require **collateral**—assets pledged against default—especially for long-term loans where the repayment horizon stretches over many years. Mortgages secured on land and property are a common example of long-term bank lending backed by tangible assets.

Short-term credit frequently takes the form of a **bank overdraft** or **trade credit**. A bank overdraft is a flexible instrument: once a business account is opened, the proprietor can withdraw funds beyond the current balance when cash is needed. Interest is charged only on the overdrawn amount, making overdrafts well suited to managing temporary cash shortfalls. **Trade credit** arises when a supplier agrees to defer payment, giving the business time to pay for purchases after delivery. This arrangement effectively provides interest-free short-term finance, provided payment is made within the agreed period.

All credit—short or long term—creates liabilities for the sole proprietor. Because liability is unlimited, private assets—including savings, property, and personal possessions—are exposed if the business fails, debts fall due, or pledged collateral must be realised. Creditors may pursue the proprietor’s personal wealth to satisfy outstanding obligations.

Once trading begins, **internal** sources of finance may become available. Retained profit—revenue exceeding expenses, reinvested rather than withdrawn—can fund growth without increasing external debt. Selling surplus or obsolete assets offers another internal option. Internal finance is valuable because it carries no interest or similar financial charges, though it does reduce the funds available for the proprietor’s personal use.

A sole proprietorship does not suit Tina and Steve. They wish to share tasks, share risk, and hold equal rights and responsibilities. Their vision is better realised through a **partnership**.

**Key ideas:** Sole proprietorships are easy to set up, offer full managerial control, and expose the owner to unlimited liability. Finance comes mainly from personal savings, credit, and eventually retained earnings. Tina and Steve need a structure that allows shared ownership and management.

## 4.2 Partnership

When two or more people jointly establish a business, the arrangement is called a **partnership**. Unlike a sole proprietorship, a partnership distributes ownership, responsibility, and risk among two or more individuals. Partners should draw up a **partnership agreement**—a formal document defining rights, responsibilities, and how profits and losses are shared. Although not always legally mandatory in every jurisdiction, such an agreement is strongly advisable because it reduces ambiguity and provides a framework for resolving disagreements.

In a **general partnership**, all partners enjoy equal rights, bear equal responsibilities, and face unlimited liability. Partners can divide tasks according to their skills, specialise in different areas of the business, and consult one another when facing difficult decisions—often arriving at better outcomes than a single proprietor acting alone. In Austria this form is known as an **Offene Gesellschaft**, abbreviated **OG**. Tina and Steve operate in Austria and choose to found an OG because they intend to participate equally in management and share financial risk. Their firm is named **T&S Computer Services OG**. Their partnership agreement will specify each partner’s ownership share, profit-and-loss allocation, partnership terms, rights and duties, decision-making procedures, dispute resolution, and other relevant provisions. By documenting these matters in advance, they protect both their business relationship and the interests of future creditors and customers.

### Available financial funds for partnerships

Partnerships resemble sole proprietorships in financial terms, with one important advantage: two or more partners may together contribute more savings and pledge more private assets as collateral than a single proprietor. A bank assessing a loan application may view the combined resources of several partners more favourably than those of one individual. External investors may also be more willing to provide funds when risk is spread across multiple owners, although in a general partnership each partner remains personally exposed.

Each partner is **solely liable for all debts** of the business—**unlimited liability** applies to every partner in a general partnership, regardless of who incurred a particular obligation. Creditors may pursue any partner’s private assets to recover the full amount owed. This joint and several liability is a defining characteristic of general partnerships and must be understood before entering one.

A **limited partnership** includes at least one partner who does not participate in management and whose liability is restricted to the capital contributed. This arrangement allows passive investors to supply funds without assuming operational responsibility or exposing their entire personal wealth. In Austria this form is the **Kommanditgesellschaft**, or **KG**. The KG thus combines active general partners, who manage the business and bear unlimited liability, with limited partners, whose financial exposure is capped.

**Key ideas:** Partnerships combine shared ownership and management with contractual clarity through a partnership agreement. General partnerships (OG) carry unlimited joint liability; limited partnerships (KG) protect passive investors. Tina and Steve’s OG reflects equal involvement and shared risk.

## 4.3 Corporations

**Corporations** are legal entities in their own right—distinct from the individuals who create, own, or manage them. As **legal persons**, they hold rights and obligations comparable to natural persons: they may own land and property, employ staff, enter contracts, and sue or be sued in their own name. This separation is one of the most significant features of the corporate form.

Founders and shareholders need not manage the company, and managers need not own shares. This division allows specialists to run large organisations while investors supply capital from a distance. Corporations are more complex and costly to establish than sole proprietorships or partnerships, often requiring formal registration, statutory capital, and ongoing disclosure obligations. In return, shareholders’ liability is typically limited to the amount invested when purchasing shares; personal assets beyond that investment are generally protected.

### 4.3.1 Shareholders are not (necessarily) managers of the business

If shareholders supply **share capital** without managing operations, who runs the corporation? Responsibility falls to the **board of directors**—individuals elected by shareholders to make major decisions and represent their interests. The senior member is the **Chief Executive Officer (CEO)**. Other common roles include the **Chief Financial Officer (CFO)**, **Chief Operating Officer (COO)**, **Chief Information Officer (CIO)**, and **Chief Marketing Officer (CMO)**.

### 4.3.2 Available financial funds for corporations

Corporations generally enjoy broader financing options than sole proprietorships or partnerships. Their funds consist chiefly of **share capital** together with loans and credit.

#### Share capital

A corporation’s capital is divided into **shares**, hence the term **share capital**. If share capital totals €1,000,000 and is split into 100,000 shares, each share (also called **stock**) represents €10, or 0.001 per cent of the total. Buyers of shares become **shareholders** and thereby acquire a proportional ownership stake in the corporation. Shares may be acquired at initial issuance—when the company first offers them to investors—or later from existing shareholders on the secondary market. If all 100,000 shares are sold at issuance, the corporation receives €1,000,000 in share capital, demonstrating how the sale of equity can mobilise substantial funds from a wide pool of investors. AT&S’s share capital amounts to €141,846,000. Share capital is normally not repaid to shareholders during the life of the company; it functions as long-term or permanent financing that underpins the corporation’s balance sheet.

A corporation’s stock may, but need not, be listed on a **stock exchange**—a regulated financial market where shares and other securities such as **bonds** can be bought and sold efficiently by individuals and institutions alike. Companies seeking a listing must satisfy regulatory requirements, including transparency standards and ongoing reporting obligations designed to protect investors.

Shares enter the market at a set price during the **initial public offering (IPO)**—the process by which a corporation first offers its shares to the public. After the IPO, **supply and demand** on the exchange determine the market price. Rising demand tends to push prices up; falling demand pulls them down. Demand shifts for many reasons: positive expectations about future profits, anticipated market-share gains, successful product launches, and favourable assessments of management quality can all attract buyers. Broader **economic indicators**—including economic growth, **interest rates**, and inflation—also shape investor behaviour. Shares often attract more buyers when the economy is strong and many people have disposable income to invest; when inflation is relatively high, eroding the real value of cash holdings, equities may appear more attractive; and when interest rates are comparatively low, fixed-income alternatives such as savings accounts and government bonds offer lower returns, making shares relatively more appealing.

An increase in share price after issuance does **not** bring additional capital to the issuing corporation. The company received its financing at the time shares were first sold; subsequent price movements affect only those who hold or trade the shares on the market. The beneficiaries of a price rise are the shareholders who can sell at the higher level, not the corporation itself.

Major global stock exchanges include the **New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)**, **Nasdaq Inc.** (New York), the **London Stock Exchange (LSE)**, the **Tokyo Stock Exchange**, **Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing**, and the **Shanghai Stock Exchange**. In Europe, leading venues beyond the LSE include the **Deutsche Börse** (Frankfurt), **NYSE Euronext** (Europe), the **SIX Swiss Exchange** (Geneva, Basel, and Zurich), **Borsa Italiana** (Milan), and the **Spanish Exchange**.

People invest in shares for several reasons:

- Supporting a business they believe in financially.
- **Annual income** through **dividends**—distributions of profit to shareholders. Dividends are discretionary, but prolonged non-payment can reduce demand and depress the share price.
- **Capital growth** from selling shares at a price above the purchase price.
- **Voting rights** at the annual stockholders’ meeting (attached to common stock). Holders of preferred shares typically forgo voting rights but receive higher dividends.
- Protection against inflation, since shares represent claims on real business assets.

In English-speaking jurisdictions, the **public limited company (PLC)** is a familiar corporate form—especially in the United Kingdom and Commonwealth countries. In the United States and elsewhere, labels such as “Inc.” (Incorporated) or “Ltd” (Limited) are common. German-speaking countries use **Aktiengesellschaft**, abbreviated **AG** (“Aktien” = shares; “Gesellschaft” = corporation). Founding an AG in Austria requires a minimum of €70,000 in capital—one reason Tina and Steve reject it for their venture.

The **European Company (SE, société européenne)** resembles an AG or PLC but is governed directly by EU law and may be established only within EU member states.

#### Liabilities

Beyond loans and credit available to unincorporated businesses, large corporations may issue **bonds**. A bond is essentially a loan from investors to the corporation: investors provide capital for a fixed term under agreed repayment and interest conditions. Bond financing can be cheaper than bank borrowing, since bond interest rates are often lower than bank loan rates. Access to large amounts of low-cost debt enables investment in long-term assets—plants, infrastructure, and other major projects.

**Key ideas:** Corporations raise equity through share capital and debt through loans, credit, and bonds. Stock exchanges facilitate share trading; IPOs and secondary-market prices respond to company prospects and macroeconomic conditions. AT&S illustrates listing history, shareholder structure, and investor metrics. Share-price gains after issuance benefit shareholders, not the company.

### 4.3.3 Private Limited Companies

Many countries recognise **private limited companies**—corporate legal persons with limited owner liability whose shares are typically **not** offered to the general public on a stock exchange but transferred among existing or invited owners. Examples include the **Limited Liability Company (LLC)** in the USA, the private company limited by shares in the UK, and the **Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung (GmbH)** in Austria and Germany.

Tina and Steve could have formed a **GmbH**, but Austria requires a minimum capital of **€35,000** for this form. They therefore preferred a **partnership**, though a GmbH remains a possible option as the business matures.

## 4.6 The choice of the source of finance

When several financing options exist, businesses typically weigh **cost**, **intended use**, and **current financial position** before committing to a particular source of funds. No single option is universally best; the right choice depends on the circumstances of the business at the time of the decision.

- **Costs** include interest on loans and credit, plus administrative expenses for issuing shares or bonds. Equity finance does not require regular interest payments, but issuing shares dilutes existing ownership and may involve substantial legal and advisory fees. Debt finance creates a contractual obligation to repay principal and interest regardless of business performance.

- **Purpose:** **Capital expenditures**—assets such as buildings, machinery, and vehicles used over many years—call for **long-term finance** matched to the useful life of the asset. Financing a ten-year asset with a three-month overdraft would create a dangerous mismatch between repayment deadlines and the revenue the asset generates. **Revenue expenditures**—such as raw materials consumed in production or wages paid to staff—can safely be covered by short-term sources that are renewed as operations continue.

- **Financial structure:** A business with a high proportion of loan capital—a **high geared** business—may struggle to secure additional credit. Lenders assess the risk that existing debt obligations will consume too large a share of future cash flows. They may demand higher interest, additional collateral, or refuse further lending altogether. Such a firm should prioritise internal finance—retained earnings—and/or seek equity from investors or partners willing to share risk without requiring fixed repayment schedules. Because loans must be repaid on schedule regardless of profitability, heavy reliance on debt increases **insolvency** risk, particularly during economic downturns when revenues fall but debt service costs remain unchanged.

**Key ideas:** Financing decisions balance cost, duration, and existing leverage. Long-term assets need long-term funding; excessive debt raises risk and constrains future borrowing.
