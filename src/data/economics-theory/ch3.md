Private households take many forms — large families, singles, couples with or without children — and businesses are just as varied. Firms differ in the factors of production they combine, the sector or sectors in which they operate, and their size. ## 3.1 Businesses combine different factors of production

> **In this section**
> You will learn what factors of production are and how the mix of those factors shapes different types of business.

As outlined in the introduction, a business is an entity that offers goods and/or services to customers. To do so, it combines **factors of production** — the resources used to create goods and services. The most important factors are:

- **labour** (all human resources),

- **land** (all natural resources),

- **capital** (resources like machinery, plant, vehicles, and financial resources), and

- **entrepreneurship** (which brings land, labour, and capital together), as well as

- **knowledge and technology**.

Depending on which factor dominates the production process, a wide range of businesses can be found. Tina and Steve mainly combine their own knowledge and skills with labour, technology, some capital, and entrepreneurship. AT&S combines all factors of production. A winemaker with large vineyards combines land, labour, capital, and entrepreneurship, though the business may also depend on personal knowledge, experience, and technology.

> **Example — Tina & Steve**
> Their computer-services venture is built on skills and know-how (knowledge), their own work effort (labour), software and equipment (technology and capital), and the entrepreneurial decision to start and run the firm.

> **Key ideas**
> - Businesses supply goods and/or services by combining factors of production.
> - The five main factors are labour, land, capital, entrepreneurship, and knowledge/technology.
> - The dominant factor varies from firm to firm — from knowledge-based micro ventures to land-intensive agriculture and capital-intensive manufacturing.

## 3.2 Businesses operate in the primary, secondary and/or tertiary sector

> **In this section**
> You will place businesses within the three-sector model, interpret how sector shares change as economies develop, and evaluate GDP as an indicator of economic activity.

Depending on what a business does, it contributes to one or more of three sectors of the economy. The **three-sector model** differentiates between three broad areas of activity.

The **primary sector** refers to the extraction of raw materials from the earth. It mainly comprises farming, fishing, mining, and forestry. Emerging countries — which are economically less developed — usually depend largely on the primary sector.

Businesses of the **secondary sector** transform raw materials into goods through manufacturing. Such businesses produce cars, ships, machinery, printed circuit boards and IC substrates (like AT&S), computers, clothes, and many other products.

![Comparison of sector shares across economies at different stages of development](/__l5e/assets-v1/61dc5e65-3821-4c3c-9439-1fe15479b39c/tb-015-009.png)

The **tertiary sector** comprises the service industry — distribution, banking, insurance, coaching, and similar activities. Tina and Steve offer services: they trade goods, develop software, and provide technical support.

As the economic development of a country advances, the tertiary sector becomes more important. In economically highly developed countries — such as EU countries with a high gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, indicating a high standard of living — the tertiary sector usually accounts for more than 70% of the output of the economy. The primary sector, as important as it may be for providing food, accounts for only a small percentage of output.

### GDP: definition and limitations

**GDP** is the total monetary value of final goods and services that are produced within a country's borders in a certain time period (usually a year). It is therefore considered to measure the overall economic activity of a country. GDP is also used as an indicator for economic growth: if GDP (adjusted for inflation) increases over time, the economy is growing.

However, the use of GDP is not undisputed. Critics say that not all sources of income are taken into account. GDP does not tell anything about the quality and/or sustainability of growth, nor is it a perfect indicator of economic wellbeing. For example, GDP would also increase after ecological disasters that require rebuilding infrastructure. However, GDP is usually correlated to some indicators of well-being like health status and happiness.

> **Key ideas**
> - The three-sector model distinguishes primary (raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), and tertiary (services) activity.
> - Advanced economies are dominated by the tertiary sector; emerging economies rely more heavily on the primary sector.
> - GDP measures the value of final output within a country's borders and tracks growth, but it has recognised limitations as a wellbeing indicator.

## 3.3 Businesses can be profit-oriented or not-for-profit

> **In this section**
> You will contrast businesses that aim to earn a profit with organisations that focus on covering costs and serving a social purpose.

Most businesses aim to operate for a longer time period. In order to do so and to thrive over time, most businesses aim to make a **profit** — that is, they intend to have higher revenues than costs and expenses. Profits are important to the business itself because they can be reinvested, which enhances the durability and sustainability of the business. Profits are also important to the owners and investors because they are the reward for the risk they have taken.

However, there are also businesses (or organisations) that are **not-for-profit** and mainly aim to cover their costs. Nevertheless, they also need to achieve a certain level of revenues — or in many cases, donations — in order to be able to offer their goods and services. Any profit they make is also beneficial to the organisation, because it can be reinvested and used to enhance the services for customers or to engage in more projects. The Red Cross, the World Wildlife Fund, and Greenpeace are examples of such non-profit organisations (NPOs).

![A profit-oriented business reinvests surplus revenues to grow and reward owners for the risks they take](/__l5e/assets-v1/a17ecb59-7dce-4086-9baa-2f634d9994c8/tb-016-010.png)

![A not-for-profit organisation focuses on covering costs and reinvesting any surplus into its mission and services](/__l5e/assets-v1/38b4212c-476e-4b9c-a6b8-bf2b62de64b1/tb-016-011.png)

> **Key ideas**
> - Profit-oriented businesses aim for revenues above costs; profits support reinvestment and reward risk-taking owners.
> - Not-for-profit organisations focus on covering costs rather than maximising profit, though surpluses can still be reinvested.
> - Examples of NPOs include the Red Cross, the World Wildlife Fund, and Greenpeace.

## 3.4 Businesses come "in all sizes", large and small

> **In this section**
> You will learn how the European Commission defines micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises and why size classification matters.

There is an enormous variety of businesses: micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSME or just SME) as well as large businesses. About 99% of all businesses in the EU are SMEs. The European Commission differentiates between these types of businesses (see http://ec.europa.eu) as presented in table 1.

| Company category | Staff headcount | Turnover | OR | Balance sheet total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Micro | < 10 | ≤ €2 m | | ≤ €2 m |
| Small | < 50 | ≤ €10 m | | ≤ €10 m |
| Medium-sized | < 250 | ≤ €50 m | | ≤ €43 m |

*Table 1. Size categories of businesses*

The definition of an SME is important for access to finance and EU support programmes targeted specifically at these enterprises. Tina and Steve have a micro company; even if they hire two assistants, they are still a micro company. AT&S, on the other hand, with about 10,000 employees, is a large company. The size of a business is also relevant in terms of legal requirements for their accounting (see chapter 6, Accounting). In many countries, small businesses are allowed to use simpler accounting methods in order to calculate their profits or losses.

> **Key ideas**
> - EU businesses are classified as micro, small, or medium-sized based on headcount, turnover, and balance sheet total.
> - SMEs account for about 99% of EU businesses and benefit from targeted support and financing.
> - Size influences accounting obligations — smaller firms often face lighter reporting requirements.

## 3.5 Businesses may be local, national or international

> **In this section**
> You will compare businesses that serve a local area, a national market, or customers across borders.

A **local** (or regional) business operates in a small, limited area. Most customers live very close to the business. It does not serve a national or international market. Tina's and Steve's business would be such a local business, at least at the beginning. The most important challenge of (small) local businesses is to have sufficient financial funds and to acquire a substantial number of customers. Many business owners face the difficulties of **undercapitalisation**: their own funds are limited and it is very hard for them to access additional funding.

A **national** business serves the home market — the market within a country — but not the international market. An insurance company or a bakery that operates all over Austria, but not abroad, is a national business. Like a local business, it has to choose a suitable location. Additionally, it has to decide how to deliver its goods and services to other places within the country. The supply chain is much longer.

An **international** (or multinational) business makes and/or sells its goods and services in more than one country. As the home market might be limited and too small for some companies, they strive to serve a larger market. A larger, international market brings along a number of challenges: an even longer supply chain, different legal and economic systems, different cultures and languages, maybe also different currencies. As the number of multinational businesses has increased over time, the world has become globalised and we have experienced **globalisation**. AT&S is an international business that has production plants in Asian countries as well as in Austria and that sells its goods to other businesses all over the world.

> **Key ideas**
> - Local businesses serve a limited area; national businesses operate across one country; international businesses work in multiple countries.
> - Local and small firms often face undercapitalisation and customer-acquisition challenges.
> - International expansion introduces longer supply chains and cross-border complexity, contributing to globalisation.

## 3.6 Businesses operate in an environment — stakeholders are important

> **In this section**
> You will identify key stakeholders, understand their interests, and see why environmental responsibility must be demonstrated with evidence, not just claims.

There is one thing that all businesses have in common: they are always integrated into an environment in which they operate. Therefore, they deal with many other people and/or businesses and need to consider their interests as well. Everyone that is (potentially) affected by the activities of a business and/or has an interest in what a business does is a **stakeholder**. Due to globalisation, the world has become a smaller place and the number of stakeholders has grown considerably. Furthermore, people are more environmentally conscious and expect businesses to preserve and protect the environment.

The diagram places the business at the centre, with owners, managers, suppliers, customers, employees, government, and communities at every level surrounding it.

**Owners** invest capital and want their investment to pay off through profit and rising business value — often reflected in share prices. Value can be realised by selling shares or the whole business; Runtastic GmbH was bought by Adidas for 220 million euros. Profit need not be the only goal: owners may also aim to solve customer problems or contribute to social welfare. Tina and Steve create value for customers and help preserve the environment by repairing and setting up used computers, reducing waste.

**Managers** (who may or may not be owners) and **employees** depend on the business for income; job security matters to them. Many identify with their work, and the business depends on them in return. Shared values and objectives among owners, managers, and employees are essential for long-term success. Figure 6 illustrates these interdependencies at AT&S.

![Interdependencies of people, values, objectives, and vision at AT&S — linking employees, managers, and the company through shared goals (source: AT&S annual report 2017/18)](/__l5e/assets-v1/73a6ba20-5847-41c6-95b2-525af9dd2213/tb-019-012.png)

**Suppliers** must deliver the right quality and quantity on time — if they fail, production stops. They expect payment and future orders; if the business fails, suppliers suffer too. A comparable mutual dependency exists with **customers**. **Communities** and the **government** are likewise affected by business activity.

Finally — and most importantly — businesses also need to act responsibly with the environment and the natural resources that they use. It is not sufficient only to claim to be environmentally friendly and to care about the environment (to "greenwash" the business activities), but it takes concrete activities and proven results. Many factors influence the success of a business. Considering the stakeholders and their (conflicting) interests is one of these factors. Other important factors include a suitable legal structure and a solid financial structure of the business, awareness of the market and changes in the market, awareness of costs and profitability. The following chapters deal with these topics.

> **Key ideas**
> - Stakeholders include owners, managers, employees, suppliers, customers, communities, and government.
> - Owners seek returns on investment; employees and managers depend on the firm for income and shared purpose.
> - Businesses must demonstrate genuine environmental responsibility with measurable results, not merely claims; stakeholder management is central to long-term success.
