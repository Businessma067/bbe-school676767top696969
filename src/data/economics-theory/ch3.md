# Chapter 3 — Focus on different types of businesses

Just as private households vary widely — from extended families to single-person homes, couples with or without children — businesses also take many different forms. What distinguishes one firm from another is the mix of factors of production it uses, the sector or sectors in which it operates, and its scale. Regardless of these differences, every business must pay attention to the stakeholders in its surroundings and to the natural environment.

## 3.1 Businesses combine different factors of production

As outlined in the introduction, a business is an organisation that supplies goods and/or services to customers. To do this, it brings together factors of production — the inputs required to create those goods and services. The principal factors are:

- labour (all human resources),

- land (all natural resources),

- capital (resources such as machinery, plant, vehicles, and financial assets), and

- entrepreneurship (which coordinates land, labour, and capital), as well as

- knowledge and technology.

The dominant factor in any given production process shapes the kind of business that emerges. Tina and Steve rely chiefly on their own knowledge and skills, together with labour, technology, some capital, and entrepreneurship. AT&S draws on every factor of production. A winemaker with extensive vineyards combines land, labour, capital, and entrepreneurship, though the operation may also depend heavily on personal knowledge, experience, and technology.

**Key ideas**

- Businesses exist to offer goods and/or services by combining factors of production.
- The main factors are labour, land, capital, entrepreneurship, and knowledge/technology.
- Different businesses emphasise different factors — from knowledge-based micro firms to capital-intensive manufacturers.

## 3.2 Businesses operate in the primary, secondary and/or tertiary sector

Depending on its activities, a business contributes to one or more of the three broad sectors of the economy. The three-sector model divides economic activity into:

The **primary sector** covers the extraction of raw materials from the earth. It includes farming, fishing, mining, and forestry. Economically less developed countries often rely heavily on this sector.

Businesses in the **secondary sector** convert raw materials into finished goods through manufacturing. They produce items such as cars, ships, machinery, printed circuit boards and IC substrates (as AT&S does), computers, and clothing.

The **tertiary sector** encompasses services — distribution, banking, insurance, coaching, and similar activities. Tina and Steve operate in this sector: they trade goods, develop software, and provide technical support.

As a country's economy develops, the tertiary sector typically gains in importance. In highly developed economies — such as EU member states with a high gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, which signals a high standard of living — services often account for more than 70% of total output. The primary sector, despite its vital role in food supply, represents only a small share.

GDP measures the total monetary value of final goods and services produced within a country's borders over a given period (usually one year). It therefore captures the overall level of economic activity. Rising GDP, adjusted for inflation, indicates economic growth. Yet GDP is not without controversy. Critics point out that not every source of income is included. GDP also says little about the quality or sustainability of growth, and it is an imperfect measure of economic wellbeing. For instance, GDP can rise after ecological disasters that trigger spending on rebuilding infrastructure and repairing damage. Even so, GDP tends to correlate with indicators of wellbeing such as health status and happiness.

**Key ideas**

- The three-sector model distinguishes primary (raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), and tertiary (services) activity.
- Advanced economies are dominated by the tertiary sector; developing economies rely more on the primary sector.
- GDP measures the value of final output within a country's borders and is used to track growth, though it has recognised limitations.

## 3.3 Businesses can be profit-oriented or not-for-profit

Most businesses plan to operate over the long term. To survive and grow, the majority seek to earn a profit — that is, to generate revenues that exceed costs and expenses. Profits matter to the firm itself because they can be reinvested, strengthening durability and sustainability. They also reward owners and investors for the risks they bear.

Not every organisation, however, is profit-oriented. Some businesses and organisations are not-for-profit and focus primarily on covering their costs. Even so, they must secure adequate revenues — or, in many cases, donations — to continue offering goods and services. Any surplus they generate can still be reinvested to improve services or expand projects. The Red Cross, the World Wildlife Fund, and Greenpeace are examples of such non-profit organisations (NPOs).

**Key ideas**

- Profit-oriented businesses aim for revenues above costs; profits support reinvestment and reward risk-taking owners.
- Not-for-profit organisations focus on covering costs rather than maximising profit, though surpluses can still be reinvested.
- Examples of NPOs include the Red Cross, the World Wildlife Fund, and Greenpeace.

## 3.4 Businesses come "in all sizes", large and small

Businesses range from micro and small enterprises through medium-sized firms to large corporations. Micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSME, or simply SME) make up the vast majority of firms. Around 99% of all businesses in the EU are SMEs. The European Commission classifies them as follows (see http://ec.europa.eu), as shown in table 1.

| Company category | Staff headcount | Turnover | OR | Balance sheet total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Micro | < 10 | ≤ €2 m | | ≤ €2 m |
| Small | < 50 | ≤ €10 m | | ≤ €10 m |
| Medium-sized | < 250 | ≤ €50 m | | ≤ €43 m |

*Table 1. Size categories of businesses*

The SME definition matters because it determines access to finance and eligibility for EU support programmes aimed at these enterprises. Tina and Steve run a micro company; even if they hire two assistants, they remain a micro company. AT&S, with roughly 10,000 employees, is a large company.

Business size also affects legal requirements for accounting (see chapter 6, Accounting). In many countries, smaller firms may use simplified accounting methods to determine profits or losses.

**Key ideas**

- EU businesses are classified as micro, small, or medium-sized based on headcount, turnover, and balance sheet total.
- SMEs account for about 99% of EU businesses and benefit from targeted support and financing.
- Size influences accounting obligations — smaller firms often face lighter reporting requirements.

## 3.5 Businesses may be local, national or international

A **local** (or regional) business serves a small, geographically limited area. Most of its customers live nearby. It does not reach national or international markets. Tina and Steve's firm is a local business, at least in its early stages. The main challenge for small local businesses is securing enough financial resources and building a solid customer base. Many owners struggle with undercapitalisation: personal funds are limited, and obtaining additional funding is difficult.

A **national** business serves the home market — the market within a single country — but not markets abroad. An insurance company or a bakery operating throughout Austria without foreign operations is a national business. Like a local firm, it must choose a suitable location. It must also decide how to deliver goods and services to customers across the country, which lengthens the supply chain considerably.

An **international** (or multinational) business produces and/or sells goods and services in more than one country. When the home market is too small, firms seek larger opportunities abroad. Operating internationally brings additional challenges: longer supply chains, differing legal and economic systems, varied cultures and languages, and possibly multiple currencies. The growth of multinational businesses has driven globalisation — the increasing integration of economies worldwide. AT&S is an international business with production plants in Asian countries and in Austria, selling its products to firms around the globe.

**Key ideas**

- Local businesses serve a limited area; national businesses operate across one country; international businesses work in multiple countries.
- Local and small firms often face undercapitalisation and customer-acquisition challenges.
- International expansion introduces longer supply chains and cross-border complexity, contributing to globalisation.

## 3.6 Businesses operate in an environment — stakeholders are important

Every business shares one characteristic: it operates within a wider environment. As a result, it interacts with many individuals and other organisations and must take their interests into account. A **stakeholder** is anyone who is (potentially) affected by a business's activities and/or has an interest in what it does. Globalisation has expanded the circle of stakeholders considerably. At the same time, people have become more environmentally aware and expect businesses to protect natural resources. Figure 5 shows the most important stakeholders of a business.

**Owners** are stakeholders because they invest capital and expect a return. They seek profit as compensation for risk and benefit when the firm's value rises — a gain often reflected in share prices. Value can be realised by selling shares or the entire business. For example, Adidas acquired Runtastic GmbH for 220 million euros. Profit need not be the sole objective; owners may also aim to solve customer problems (such as computer issues) or contribute to social welfare. Tina and Steve create value for customers and help protect the environment by repairing and setting up used computers, thereby reducing waste.

**Managers** (who may or may not also be owners) and **employees** are stakeholders because they rely on the business for income; job security matters to them. Many also identify with their work and their contribution to the firm. The relationship runs both ways — the business depends on its people. Shared values and objectives among owners, managers, and employees are essential for long-term success. Figure 6 illustrates these interdependencies at AT&S.

**Suppliers** are vital to production. Timely delivery of the right quality and quantity is essential; if a supplier fails, production stops. In return, suppliers expect payment and future orders. If the business fails, suppliers may suffer too. A comparable mutual dependency exists between a business and its **customers**. **Communities** — local, national, and international — and the **government** are likewise affected by and dependent on business activity.

Many factors shape business success. Managing stakeholder relationships — including their sometimes conflicting interests — is one of them. Others include an appropriate legal structure, a sound financial foundation, awareness of market conditions and changes, and attention to costs and profitability. The chapters that follow explore these topics in detail.

**Key ideas**

- Stakeholders include owners, managers, employees, suppliers, customers, communities, and government.
- Owners seek returns on investment; employees and managers depend on the firm for income and shared purpose.
- Businesses must demonstrate genuine environmental responsibility, not merely claim it; stakeholder management is central to long-term success.
