# Chapter 11 — Differentiation and single-variable optimization

This chapter begins with the first subtopic used in the practice bank: **Differentiation rules & mechanics**.

The core skill is not memorising disconnected formulas. It is learning to read a business quantity, decide **which function depends on the variable**, and then differentiate that function correctly.

In the BBE setting, derivatives usually appear as:

- **marginal cost**
- **marginal revenue**
- **marginal profit**
- **marginal product**
- the **slope of a tangent**

The notation changes, but the logic does not.

## 11.1 Differentiation rules & mechanics

### The derivative

The derivative of $f$ at $x$ is

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},
$$

when the limit exists.

In applications, $f'(x)$ tells the **instantaneous rate of change** of the quantity $f$ with respect to the variable $x$.

### Tangent lines

If the graph of $y=f(x)$ is differentiable at $x=a$, the tangent line there has slope $f'(a)$ and equation

$$
y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a).
$$

That is why a question can move back and forth between:

- the derivative value
- the slope of the graph
- the tangent line equation

### Rules you must know

For the first subsection, the most important rules are:

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{n})=nx^{n-1},
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}(cf(x))=cf'(x),
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(f(x)+g(x)\bigr)=f'(x)+g'(x),
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(f(x)g(x)\bigr)=f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x),
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right)=\dfrac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^{2}},
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(f(g(x))\bigr)=f'(g(x))g'(x).
$$

For logarithmic and exponential expressions, also remember:

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x=\dfrac{1}{x},
\qquad
\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x}=e^{x}.
$$

### The exam habit that matters

In BBE-style tasks, students often lose points not because the derivative rule is advanced, but because they differentiate the **wrong function**.

For example:

- if a stem gives **price** $p(q)$ and asks about **marginal revenue**, you need
  $$R(q)=q\,p(q)$$
  first, and only then differentiate $R$
- if a stem gives **total cost** $C(q)$ and asks for **average cost**, you need
  $$A(q)=\dfrac{C(q)}{q}$$
  first, and only then differentiate $A$

Always translate the story into the correct function before applying rules.

### What to expect in the practice bank

The first differentiation subsection focuses on:

- tangents and derivative values at named points
- power-rule derivatives inside business formulas
- product-rule revenue and response models
- quotient-rule average-cost and ratio models
- chain-rule derivatives for nested roots, powers, and logarithms
- interpretation of derivative units in context

## 11.2 Economic interpretation of the derivative

In economics, the word **marginal** usually means **a derivative**.

| Economic name | Calculus object |
|---|---|
| Marginal cost | $C'(Q)$ |
| Marginal revenue | $R'(Q)$ |
| Marginal profit | $P'(Q)=R'(Q)-C'(Q)$ |
| Marginal utility | $U'(x)$ |

Profit is written $P$, not $\pi$. Quantity is written $Q$. Price, when it appears, is the separate lowercase schedule $p(Q)$.

### What a marginal quantity says in words

If $C'(Q_0)=c$, then near output $Q_0$ each extra unit of output changes total cost by about $c$ euros.

More precisely,

$$
C(Q_0+\Delta Q)-C(Q_0)\approx C'(Q_0)\Delta Q
$$

for small $\Delta Q$. Putting $\Delta Q=1$ gives the familiar one-unit reading, which is useful but still an approximation.

The same language applies to revenue, profit, and utility: the derivative is a **local rate of change**, not the level of the function.

### Which function must be differentiated?

Read the economic question first.

- Extra cost of a little more output → differentiate **total cost** $C$, not average cost $A=\dfrac{C}{Q}$.
- Extra revenue of a little more output → differentiate **revenue** $R$, not the price schedule $p$ alone.
- Extra profit of a little more output → differentiate **profit** $P=R-C$.
- Extra satisfaction from a little more of a good → differentiate **utility** $U$.

### Marginal revenue versus price

If $R(Q)=Q\cdot p(Q)$ and price must fall to sell more, then

$$
R'(Q)=p(Q)+Q\cdot p'(Q).
$$

The second term is typically negative, so marginal revenue can lie **below** the current price.

### The expand-or-not test

Because $P'=R'-C'$:

- if $R'(Q)>C'(Q)$, then $P'(Q)>0$: a small expansion raises profit
- if $R'(Q)<C'(Q)$, then $P'(Q)<0$: a small expansion lowers profit
- if $R'(Q)=C'(Q)$, then $P'(Q)=0$: a stationary point for profit

Zero marginal profit is **not** the same as zero total profit.

### Average versus marginal

Average cost $A(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q}$ and marginal cost $C'(Q)$ are different objects. At an interior minimum of average cost one has the classical relation

$$
C'(Q)=A(Q).
$$

The same pattern appears for average tax rates, average saving ratios, and average utility: an average is a quotient; a marginal quantity is a derivative.

### What to expect in the practice bank

Subsection 11.2 emphasises verbal economic logic:

- recognizing which function a scenario asks you to differentiate
- reading reported derivatives in euros-per-unit language
- comparing marginal revenue and marginal cost without heavy algebra
- separating levels ($C$, $R$, $P$) from rates ($C'$, $R'$, $P'$)
- comparing two firms, and reading piecewise cost, revenue, or profit rules

## 11.3 Finding and classifying optima

Optimization is **one continuous skill**: find candidate inputs, then prove what each candidate is (local max, local min, or neither), and finally compare values when a global answer on an interval is required.

### Where the slope must be zero

If a differentiable function $f$ has a local or global extremum at an **interior** point $c$, then

$$
f'(c)=0.
$$

In words: at an interior peak or lowest point the tangent is flat. Points where $f'(c)=0$ are only **candidates**. The same equation can sit at a maximum, a minimum, or neither (for example a horizontal inflection).

### Candidate list on an interval

To locate extrema of $f$ on an interval $I$, search among:

1. interior points where $f'(x)=0$
2. endpoints of $I$, if they belong to $I$
3. interior points where $f'$ fails to exist

An endpoint maximum need **not** satisfy $f'=0$.

### Reading the first derivative (sign chart)

At a point $c$ where $f'(c)=0$:

- if $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$, then $c$ is a local maximum
- if $f'$ changes from $-$ to $+$, then $c$ is a local minimum
- if $f'$ does not change sign, then $c$ is neither

### Reading the second derivative

If $f'(c)=0$ and $f$ is twice differentiable near $c$:

- $f''(c)<0$ ⇒ strict local maximum (downward bend)
- $f''(c)>0$ ⇒ strict local minimum (upward bend)
- $f''(c)=0$ ⇒ **inconclusive** — use a sign chart or compare nearby values

### Necessary versus sufficient curvature

$f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)<0$ is a **sufficient** test for a strict local maximum.

At an interior local maximum of a twice differentiable function one must have $f''(c)\le 0$, but $f''(c)=0$ can still occur at a peak. Do not confuse “necessary” with “sufficient.”

### Local versus global

A local maximum is best only nearby. On a closed interval, a global maximum is found by comparing function **values** at all candidates (flat-slope points and endpoints). A local second-derivative test does not by itself prove a global optimum.

### Economic reading

For profit $P(Q)=R(Q)-C(Q)$:

- interior profit peak candidates solve $P'(Q)=0$, or equivalently $R'(Q)=C'(Q)$
- a local profit peak needs $P''(Q)<0$ (or a sign change of $P'$ from $+$ to $-$)

“Slope zero” is not the same as “optimal.” Minimizing cost and maximizing profit are different problems on different functions.

### Constraints reduced to one variable

Many area, budget, and resource stories start with a constraint (fixed fence length, fixed budget, fixed output). Solve the constraint for one variable, substitute into the objective, then optimize the resulting **single-variable** function with the tests above.

### Elasticity and revenue

For a demand curve $Q=D(p)$, the price elasticity is

$$
El_{Q}(p)=\dfrac{D'(p)\cdot p}{D(p)}.
$$

Revenue $R(p)=p\cdot D(p)$ has a flat slope where $El_{Q}(p)=-1$ (when that point exists and the usual regularity holds). Always compute $R'$ from the given demand rather than memorising the slogan alone.

- profit maximisation and cost minimisation
- area and resource constraints reduced to one variable
- elasticity and revenue
- two-scenario comparisons with opposite second-derivative signs
- detailed written reasoning: slope-zero candidates, then local shape, then global comparison when needed

## 11.4 Interpreting graphs without algebra

Often the exam shows a **picture** of $f$ or of $f'$ and asks you to reason about increasing/decreasing behaviour, local extrema, and concavity **without computing a formula**. Read **coordinates and heights** off the figure.

### If the figure is the graph of $f'$

Read three different messages from the same picture:

1. **Sign of $f'$** — where the curve is above the horizontal axis, $f'>0$ and $f$ is increasing; where it is below, $f'<0$ and $f$ is decreasing.
2. **Zeros of $f'$ with a sign change** — a change from $-$ to $+$ is a local minimum of $f$; a change from $+$ to $-$ is a local maximum of $f$.
3. **Whether $f'$ itself is rising or falling** — rising $f'$ means $f''>0$ (concave up); falling $f'$ means $f''<0$ (concave down). A highest or lowest point on the graph of $f'$ is an inflection point of $f$.

Do **not** confuse a maximum of $f'$ with a maximum of $f$. At a peak of $f'$ the slope of $f$ is usually large and positive — $f$ is climbing steeply, not sitting at a summit.

### If the figure is the graph of $f$

Local highest and lowest points of the drawn curve are local extrema. Rising stretches are increasing intervals; falling stretches are decreasing. A change from cup-shaped to cap-shaped bending is an inflection. An axis crossing of $f$ is a root ($f=0$), which is **not** the same as a horizontal tangent ($f'=0$).

### Several curves on one plane

When two or three curves share the **same axes**, the stem usually names them (for example brown $=f'$, green $=f''$). Use the shared scale to compare values at a given $x$, count zeros, and check whether extrema of one curve line up with zeros of another.

### What to expect in the practice bank

Subsection 11.4 is picture-first:

- single graphs of $f'$ or $f$ that are **not** plain parabolas (cubics, quartics, rational shapes, skewed exponential products)
- when two or three curves appear, they share **one coordinate plane** (one pair of axes, with a legend)
- stems ask about **concrete coordinates and values** (signs at a given $x$, zero locations, height comparisons), not “which curve is $f$ / $f'$ / $f''$”
- when the figure already shows the needed heights and signs, reason from the picture — do not invent algebraic formulas
- no wavy trigonometric graphs in this bank
- about forty True/False items spanning easy single-curve reading through harder multi-curve comparisons
- items are ordered by difficulty (easier first), so the bank rises steadily instead of resetting halfway

## 11.5 Exam-style tasks

These are the hardest items in the chapter. A single stem is a **business story** that can mix any of the earlier skills: differentiation rules, marginal interpretation, optimisation, piecewise schedules, elasticity, average-cost changes, and comparisons between rivals.

### How exam stems are built

Typical ingredients (not every task uses all of them):

- a revenue function $R$ and a cost function $C$, so profit is $P=R-C$
- a piecewise time or cost schedule that switches at a capacity threshold
- an average-cost derivative $AC'=(\,C/Q\,)'$ read as the approximate change in average cost from one extra unit
- a rival firm with the same level at one output but a different marginal curve — integrate the inequality to compare later levels
- demand $D(p)$, revenue $R(p)=p\cdot D(p)$, and elasticity $El_{Q}(p)=D'(p)\cdot p/D(p)$

### Working method

1. **Write the objects you need** — $P$, $AC$, $R'$, $C'$, one-sided slopes of a piecewise function.
2. **Solve for critical points** on each smooth piece; check endpoints and switches separately.
3. **Do not confuse** a maximum of revenue with a maximum of profit, or continuity of $C$ with equality of left and right derivatives.
4. **For rival comparisons**, if $R_e(a)=R(a)$ and $R_e'>R'$ on $[a,b]$, then $R_e(b)>R(b)$.

### What to expect in the practice bank

- about fifteen multi-select True/False cases, all difficulty $5/5$
- long worded contexts with several formulas in one stem
- statements that combine calculation with verbal economic logic
- explanations in the same tutoring voice as the binomial exam bank: derive, then conclude
