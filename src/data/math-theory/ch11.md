# Chapter 11 — Differentiation and single-variable optimization

Differentiation is the mathematics of **instantaneous rates of change**. In a BBE exam the same idea appears under many names: the slope of a tangent, marginal cost, marginal revenue, marginal profit, marginal utility, or “how much average cost changes when output rises by a little.”

The situations look different — chairs, advertising clicks, fence length, inventory, taxes — but the workflow is always the same:

1. Identify the **function** the claim is really about.
2. Differentiate that function correctly.
3. Interpret the derivative (or the critical point) in the story’s units.
4. When several claims appear, keep levels ($C$, $R$, $P$) separate from rates ($C^{\prime}$, $R^{\prime}$, $P^{\prime}$).

This guide starts from the definition of the derivative and builds every rule and economic reading you need for the chapter. Later sections show how BBE exam stems combine those ideas: piecewise schedules, rival comparisons, elasticity, graph reading, and mixed algebra-plus-figure tasks.

## Learning objectives

- Read the Newton quotient and the tangent-line equation as two views of the same derivative.
- Apply the power, sum, product, quotient and chain rules, including $\ln$ and $e^{x}$.
- Translate a business story into the correct function before differentiating.
- Interpret marginal cost, revenue, profit and utility in euros-per-unit language.
- Decide whether a small expansion raises or lowers profit by comparing $R^{\prime}$ and $C^{\prime}$.
- Separate average quantities from marginal quantities, and use $C^{\prime}=A$ at an interior average-cost minimum.
- Find and classify optima with first-derivative sign charts and the second-derivative test.
- Reduce a constrained story to one variable, then optimize.
- Connect price elasticity of demand to the slope of revenue.
- Read graphs of $f$, $f^{\prime}$ and $f^{\prime\prime}$ without inventing formulas.
- Solve BBE-style multi-claim stems that mix piecewise rules, rivals, taxes, figures and algebra.

## 11.1 What the derivative is

### The Newton quotient

Fix a function $f$ and a point $x$. For a small step $h\neq 0$, the average rate of change of $f$ from $x$ to $x+h$ is

$$
\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
$$

The derivative is the limit of that average as the step shrinks to zero:

$$
f^{\prime}(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},
$$

when the limit exists. In words: $f^{\prime}(x)$ is the **instantaneous rate of change** of $f$ with respect to $x$.

If the limit exists at every point of an interval, $f$ is **differentiable** there. If left-hand and right-hand limits of the quotient disagree at a kink, the derivative fails to exist at that point even when $f$ is continuous.

**Example.** Let $f(x)=x^{2}$ and compute the derivative from the definition at a general $x$:

$$
\dfrac{(x+h)^{2}-x^{2}}{h}=\dfrac{x^{2}+2xh+h^{2}-x^{2}}{h}=2x+h.
$$

Sending $h\to 0$ gives $f^{\prime}(x)=2x$. The same limit at $x=3$ is $6$.

Exam stems sometimes ask you to recognize that a reported Newton quotient with a concrete $h$ is only an **approximation** to $f^{\prime}(x)$, not the exact derivative. Keeping one extra digit until the comparison is finished matters when a claim says “more than” or “less than.”

### The tangent line

If $f$ is differentiable at $x=a$, the graph $y=f(x)$ has a unique non-vertical tangent line at the point $\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)$. That line has slope $f^{\prime}(a)$ and equation

$$
y=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)(x-a).
$$

So a question can move freely between three equivalent objects:

- the number $f^{\prime}(a)$,
- the slope of the graph at $x=a$,
- the equation of the tangent line at that point.

**Example.** For $f(x)=x^{2}$, at $a=3$ we have $f(3)=9$ and $f^{\prime}(3)=6$, so the tangent is

$$
y=9+6(x-3)=6x-9.
$$

A claim that the tangent at $x=3$ is $y=6x+9$ is false: the slope is correct, but the intercept is wrong.

### Units and language

Always attach units to the derivative. If $C(Q)$ is total cost in euros and $Q$ is output in chairs, then $C^{\prime}(Q)$ is measured in **euros per chair**. If $U(x)$ is utility and $x$ is litres of juice, then $U^{\prime}(x)$ is utility per litre. The derivative is a rate, not a stock.

A useful one-unit approximation is

$$
f(a+1)-f(a)\approx f^{\prime}(a),
$$

but it is still an approximation. For a general small step $\Delta x$,

$$
f(a+\Delta x)-f(a)\approx f^{\prime}(a)\,\Delta x.
$$

## 11.2 Differentiation rules

The definition is rarely used on every exam item. Instead you build derivatives from a short list of rules. Learn each rule with its **shape**, not only its symbols.

### Constant multiple and sum

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(c\,f(x)\bigr)=c\,f^{\prime}(x),
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(f(x)+g(x)\bigr)=f^{\prime}(x)+g^{\prime}(x).
$$

Constants factor out; sums differentiate term by term. There is no product hidden in $f+g$.

### Power rule

For any real exponent $n$ (on the domain where the expression makes sense),

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{n})=n\,x^{n-1}.
$$

Rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers before differentiating:

$$
\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2},\qquad
\dfrac{1}{x}=x^{-1},\qquad
\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}=x^{-2},\qquad
\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}=x^{2/3}.
$$

**Example.**

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(5x^{4}-3x^{-2}+7\bigr)=20x^{3}+6x^{-3}=20x^{3}+\dfrac{6}{x^{3}}.
$$

### Product rule

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl(f(x)g(x)\bigr)=f^{\prime}(x)g(x)+f(x)g^{\prime}(x).
$$

In words: differentiate the first factor and keep the second, then keep the first and differentiate the second, then add.

**Example.** Revenue $R(Q)=Q\cdot p(Q)$ with $p(Q)=40-2Q$:

$$
R(Q)=40Q-2Q^{2},
$$

$$
R^{\prime}(Q)=40-4Q.
$$

You can also keep the product form:

$$
R^{\prime}(Q)=1\cdot p(Q)+Q\cdot p^{\prime}(Q)=p(Q)+Q(-2)=40-2Q-2Q=40-4Q.
$$

Both routes must agree. If they do not, a product-rule sign or a missed factor is usually the cause.

### Quotient rule

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right)=\dfrac{f^{\prime}(x)g(x)-f(x)g^{\prime}(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}}.
$$

The minus sign is ordered: derivative of the top times the bottom, **minus** top times derivative of the bottom, all over bottom squared.

Average cost is the classic BBE quotient:

$$
A(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q},\qquad
A^{\prime}(Q)=\dfrac{C^{\prime}(Q)\,Q-C(Q)\cdot 1}{Q^{2}}.
$$

**Example.** Let $C(Q)=Q^{2}+10Q+25$. Then

$$
A(Q)=Q+10+\dfrac{25}{Q},
$$

$$
A^{\prime}(Q)=1-\dfrac{25}{Q^{2}}.
$$

At $Q=5$, $A^{\prime}(5)=0$. That is a candidate for an average-cost stationary point; classification needs a further test (see Section 11.4).

### Chain rule

If $y=f(u)$ and $u=g(x)$, then

$$
\dfrac{dy}{dx}=f^{\prime}(g(x))\,g^{\prime}(x).
$$

Differentiate the outer function, leave the inner expression alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inner expression.

**Example.** Advertising response $S(a)=\sqrt{9+4a}=(9+4a)^{1/2}$:

$$
S^{\prime}(a)=\dfrac{1}{2}(9+4a)^{-1/2}\cdot 4=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{9+4a}}.
$$

**Example.** Nested cost $C(Q)=\bigl(2Q^{2}+1\bigr)^{3}$:

$$
C^{\prime}(Q)=3\bigl(2Q^{2}+1\bigr)^{2}\cdot 4Q=12Q\bigl(2Q^{2}+1\bigr)^{2}.
$$

### Logarithm and exponential

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x=\dfrac{1}{x}\quad(x>0),
$$

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x}=e^{x}.
$$

With the chain rule:

$$
\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln g(x)=\dfrac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)},
\qquad
\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{g(x)}=e^{g(x)}g^{\prime}(x).
$$

Learning-curve and continuous-growth models often use these forms. For $\ln\bigl(C(Q)\bigr)$, the derivative is $\dfrac{C^{\prime}(Q)}{C(Q)}$, not $C^{\prime}(Q)$ alone.

### The habit that prevents most rule errors

Before any differentiation, write one sentence:

> I need the derivative of **this** function with respect to **that** variable.

Common traps:

- The stem gives price $p(Q)$ but asks about **marginal revenue** → form $R(Q)=Q\,p(Q)$ first.
- The stem gives total cost $C(Q)$ but asks about **average cost** → form $A(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q}$ first.
- The stem gives profit pieces separately → form $P=R-C$ before setting $P^{\prime}=0$.

Differentiating the wrong object is more common than misremembering the product rule.

## 11.3 Economic meaning of the derivative

In economics, **marginal** almost always means **a derivative**.

| Economic name | Calculus object | Typical units |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal cost | $C^{\prime}(Q)$ | euros per unit of output |
| Marginal revenue | $R^{\prime}(Q)$ | euros per unit of output |
| Marginal profit | $P^{\prime}(Q)=R^{\prime}(Q)-C^{\prime}(Q)$ | euros per unit of output |
| Marginal product | derivative of output with respect to an input | output per unit of input |
| Marginal utility | $U^{\prime}(x)$ | utility per unit of the good |

Course convention in this bank: profit is written $P$ (not $\pi$). Quantity is $Q$. The price **schedule** is the separate lowercase function $p(Q)$.

### Marginal cost in detail

**Marginal cost** is the derivative of **total cost**:

$$
MC(Q)=C^{\prime}(Q).
$$

It answers one precise question: near the current output $Q$, how many euros does total cost rise when output rises by a little?

If $C^{\prime}(Q_{0})=12$, then

$$
C(Q_{0}+\Delta Q)-C(Q_{0})\approx 12\,\Delta Q
$$

for small $\Delta Q$. With $\Delta Q=1$ one says “one more unit costs about $12$ euros,” but that one-unit slogan is still an approximation to the derivative.

Three objects must stay separate:

- **Total cost** $C(Q)$ — euros for producing $Q$ units in all.
- **Average cost** $A(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q}$ — euros **per unit** if $Q$ units are produced.
- **Marginal cost** $C^{\prime}(Q)$ — the **local slope** of total cost at $Q$.

A positive marginal cost only says that total cost is rising. It does **not** say that average cost is rising. Average cost falls precisely when $A^{\prime}(Q)<0$, which can happen while $C^{\prime}(Q)>0$.

**Worked example.** Let $C(Q)=200+12Q+\dfrac{3}{100}Q^{2}$. Then

$$
C^{\prime}(Q)=12+\dfrac{3}{50}Q.
$$

At $Q=100$,

$$
C^{\prime}(100)=12+\dfrac{3}{50}\cdot 100=18.
$$

So near $100$ units, each extra unit adds about $18$ euros to total cost. Average cost at the same point is

$$
A(100)=\dfrac{C(100)}{100}=\dfrac{200+1200+300}{100}=17,
$$

which is **not** equal to $18$. Marginal and average meet only at special outputs (an interior minimum of $A$).

On a figure, rising $MC$ against falling or flatter $MR$ is the usual geometry for a profit maximum: expand while $MR>MC$, and stop where they cross with $MC$ cutting from below.

[[FIGURE:diff-ac-mc|Figure — MC cuts AC at an interior average-cost minimum. That is an AC story, not automatically a profit maximum.]]

### Reading a reported derivative in words

If $C^{\prime}(Q_{0})=12$, then near output $Q_{0}$ each extra unit of output changes total cost by about $12$ euros. The same language applies to revenue, profit and utility. The derivative never tells you the **level** of the function by itself. Knowing $C^{\prime}(10)=12$ does not tell you $C(10)$.

### Which function must be differentiated?

Match the economic question to the object:

- Extra cost of a little more output → differentiate **total cost** $C$, not average cost.
- Extra revenue of a little more output → differentiate **revenue** $R$, not price alone.
- Extra profit of a little more output → differentiate **profit** $P=R-C$.
- Extra satisfaction from a little more of a good → differentiate **utility** $U$.

### Marginal revenue versus price

If selling more requires a lower price along $p(Q)$, then

$$
R(Q)=Q\cdot p(Q),
$$

$$
R^{\prime}(Q)=p(Q)+Q\cdot p^{\prime}(Q).
$$

When $p^{\prime}(Q)<0$, the second term is negative, so

$$
R^{\prime}(Q)<p(Q)
$$

at interior points with $Q>0$. Marginal revenue lies **below** the current price. Confusing $R^{\prime}$ with $p$ is a classic exam error.

**Example.** Let $p(Q)=50-Q$. Then $R(Q)=50Q-Q^{2}$ and $R^{\prime}(Q)=50-2Q$. At $Q=10$,

$$
p(10)=40,\qquad R^{\prime}(10)=30.
$$

Price is $40$; marginal revenue is only $30$.

### The expand-or-not test

Because $P^{\prime}=R^{\prime}-C^{\prime}$:

- if $R^{\prime}(Q)>C^{\prime}(Q)$, then $P^{\prime}(Q)>0$: a small expansion **raises** profit;
- if $R^{\prime}(Q)<C^{\prime}(Q)$, then $P^{\prime}(Q)<0$: a small expansion **lowers** profit;
- if $R^{\prime}(Q)=C^{\prime}(Q)$, then $P^{\prime}(Q)=0$: a stationary point for profit.

Zero marginal profit is **not** zero total profit. A firm can have $P^{\prime}(Q)=0$ while $P(Q)$ is large and positive, or while $P(Q)$ is negative.

### Average versus marginal

Average cost is the quotient

$$
A(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q}.
$$

Marginal cost is the derivative $C^{\prime}(Q)$. They answer different questions:

- $A(Q)$: cost **per unit** if you produce $Q$ units in total;
- $C^{\prime}(Q)$: how total cost changes when output rises a little from $Q$.

At an interior minimum of average cost (where $A^{\prime}(Q)=0$ and the second-derivative or sign test confirms a min), the classical relation

$$
C^{\prime}(Q)=A(Q)
$$

holds. The same pattern appears for other averages: an average is a quotient; a marginal quantity is a derivative of a total.

**Worked sketch.** From

$$
A^{\prime}(Q)=\dfrac{C^{\prime}(Q)\,Q-C(Q)}{Q^{2}},
$$

setting $A^{\prime}(Q)=0$ with $Q\neq 0$ forces $C^{\prime}(Q)Q=C(Q)$, hence $C^{\prime}(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q}=A(Q)$.

### Comparing two firms at one output

Two rivals can have the **same** cost (or revenue, or profit) at one output $Q_{0}$ and still have different futures. What matters for a small move is the marginal comparison at $Q_{0}$. What matters for a later level comparison on an interval is the integral of the marginal gap (Section 11.6).

## 11.4 Finding and classifying optima

Optimization is one continuous skill: list candidates, classify each candidate, then compare values when a global answer is required.

### Where the slope must be zero

If a differentiable function $f$ has a local extremum at an **interior** point $c$ of its domain, then

$$
f^{\prime}(c)=0.
$$

At an interior peak or trough the tangent is flat. Points where $f^{\prime}(c)=0$ are only **candidates**. The same equation can sit at a maximum, a minimum, or neither (for example a horizontal inflection).

### Candidate list on an interval

To locate extrema of $f$ on an interval $I$, search among:

1. interior points where $f^{\prime}(x)=0$;
2. endpoints of $I$, if they belong to $I$;
3. interior points where $f^{\prime}$ fails to exist (kinks, corners, vertical tangents).

An endpoint maximum need **not** satisfy $f^{\prime}=0$. On a closed interval $[a,b]$, the global max and min exist for a continuous $f$, and they occur among these candidates.

### First-derivative sign chart

At a point $c$ where $f^{\prime}(c)=0$ (or where $f^{\prime}$ changes across a kink):

- if $f^{\prime}$ changes from $+$ to $-$, then $c$ is a local **maximum**;
- if $f^{\prime}$ changes from $-$ to $+$, then $c$ is a local **minimum**;
- if $f^{\prime}$ does not change sign, then $c$ is neither (often a horizontal inflection).

**Example.** Let $f(x)=x^{3}$. Then $f^{\prime}(x)=3x^{2}$, so $f^{\prime}(0)=0$, but $f^{\prime}$ is nonnegative on both sides. There is no local max or min at $0$.

### Second-derivative test

If $f^{\prime}(c)=0$ and $f$ is twice differentiable near $c$:

- $f^{\prime\prime}(c)<0$ ⇒ strict local **maximum** (the graph bends down);
- $f^{\prime\prime}(c)>0$ ⇒ strict local **minimum** (the graph bends up);
- $f^{\prime\prime}(c)=0$ ⇒ **inconclusive** — return to a sign chart or compare nearby values.

### Necessary versus sufficient curvature

The pair $f^{\prime}(c)=0$ and $f^{\prime\prime}(c)<0$ is a **sufficient** test for a strict local maximum.

At an interior local maximum of a twice differentiable function one must have $f^{\prime\prime}(c)\le 0$, but $f^{\prime\prime}(c)=0$ can still occur at a genuine peak. Do not confuse “necessary” with “sufficient,” and do not treat $f^{\prime\prime}(c)=0$ as an automatic rejection of a maximum.

### Local versus global

A local maximum is best only in a neighbourhood. On a closed interval, a global maximum is found by comparing function **values** at all candidates (flat-slope points, kinks, and endpoints). A local second-derivative test does not by itself prove a global optimum on a bounded domain.

**Example.** Profit $P(Q)=-Q^{3}+12Q^{2}-21Q$ on $[0,10]$. Then

$$
P^{\prime}(Q)=-3Q^{2}+24Q-21=-3(Q^{2}-8Q+7)=-3(Q-1)(Q-7).
$$

Critical points: $Q=1$ and $Q=7$. Second derivatives:

$$
P^{\prime\prime}(Q)=-6Q+24,\qquad P^{\prime\prime}(1)=18>0,\qquad P^{\prime\prime}(7)=-18<0.
$$

So $Q=1$ is a local min and $Q=7$ is a local max. For the global answer on $[0,10]$, evaluate:

$$
P(0),\;P(1),\;P(7),\;P(10)
$$

and compare the four numbers. The local max at $7$ might or might not win against an endpoint.

### Economic reading of optima

For profit $P(Q)=R(Q)-C(Q)$:

- interior profit-peak candidates solve $P^{\prime}(Q)=0$, or equivalently $R^{\prime}(Q)=C^{\prime}(Q)$;
- a local profit peak needs $P^{\prime\prime}(Q)<0$, or a sign change of $P^{\prime}$ from $+$ to $-$.

Minimizing cost and maximizing profit are different problems on different functions. Maximizing revenue ($R^{\prime}=0$) is yet another problem: its solution need not maximize $P$.

### Constraints reduced to one variable

Many area, budget, fence and resource stories start with a constraint. The method is always:

1. Solve the constraint for one variable in terms of the other.
2. Substitute into the objective to get a **single-variable** function.
3. Differentiate, find candidates, classify, and (if needed) compare endpoint values allowed by the constraint.

**Example.** A rectangular enclosure uses $L$ metres of fencing for three sides (two widths $x$ and one length $y$), so $2x+y=L$ and area $A=xy$. Then $y=L-2x$ and

$$
A(x)=x(L-2x)=Lx-2x^{2},\qquad 0\le x\le \dfrac{L}{2}.
$$

$$
A^{\prime}(x)=L-4x=0\Rightarrow x=\dfrac{L}{4},\qquad y=\dfrac{L}{2}.
$$

Since $A^{\prime\prime}(x)=-4<0$, this critical point is a maximum.

### Elasticity and revenue

For a demand curve $Q=D(p)$, the **price elasticity of demand** is

$$
El_{Q}(p)=\dfrac{D^{\prime}(p)\cdot p}{D(p)}.
$$

Revenue as a function of price is $R(p)=p\cdot D(p)$, so

$$
R^{\prime}(p)=D(p)+p\,D^{\prime}(p)=D(p)\bigl(1+El_{Q}(p)\bigr).
$$

Where demand is differentiable and $D(p)\neq 0$, one has $R^{\prime}(p)=0$ precisely when $El_{Q}(p)=-1$. Always compute $R^{\prime}$ from the given demand rather than memorising the slogan alone, and check that the stationary point is the type of extremum the claim asserts.

**Example.** Let $D(p)=100-2p$ for $0\le p\le 50$. Then

$$
El_{Q}(p)=\dfrac{-2\cdot p}{100-2p}=\dfrac{-p}{50-p}.
$$

Setting $El_{Q}(p)=-1$ gives $p=50-p$, so $p=25$. Also $R(p)=100p-2p^{2}$ has $R^{\prime}(p)=100-4p$, which vanishes at the same $p=25$.

## 11.5 Interpreting graphs without algebra

Often the exam shows a **picture** of $f$ or of $f^{\prime}$ and asks you to reason about increasing/decreasing behaviour, local extrema and concavity **without computing a formula**. Read coordinates and heights off the figure. Do not invent an algebraic expression “that looks similar.”

### Worked pattern — the figure is the graph of $f^{\prime}$

[[FIGURE:diff-fprime-sign|Figure — Graph of $f^{\prime}$. Zeros at $x=1$ and $x=5$; $f^{\prime}>0$ between them.]]

Read the picture in three layers:

1. **Sign of $f^{\prime}$.** Above the axis $\Rightarrow f$ increasing; below $\Rightarrow f$ decreasing.
2. **Zeros with a sign change.** From $-$ to $+$ at $x=1$ $\Rightarrow$ local **minimum** of $f$. From $+$ to $-$ at $x=5$ $\Rightarrow$ local **maximum** of $f$.
3. **Slope of $f^{\prime}$ itself.** Rising $f^{\prime}$ means $f^{\prime\prime}>0$ (concave up). Falling $f^{\prime}$ means $f^{\prime\prime}<0$ (concave down). A peak or trough of $f^{\prime}$ is an inflection candidate for $f$.

Do **not** confuse a maximum of $f^{\prime}$ with a maximum of $f$. At a peak of $f^{\prime}$ the slope of $f$ is usually large and positive: $f$ is climbing steeply, not sitting at a summit.

**How to answer a typical claim on this figure.**

1. Name what is plotted ($f^{\prime}$, not $f$).
2. At the $x$-value in the claim, read the sign or the crossing.
3. Translate into a statement about $f$ (increasing / local max / inflection).
4. Only then decide True/False.

### Worked pattern — the figure is the graph of $f$

[[FIGURE:diff-f-extrema|Figure — Graph of $f$ with a local max at $a$, a local min at $b$, and an inflection between them.]]

Local highest and lowest points of the drawn curve are local extrema. Rising stretches are increasing intervals; falling stretches are decreasing. A change from cup-shaped to cap-shaped bending is an inflection. An axis crossing of $f$ is a root ($f=0$), which is **not** the same as a horizontal tangent ($f^{\prime}=0$).

### If the figure is the graph of $f^{\prime\prime}$

Sign of $f^{\prime\prime}$ is concavity of $f$. Zeros of $f^{\prime\prime}$ are inflection candidates for $f$, provided concavity actually changes. The height of $f^{\prime\prime}$ is **not** the height of $f$ or of $f^{\prime}$.

### Several curves on one plane

When two or three curves share the **same axes**, the stem usually names them (for example brown $=f^{\prime}$, green $=f^{\prime\prime}$). Use the shared scale to:

- compare values at a given $x$,
- count zeros,
- check whether extrema of one curve line up with zeros of another.

Typical true relations on one plane:

- a zero of $f^{\prime}$ with a $+/-$ sign change aligns with a local max of $f$;
- a zero of $f^{\prime\prime}$ with a concavity change aligns with an inflection of $f$;
- an extremum of $f^{\prime}$ aligns with a zero of $f^{\prime\prime}$.

### Economic multi-curve planes

BBE figures often plot several business curves together:

- $MC$ against $MR$ — expand while $MR>MC$; a crossing with the right second-order story is a profit stationary point;
- $AC$ against $MC$ — $MC$ cuts $AC$ at an interior $AC$ minimum;
- $MU$ against opportunity cost — consume more while marginal utility exceeds the opportunity-cost line;
- twin $P^{\prime}$ curves for two firms — compare signs and zeros, not the levels of $P$ unless those levels are also given.

A common trap is to treat a crossing of $MC$ and $AC$ as a profit maximum. That crossing is about average cost, not about $R^{\prime}=C^{\prime}$.

### Checklist for graph claims

1. Read the legend: which curve is drawn?
2. Translate the claim into a statement about signs, zeros, heights or slopes.
3. Check the figure at the named $x$-coordinate.
4. Conclude True/False from what is actually visible — not from a formula you wish were there.
5. On multi-curve planes, refuse to mix “$AC$ minimum” with “profit maximum” unless the figure forces them to coincide.

## 11.6 Exam-style BBE methods

Subsection 11.5 mixes everything above into long multi-claim stems. The difficulty is rarely one exotic formula. It is keeping several objects straight while the story switches between revenue, cost, averages, piecewise schedules, rivals and figures.

### Step 0 — Name the objects

Before calculating, write a short list:

- totals: $R$, $C$, $P=R-C$, $U$, $T$ (time), …
- averages: $AC=\dfrac{C}{Q}$, average tax, …
- marginals: $R^{\prime}$, $C^{\prime}$, $P^{\prime}$, $AC^{\prime}$, …
- any switch points: capacity thresholds, tax brackets, kink locations.

If a claim mentions “average cost rises,” you need the sign of $AC^{\prime}$, not the sign of $C^{\prime}$.

### Method A — Profit from separate $R$ and $C$

Given smooth $R$ and $C$:

$$
P=R-C,\qquad P^{\prime}=R^{\prime}-C^{\prime},\qquad P^{\prime\prime}=R^{\prime\prime}-C^{\prime\prime}.
$$

Interior candidates: $R^{\prime}=C^{\prime}$. Classify with $P^{\prime\prime}$ or a sign chart. Compare with endpoints or capacity limits when the domain is restricted.

**Typical false claim:** “Revenue is maximized at the same output as profit.” Only true if that common point happens to satisfy both $R^{\prime}=0$ and $R^{\prime}=C^{\prime}$, which forces $C^{\prime}=0$ there — not the usual case.

### Method B — Piecewise schedules

A build-time, cost or tax rule may switch at a threshold $Q=Q_{*}$:

$$
T(Q)=\begin{cases} T_{1}(Q), & Q\le Q_{*},\\ T_{2}(Q), & Q>Q_{*}. \end{cases}
$$

Work piece by piece:

1. Differentiate on each open piece where the formula is smooth.
2. Check continuity of the level at $Q_{*}$ if the stem claims continuity.
3. Compare left- and right-hand derivatives at $Q_{*}$. Equality of levels does **not** imply equality of slopes.
4. For optimization on an interval that contains $Q_{*}$, include $Q_{*}$ itself as a candidate, together with critical points on each piece and the endpoints.

**Typical false claim:** “Because cost is continuous at the capacity threshold, marginal cost is also continuous there.” Continuity of $C$ does not force $C_{-}^{\prime}=C_{+}^{\prime}$.

### Method C — Rival comparison by integrating a marginal gap

Suppose two firms satisfy $R_{e}(a)=R(a)$ at some output $a$, and

$$
R_{e}^{\prime}(Q)>R^{\prime}(Q)\quad\text{for all }Q\in(a,b).
$$

Then the gap $R_{e}-R$ is strictly increasing on $[a,b]$, so

$$
R_{e}(b)>R(b).
$$

The same pattern works for cost or profit with the appropriate inequality direction. You do **not** need antiderivative formulas if the stem already gives the marginal comparison on the whole interval; the integral of a positive function is positive.

**Typical false claim:** “Same level at $a$ and larger marginal on $(a,b)$ still leaves the levels equal at $b$.” False — the gap widens.

### Method D — Average-cost change from one extra unit

The derivative $AC^{\prime}(Q)$ is the local rate of change of average cost. Claims such as “producing one more unit lowers average cost” are statements about the **sign** of $AC^{\prime}$ near the named output (with the usual one-unit approximation). Compute

$$
AC^{\prime}(Q)=\dfrac{C^{\prime}(Q)\,Q-C(Q)}{Q^{2}}
$$

or differentiate a simplified expression for $AC(Q)$. Do not answer from the sign of $C^{\prime}$ alone.

### Method E — Inverse demand and revenue

If the stem gives inverse demand $p=p(Q)$, revenue is still $R(Q)=Q\,p(Q)$. If it gives demand $Q=D(p)$, revenue may be written $R(p)=p\,D(p)$. Choose the variable the claim uses. Elasticity statements should be checked with

$$
El_{Q}(p)=\dfrac{D^{\prime}(p)\cdot p}{D(p)}
$$

or the equivalent $Q$-form when inverse demand is given and you rewrite carefully.

### Method F — Taxes that shift marginal cost

A per-unit tax $t$ added to private marginal cost produces a social or post-tax marginal schedule

$$
MC_{\text{tax}}(Q)=MC(Q)+t
$$

when the tax is constant per unit. A profit-maximizing firm then solves $MR(Q)=MC(Q)+t$, which typically lowers the optimal output relative to the no-tax case. Do not shift $MR$ instead of $MC$ unless the story explicitly taxes revenue that way.

### Method G — Learning curves and inventory (EOQ-style)

Learning models often give average or unit cost falling with cumulative output, for example

$$
AC(Q)=a+bQ^{-c}\quad(c>0).
$$

Differentiate explicitly; do not assume “learning” automatically means $MC=AC$.

Inventory / EOQ-style objectives typically balance an ordering term that falls with lot size against a holding term that rises with lot size, for example

$$
K(Q)=\dfrac{A}{Q}+hQ
$$

(up to positive constants). Then

$$
K^{\prime}(Q)=-\dfrac{A}{Q^{2}}+h,
$$

and $K^{\prime}(Q)=0$ forces $Q=\sqrt{A/h}$ in the simplest case. Confirm it is a minimum with $K^{\prime\prime}>0$ or a sign chart.

### Method H — Utility, risk and concavity

If $U$ is concave ($U^{\prime\prime}<0$), marginal utility is decreasing. Risk or “prefer the average to the gamble” stories lean on Jensen-type comparisons: for a concave $U$ and a mean-preserving spread, expected utility falls. You rarely need the full Jensen theorem statement — you need the sign of $U^{\prime\prime}$ and the correct comparison the claim asserts.

### Method I — Figure plus algebra in one stem

Many hard items show a graph (cubic $P^{\prime}$, curved $MC$ against flat or sloping $MR$, twin $P^{\prime}$ curves, $f$/$f^{\prime}$/$f^{\prime\prime}$, tax-shifted $MC$) **and** give formulas. Use each source for what it uniquely provides:

- read signs, crossings and heights from the figure;
- use algebra when a claim needs an exact critical value or a derivative computation not visible as a labelled height.

Never override a clearly labelled figure height with an algebraic guess, and never ignore a given formula because a sketch “looks” sufficient.

### Method J — Newton quotients in context

When a stem reports

$$
\dfrac{C(Q_{0}+h)-C(Q_{0})}{h}
$$

for a concrete $h$, treat it as an average rate over that step. Claims that identify it with $C^{\prime}(Q_{0})$ are only approximately true. If both the exact derivative and the quotient are available, compare them carefully against any “more than / less than” wording.

## 11.7 Worked multi-claim patterns

The following patterns recur across exam-style tasks. Each shows the reasoning order rather than a single numeric answer key.

### Pattern 1 — Revenue peak is not profit peak

Given $R(Q)=-3Q^{2}+90Q$ and a cubic cost $C(Q)$,

$$
R^{\prime}(Q)=-6Q+90=0\Rightarrow Q=15.
$$

Profit stationary points solve $R^{\prime}(Q)=C^{\prime}(Q)$, which generally yields a different $Q$. A claim that “profit is maximized at $Q=15$ because revenue peaks there” is false unless $C^{\prime}(15)=0$ as well.

### Pattern 2 — Piecewise time, smooth profit

Profit may be smooth and have a clean interior max at $Q=10$, while a piecewise build-time schedule $T(Q)$ has a kink at another threshold. Claims about $T^{\prime}$ at the kink must use one-sided derivatives. Claims about $P$ can ignore the kink in $T$ entirely if $P$ is not defined through $T$.

### Pattern 3 — Same starting level, different marginals

Firm A and firm E start with $R_{A}(8)=R_{E}(8)$. If $R_{E}^{\prime}>R_{A}^{\prime}$ on $(8,12)$, then $R_{E}(12)>R_{A}(12)$. A claim that “they still have equal revenue at $12$” is false. A claim that “E’s revenue advantage at $12$ equals the integral of $R_{E}^{\prime}-R_{A}^{\prime}$ from $8$ to $12$” is the right idea.

### Pattern 4 — $MC$ crosses $AC$ while $MR$ is elsewhere

On a figure, $MC$ may cut $AC$ at its minimum while $MR$ crosses $MC$ at a larger output. Then:

- the $AC$-minimizing output is **not** the profit-maximizing output;
- expand-or-not between those two outputs depends on whether $MR$ still lies above $MC$.

### Pattern 5 — Elasticity slogan without checking $R^{\prime}$

A stem gives $D(p)$ and claims “revenue is maximized where $|El_{Q}|=1$.” Compute $El_{Q}$ and $R^{\prime}$; confirm the point lies in the domain; confirm it is a maximum (for linear demand it is). If the elasticity equals $-1$ only outside the feasible price range, the slogan does not produce an interior revenue max on that domain.

### Pattern 6 — Tax shifts the crossing

Without tax, $MR=MC$ at $Q_{1}$. With a per-unit tax $t$, solve $MR=MC+t$ to get $Q_{2}<Q_{1}$ under the usual downward $MR$ and upward $MC$. A claim that the tax raises the profit-maximizing quantity is false in that standard geometry.

## 11.8 Common errors

### Error 1: differentiating the wrong function

The stem asks about marginal revenue, but the student differentiates price. Or it asks about average cost, but the student differentiates total cost and stops. Always form the object named by the claim.

### Error 2: confusing levels with rates

Knowing $C(10)=400$ does not determine $C^{\prime}(10)$. Knowing $P^{\prime}(10)=0$ does not mean profit is zero. Knowing $R^{\prime}>0$ means revenue is locally increasing, not that revenue is large.

### Error 3: treating $R^{\prime}=0$ as the profit rule

Profit candidates solve $R^{\prime}=C^{\prime}$, not $R^{\prime}=0$. Revenue maximization and profit maximization coincide only in special cases.

### Error 4: product-rule and quotient-rule sign mistakes

For products, both terms are added. For quotients, the second term is subtracted. Writing $\dfrac{f^{\prime}g+fg^{\prime}}{g^{2}}$ for a quotient is wrong.

### Error 5: chain rule with missing inner derivative

Differentiating $(3Q^{2}+1)^{5}$ as $5(3Q^{2}+1)^{4}$ forgets the factor $6Q$. The outer power rule is only the first step.

### Error 6: calling every critical point a maximum

$f^{\prime}(c)=0$ is necessary for an interior extremum of a differentiable $f$, not sufficient. Use a sign chart or $f^{\prime\prime}$. And $f^{\prime\prime}(c)=0$ is inconclusive, not an automatic “neither.”

### Error 7: ignoring endpoints and kinks

On a closed interval, the global extremum may sit at an endpoint. At a piecewise switch, include the switch point and compare one-sided slopes. Continuity of the level does not give differentiability.

### Error 8: reading a graph of $f^{\prime}$ as if it were $f$

A peak on the $f^{\prime}$ graph is where $f$ is steepest, not where $f$ peaks. Zeros of $f^{\prime}$ (with sign change) locate extrema of $f$.

### Error 9: average-cost logic from the wrong sign

$AC$ falls when $AC^{\prime}<0$. That can happen even if $C^{\prime}>0$. Marginal cost being positive does not mean average cost is rising.

### Error 10: rival levels without integrating the gap

Equal values at $a$ plus a strict marginal advantage on $(a,b)$ force a strict level advantage at $b$. Leaving the levels equal at $b$ contradicts the fundamental theorem of calculus.

### Error 11: elasticity memorised without domain checks

$El_{Q}=-1$ identifies a stationary point of $R(p)$ only under the usual regularity and only when that price is feasible. Always reconnect to $R^{\prime}$.

### Error 12: slash division and broken prime notation in written work

In this course’s written answers, prefer $\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q}$ over $C(Q)/Q$, and write $C^{\prime}$, $P^{\prime\prime}$ with proper primes. Sloppy notation often tracks sloppy object choice.

| Question | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| What is $f^{\prime}(a)$? | Instantaneous rate of change; slope of the tangent at $x=a$. |
| Tangent line at $x=a$ | $y=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)(x-a)$. |
| Power / product / quotient / chain | Apply the matching rule; rewrite roots as powers first. |
| Marginal cost / revenue / profit | $C^{\prime}$, $R^{\prime}$, $P^{\prime}=R^{\prime}-C^{\prime}$. |
| Expand output a little? | Expand if $R^{\prime}>C^{\prime}$; contract if $R^{\prime}<C^{\prime}$. |
| MR versus price | $R^{\prime}=p+Qp^{\prime}$; usually $R^{\prime}<p$ when $p^{\prime}<0$. |
| Average cost | $A=\dfrac{C}{Q}$; differentiate with the quotient rule. |
| Interior min of $A$ | $A^{\prime}=0$ forces $C^{\prime}=A$ (when $Q\neq 0$). |
| Interior extremum candidate | $f^{\prime}=0$ (plus kinks / endpoints on a closed interval). |
| Local max / min test | Sign chart of $f^{\prime}$, or $f^{\prime\prime}<0$ / $f^{\prime\prime}>0$. |
| Global on $[a,b]$ | Compare values at all candidates. |
| Constraint story | Solve constraint → one-variable objective → optimize. |
| Elasticity | $El_{Q}(p)=\dfrac{D^{\prime}(p)\,p}{D(p)}$; $R^{\prime}=0$ when $El_{Q}=-1$ (regular case). |
| Graph of $f^{\prime}$ | Sign → mono of $f$; zero+sign change → extremum of $f$; slope of $f^{\prime}$ → concavity of $f$. |
| Piecewise rule | Differentiate per piece; check one-sided derivatives at switches. |
| Rival after equal start | Integrate / compare marginals on the interval to rank later levels. |
| Tax per unit | Replace $MC$ by $MC+t$ in the $MR=MC$ condition. |

Central formulas:

$$
f^{\prime}(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},
$$

$$
\bigl(fg\bigr)^{\prime}=f^{\prime}g+fg^{\prime},
\qquad
\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)^{\prime}=\dfrac{f^{\prime}g-fg^{\prime}}{g^{2}},
\qquad
\bigl(f\circ g\bigr)^{\prime}=f^{\prime}(g)\,g^{\prime},
$$

$$
R(Q)=Q\,p(Q),\qquad R^{\prime}(Q)=p(Q)+Q\,p^{\prime}(Q),
$$

$$
P=R-C,\qquad P^{\prime}=R^{\prime}-C^{\prime},
$$

$$
A(Q)=\dfrac{C(Q)}{Q},\qquad A^{\prime}(Q)=\dfrac{C^{\prime}(Q)\,Q-C(Q)}{Q^{2}},
$$

$$
El_{Q}(p)=\dfrac{D^{\prime}(p)\cdot p}{D(p)}.
$$

**Working order.** Name the function the claim is about. Differentiate that function. Interpret the derivative in context, or solve $f^{\prime}=0$ and classify. On graphs, read the legend first. On piecewise stems, work piece by piece and treat switch points as separate candidates. On rival stems, separate “same level now” from “larger marginal from now on.” Keep extra precision until any strict inequality is settled.

**Self-check.** Can you explain why $R^{\prime}$ can be smaller than price? Why is $P^{\prime}=0$ not the same as $P=0$? How do you turn a fence-length constraint into a one-variable area function? What does a sign change of $f^{\prime}$ from $+$ to $-$ tell you? Why is a peak of the graph of $f^{\prime}$ not a peak of $f$? How do you test whether average cost is falling? If two firms have equal revenue at $Q=8$ and firm E has higher marginal revenue on $(8,12)$, who has higher revenue at $12$? Why does continuity of a piecewise cost function fail to imply continuity of marginal cost? And why does maximizing revenue usually fail to maximize profit?
