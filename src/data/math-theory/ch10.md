# Chapter 10 — Exponential and logarithmic functions

Exponential and logarithmic functions describe growth and decay that multiply rather than add. A capital stock, a population, a radioactive sample, and a temperature gap can all follow the same mathematics. The logarithm is the tool that turns multiplication back into addition, recovers a growth force from data, and reads a semi-log graph.

This chapter follows the live map: exponential functions (10.1), logarithmic functions (10.2), and exam-style mixes (10.3). It starts from the definitions and builds every move the practice bank uses: continuous versus discrete models, doubling and half-life, recovering force from tables, piecewise switches, domains of logs, change of base, and log-log elasticity. The closing examples are written in the same True/False style as the course tasks.

## Learning objectives

- Recognise $f(x)=a\cdot b^{x}$ and the continuous model $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$.
- Tell continuous force $k$ from a discrete annual factor $1+r$, and know that $e^{k}\neq 1+k$ in general.
- Compute doubling times and half-lives, and rebuild $k$ from them.
- Recover a constant force from two observations or from equal log-increments in a table.
- Read a semi-log graph: $\ln P(t)$ is a straight line with slope $k$.
- Handle piecewise continuous force, continuity at a switch, and the true path-average force.
- Find when two exponential paths meet, and apply GDP/per-capita, cooling, and decay models.
- Use the definition of $\log_{b}$, the inverse identities, and the product / quotient / power laws with domain checks.
- Change base, rebuild a base from graph points, and untangle nested or rational log domains.
- Read constant elasticity from a log-log demand and judge ratio claims on letter scales.
- Work mixed exam packs that combine force recovery, hitting clocks, and log constraints.

## 10.1 Exponential functions

### What an exponential function is

An **exponential function** has the variable in the exponent. The basic form is

$$
f(x)=a\cdot b^{x},
$$

where $a\neq 0$ is a vertical scale, and the base satisfies $b>0$ and $b\neq 1$.

| Piece | Role |
| --- | --- |
| $a$ | Starting level when $x=0$, because $b^{0}=1$, so $f(0)=a$. |
| $b$ | One-step multiplier: $f(x+1)=b\cdot f(x)$. |
| $x$ | The exponent (often time). |

This is not a power function. In $x^{3}$ the variable is the base. In $2^{x}$ the variable is the exponent. Mixing the two names is a common exam trap.

**Example 1.** $f(t)=80\cdot (1.05)^{t}$ starts at $80$ and multiplies by $1.05$ every unit of time. After one step the level is $84$. After two steps it is $88.2$, not $80+2\cdot 4$.

### Growth and decay

Assume $a>0$. Then:

| Base | Behaviour | Name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $b>1$ | Strictly increasing | exponential growth |
| $0<b<1$ | Strictly decreasing | exponential decay |

The range of $f$ is $(0,\infty)$. An exponential with positive scale never hits zero and never becomes negative. There is no real $x$ with $b^{x}\le 0$.

If $a<0$, the graph is reflected through the horizontal axis and the range becomes $(-\infty,0)$. Most applied models in this chapter keep $a=P_0>0$.

**Example 2.** $g(t)=50\cdot (1/2)^{t}$ halves every unit of time. It is decay with base $1/2$. The values stay positive for every real $t$.

### Continuous force: $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$

In finance and growth models the course almost always uses the continuous form

$$
P(t)=P_0 e^{kt},
$$

with $P_0>0$. The letter $k$ is the **continuous force** (also called the continuous growth rate). The number $e\approx 2.71828\ldots$ is the base of the natural exponential.

Unpack the formula:

- At $t=0$, $e^{0}=1$, so $P(0)=P_0$.
- Over any interval of length $1$, the level is multiplied by $e^{k}$.
- Over an interval of length $h$, the multiplier is $e^{kh}$.

So

$$
\frac{P(t+h)}{P(t)}=e^{kh}
$$

for every $t$. The relative growth depends only on the length of the interval, not on the calendar date.

Taking natural logs,

$$
\ln P(t)=\ln P_0+kt.
$$

On a plot of $\ln P$ against $t$, a pure continuous exponential becomes a straight line with slope $k$ and intercept $\ln P_0$.

**Sign of $k$.**

| Force | Path |
| --- | --- |
| $k>0$ | Growth: $P$ strictly increasing |
| $k=0$ | Constant path $P(t)=P_0$ |
| $k<0$ | Decay: $P$ strictly decreasing |

**Example 3.** Capital starts at $P_0=1000$ with force $k=0.05$. After $10$ years,

$$
P(10)=1000\,e^{0.5}\approx 1648.72.
$$

The one-year multiplier is $e^{0.05}\approx 1.05127$, not $1.05$.

### Continuous force versus the slogan “$1+k$”

A frequent False claim is that the continuous one-year multiplier equals $1+k$. That would replace $e^{kt}$ by $(1+k)^{t}$. Those are different functions.

For $k>0$,

$$
e^{k}>1+k,
$$

so $P_0 e^{kt}$ grows strictly faster than $P_0(1+k)^{t}$. Equality holds only at $k=0$.

| Object | Formula | One-year factor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Continuous force $k$ | $P_0 e^{kt}$ | $e^{k}$ |
| Naive linear rate $k$ | $P_0(1+k)^{t}$ | $1+k$ |
| Discrete annual rate $r$ | $P_0(1+r)^{t}$ | $1+r$ |

Matching a discrete model to a continuous one means choosing $r$ so that

$$
1+r=e^{k}\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad r=e^{k}-1,
$$

not $r=k$.

**Example 4.** Take $k=0.05$. Then $e^{0.05}-1\approx 0.05127$. A discrete annual rate of $5.127\%$ matches the continuous force $5\%$ over every integer year. A discrete rate of exactly $5\%$ does not.

### Discrete compounding beside a continuous path

A common stem places two rivals side by side:

$$
P(t)=P_0 e^{kt},\qquad Q(t)=P_0(1+r)^{t},
$$

with the same start $P_0$ but $1+r\neq e^{k}$. Then:

- $P(0)=Q(0)=P_0$;
- for every $t>0$ the paths disagree;
- if someone sets $r=k>0$, then $Q(t)<P(t)$ for every $t>0$, because $1+k<e^{k}$.

Rewriting a geometric discrete path in continuous language is always possible:

$$
(1+r)^{t}=e^{t\ln(1+r)}.
$$

The continuous force hidden inside the discrete factor $1+r$ is $\ln(1+r)$, not $r$.

Likewise, a pure exponential $a^{t}$ with $a>0$ rewrites as

$$
a^{t}=e^{t\ln a},
$$

so its continuous force is $\ln a$.

**Example 5.** The discrete path $Q(t)=P_0\cdot 2^{t}$ has force $\ln 2$. Doubling every year is continuous force $\ln 2$, not force $2$ and not force $1$.

### Doubling time and half-life

Start from $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$ with $k\neq 0$.

**Doubling time** (for $k>0$) is the time $T$ such that $P(T)=2P_0$:

$$
e^{kT}=2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T=\frac{\ln 2}{k}.
$$

**Half-life** (for $k<0$, or for decay written $P(t)=P_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ with $\lambda>0$) is the time $H$ such that $P(H)=\frac12 P_0$:

$$
e^{kH}=\frac12\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad H=\frac{\ln(1/2)}{k}=\frac{\ln 2}{|k|}.
$$

For the decay form $P_0 e^{-\lambda t}$,

$$
H=\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.
$$

Both clocks are independent of $P_0$. Doubling multiplies the level by $2$; it does not add $P_0$. After two doubling periods the level is $4P_0$, never $3P_0$.

If a stem gives the doubling time $T$ and asks for the force,

$$
k=\frac{\ln 2}{T}.
$$

If it gives a half-life $H$ for decay force $\lambda$,

$$
\lambda=\frac{\ln 2}{H}.
$$

**Example 6.** A fund doubles every $14$ years under continuous force. Then

$$
k=\frac{\ln 2}{14}.
$$

The path is $P(t)=P_0 e^{(\ln 2)t/14}$. At $t=28$ the fund has quadrupled.

### Recovering force from two observations

Suppose a continuous-exponential path is known to be of the form $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$, and two positive levels are observed at times $t_1<t_2$:

$$
k=\frac{\ln P(t_2)-\ln P(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}=\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\ln\frac{P(t_2)}{P(t_1)}.
$$

This is a log chord. It is not the ordinary slope of the levels,

$$
\frac{P(t_2)-P(t_1)}{t_2-t_1},
$$

which is not a force.

Once $k$ is known, anchor at one observation:

$$
P(t)=P(t_1)\,e^{k(t-t_1)}.
$$

Watch the time shift. The false rewrite $P(t)=P(t_1)e^{k(t+t_1)}$ flips the sign in the exponent and fails at $t=t_1$.

**Table test for constant force.** If observations are equally spaced in time by step $h$, a pure continuous exponential has constant ratios $P(t_{i+1})/P(t_i)$, and therefore constant log-increments

$$
\ln P(t_{i+1})-\ln P(t_i)=kh.
$$

If those log-increments are not constant, the table is not a pure continuous exponential with one force.

**Example 7.** Levels at $t=0,2,4$ are $100$, $120$, $144$. The ratios are $1.2$ and $1.2$, so the log-increments agree. The force on a step of length $2$ is $\ln 1.2$, hence

$$
k=\frac{\ln 1.2}{2}.
$$

If the third level had been $150$ instead of $144$, the second ratio would break, and no single $k$ would fit all three points.

### Semi-log graphs

A **semi-log** plot shows $t$ on the horizontal axis and $\ln P(t)$ (or $\log_{10} P(t)$) on the vertical axis.

For $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$,

$$
\ln P(t)=\ln P_0+kt
$$

is a straight line. The slope of that line is exactly the continuous force $k$. A negative slope means decay.

Exam claims that misread a semi-log graph usually do one of these:

- treat the slope of $\ln P$ as if it were the slope of $P$;
- claim the path is linear in the original level;
- confuse $\log_{10}$ slope with natural-force $k$ without the change-of-base factor $\ln 10$.

If the vertical axis is $\log_{10} P$, then

$$
\log_{10} P(t)=\log_{10} P_0+\frac{k}{\ln 10}\,t,
$$

so the drawn slope is $k/\ln 10$, not $k$.

**Example 8.** A semi-log plot of $\ln P$ against $t$ rises by $0.4$ when $t$ increases by $5$. The force is

$$
k=\frac{0.4}{5}=0.08.
$$

### Piecewise continuous force

Many hard tasks switch the force at a letter time $T$:

$$
P(t)=
\begin{cases}
P_0 e^{k_1 t}, & 0\le t\le T,\\[4pt]
P_0 e^{k_1 T}\,e^{k_2(t-T)}, & t>T.
\end{cases}
$$

At the switch,

$$
P(T)=P_0 e^{k_1 T}
$$

from both sides, so the path is continuous even when $k_1\neq k_2$. Continuity of the level is not the same as constancy of the force. The derivative (instantaneous growth) jumps when the force jumps, but the graph of $P$ itself does not jump.

For $t>T$,

$$
\ln P(t)=\ln P_0+k_1 T+k_2(t-T).
$$

The early segment still has average force $k_1$. Replacing the whole path by a single force $k_1$ for all $t\ge 0$ changes every value after $T$ whenever $k_2\neq k_1$.

### Path-average force

Over an interval $[0,t]$ with $t>0$, the **path-average force** is

$$
\bar k=\frac{1}{t}\ln\frac{P(t)}{P_0}.
$$

By construction,

$$
P(t)=P_0 e^{\bar k\, t}.
$$

So $\bar k$ always reproduces the endpoint. It is not the arithmetic mean of the piece forces.

For the two-piece path above with horizon $t>T$,

$$
\ln\frac{P(t)}{P_0}=k_1 T+k_2(t-T),
\qquad
\bar k=\frac{k_1 T+k_2(t-T)}{t}.
$$

The naive average $(k_1+k_2)/2$ is wrong unless the two pieces happen to have equal length and you are averaging something else that is not this weighted log-average.

**Example 9.** $P_0=900$, force $0.07$ until $t=2$, then force $0.03$ until $t=8$.

$$
\ln\frac{P(8)}{900}=0.07\cdot 2+0.03\cdot 6=0.32,
\qquad
\bar k=\frac{0.32}{8}=0.04.
$$

The arithmetic mean $(0.07+0.03)/2=0.05$ is larger than the true path-average force.

### Hitting a target and crossing times

**Hitting clock.** For a pure path $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$ with $k\neq 0$ and target $M>0$,

$$
P(t_{\mathrm{hit}})=M\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_{\mathrm{hit}}=\frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{M}{P_0},
$$

provided the sign of $k$ makes the target reachable (growth toward a higher target, or decay toward a lower positive target).

If the force switches before the target is reached, you must solve on the correct piece. Ignoring the switch and writing $\ln(M/P_0)/k_1$ is False whenever the hit lies after the kink.

**Crossing of two paths.** Let

$$
A(t)=A_0 e^{k_A t},\qquad B(t)=B_0 e^{k_B t}
$$

with $A_0,B_0>0$ and $k_A\neq k_B$. Setting $A(t)=B(t)$ and taking logs gives

$$
t^{*}=\frac{\ln(B_0/A_0)}{k_A-k_B}=\frac{\ln(A_0/B_0)}{k_B-k_A}.
$$

The form $\ln(A_0/B_0)/(k_A-k_B)$ has the wrong sign pairing and is a standard False claim. After the meeting time, the path with the larger force leads forever.

**Example 10.** $A_0=1000$, $k_A=0.03$, $B_0=1500$, $k_B=0.01$.

$$
t^{*}=\frac{\ln(1500/1000)}{0.03-0.01}=\frac{\ln 1.5}{0.02}\approx 20.27.
$$

Population $A$ starts lower but has the larger force, so it overtakes $B$ and stays ahead.

### Applied models that reuse the same algebra

**GDP per capita.** If aggregate output and population are

$$
Y(t)=Y_0 e^{gt},\qquad N(t)=N_0 e^{pt},
$$

then per capita output is

$$
y(t)=\frac{Y(t)}{N(t)}=y_0 e^{(g-p)t},\qquad y_0=\frac{Y_0}{N_0}.
$$

The continuous force on $y$ is $g-p$, not $g+p$. The path $y$ is increasing precisely when $g>p$. Both $g>0$ and $p>0$ are not enough.

**Newton cooling.** A temperature gap decaying toward ambient $T_a$ often has the form

$$
T(t)-T_a=\bigl(T(0)-T_a\bigr)e^{-kt}
$$

with $k>0$. The force acts on the gap, not on the raw temperature reading. The ambient level is a horizontal asymptote.

**Radioactive decay.** With decay constant $\lambda>0$,

$$
P(t)=P_0 e^{-\lambda t},\qquad H=\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.
$$

Claims that treat $\lambda$ as a discrete annual percentage, or that add $\lambda$ instead of multiplying by $e^{-\lambda t}$, are False.

**Units.** If a stem measures a stock in millions, the force recovered from ratios is unchanged, because constants cancel in $P(t_2)/P(t_1)$. Mixing a raw count with a “millions” series without converting is a unit trap, not a force trap.

### Nested exponential–log compositions

Because $\ln$ and $\exp$ undo each other on the right domains,

$$
e^{\ln u}=u\quad(u>0),\qquad \ln(e^{v})=v.
$$

Useful rewrites in letter form:

$$
e^{k\ln(P/Q)}=\left(\frac{P}{Q}\right)^{k}\qquad(P/Q>0),
$$

$$
\ln\bigl(P_0 e^{kt}\bigr)=\ln P_0+kt.
$$

These identities are algebraic. They do not by themselves say anything about whether a discrete rival matches the continuous path.

## 10.2 Logarithmic functions

### Definition

The logarithm is the inverse of the exponential. For $b>0$, $b\neq 1$, and $x>0$,

$$
y=\log_{b} x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^{y}=x.
$$

Read $\log_{b} x$ as “the exponent you put on $b$ to obtain $x$”.

| Special case | Notation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural log | $\ln x=\log_{e} x$ | exponent on $e$ |
| Common log | $\log_{10} x$ (often written $\log x$ in decimal contexts) | exponent on $10$ |

Immediate values:

$$
\log_{b} 1=0,\qquad \log_{b} b=1,\qquad \log_{b}(b^{r})=r.
$$

**Example 1.** $\log_{2} 8=3$ because $2^{3}=8$. $\log_{2}(1/2)=-1$ because $2^{-1}=1/2$. $\ln e^{5}=5$.

### Domain and range

For a fixed base $b>0$, $b\neq 1$:

- the **domain** of $\log_{b}$ is $(0,\infty)$;
- the **range** is all real numbers.

Zero and negative arguments are never allowed. A claim that “zero arguments are always allowed in log domains” is False.

If the argument is an expression $g(x)$, the domain condition is $g(x)>0$, together with any further restrictions coming from $g$ itself (denominators, roots, and so on).

### Graphs, intercepts, and asymptotes

The graph of $y=\log_{b} x$ for $b>1$:

- meets the horizontal axis at $x=1$, because $\log_{b} 1=0$;
- passes through $(b,1)$;
- is strictly increasing;
- has a vertical asymptote $x=0$ (approached from the right).

For $0<b<1$ the graph is strictly decreasing, still with vertical asymptote $x=0$ and still with $\log_{b} 1=0$.

A horizontal shift $\log_{b}(x-c)$ moves the asymptote to $x=c$. A claim that rebuilds a shift from an asymptote is asking you to read that vertical line.

Comparing two bases $b_1,b_2>1$: both graphs share the asymptote $x=0$ and the intercept $(1,0)$, but they pass through different unit points $(b_i,1)$. On $(1,\infty)$ the larger base grows more slowly in the log scale: $\log_{b} x$ decreases as $b$ increases, for fixed $x>1$.

**Example 2.** For $f(x)=\log_{2} x$:

- $f(1)=0$;
- $f(2)=1$;
- $f(1/2)=-1$;
- $f$ is strictly increasing, not decreasing.

### Inverse relationship with exponentials

The maps $t\mapsto b^{t}$ and $y\mapsto\log_{b} y$ undo each other:

$$
\log_{b}(b^{t})=t\qquad\text{for every real }t,
$$

$$
b^{\log_{b} x}=x\qquad\text{for every }x>0.
$$

Domain matching matters in compositions. The expression $\log_{b}(b^{t})$ is defined for every real $t$. The expression $b^{\log_{b} x}$ requires $x>0$. A nested claim such as $\log_{b}(\log_{b} x)$ needs $\log_{b} x>0$, which is a stricter condition than $x>0$.

**Example 3.** For base $2$, $\log_{2}(\log_{2} x)$ needs $\log_{2} x>0$, hence $x>2^{0}=1$. The domain is $(1,\infty)$, not $(0,\infty)$.

### Logarithm laws (with domain)

For $x>0$, $y>0$, and real $r$,

$$
\log_{b}(xy)=\log_{b} x+\log_{b} y,
$$

$$
\log_{b}\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)=\log_{b} x-\log_{b} y,
$$

$$
\log_{b}(x^{r})=r\log_{b} x.
$$

Each law needs its hypotheses.

| Slogan | Status |
| --- | --- |
| $\log(xy)=\log x+\log y$ with $x>0$, $y>0$ | True |
| Same identity when $x<0$ and $y<0$ | False: each separate log is undefined, even if $xy>0$ |
| $\log(x+y)=\log x+\log y$ | False in general |
| $\log(x^{2})=2\log x$ for all $x\neq 0$ | False: right side needs $x>0$; left side needs $x\neq 0$. Prefer $\log(x^{2})=2\log|x|$ when that is the intended identity |

**Product versus sum of logs.** Compare

$$
p(x)=\log(b-x)+\log(x-a),\qquad q(x)=\log\bigl((b-x)(x-a)\bigr)
$$

with $a<b$ and base $>1$. Both require a positive-argument structure that forces the open interval $(a,b)$. On that interval, $p=q$. Outside it, $p$ cannot be defined by splitting into two logs even if the product happens to be positive in some other configuration of signs. In the two-linear-factor case with $a<b$, the product is positive precisely on $(a,b)$ anyway, so the natural domains agree. The exam point is still: the sum-of-logs form demands each factor positive separately.

**Example 4.** On $(3,\infty)$,

$$
\log\frac{2x+1}{x-3}=\log(2x+1)-\log(x-3),
$$

because both $2x+1$ and $x-3$ are positive there. On $(-\infty,-1/2)$ the quotient is positive, but $x-3<0$, so you must not split with the ordinary quotient law written for two positive factors.

### Change of base

For any valid bases $b$ and $c$, and for $x>0$,

$$
\log_{b} x=\frac{\log_{c} x}{\log_{c} b}=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}=\frac{\log_{10} x}{\log_{10} b}.
$$

This turns every log into a ratio of natural logs (or common logs). It is an identity, not an approximation.

Consequences used constantly:

$$
\log_{b} a=\frac{1}{\log_{a} b}\qquad(a>0,\,a\neq 1),
$$

$$
\ln x=\log_{b} x\cdot \ln b.
$$

**Example 5.** $\log_{2} 10=\ln 10/\ln 2$. A verification table that checks $\log_{b} x\cdot\log_{x} b=1$ for several pairs is simply testing this reciprocal relation.

### Rebuilding a base from graph points

If a log graph passes through $(u,v)$ with $u>0$ and the base is unknown,

$$
\log_{b} u=v\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^{v}=u\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b=u^{1/v}
$$

when $v\neq 0$. The intercept condition $\log_{b} 1=0$ is automatic for every base and does not identify $b$. A unit point $(b,1)$ does identify the base directly.

If the graph passes through $(e,1)$, then $\log_{b} e=1$, so $b=e$, i.e. the function is $\ln$.

**Example 6.** A graph of $\log_{b}$ passes through $(8,3)$. Then $b^{3}=8$, so $b=2$.

### Tangled domains

Hard tasks stack restrictions. Work them in order.

1. **Inner expressions.** Denominators $\neq 0$, roots defined, absolute values fine, and so on.
2. **Log argument.** The full argument of each log must be $>0$.
3. **Nested logs.** Each intermediate log that becomes an argument of an outer log must itself be positive.
4. **Parameters.** Solve inequalities in $x$ carefully; a parameter may flip inequality directions.

**Rational argument.** For

$$
m(x)=\log\left(\frac{2x+1}{x-3}\right)
$$

with base $>1$, require $(2x+1)/(x-3)>0$. Critical points $x=-1/2$ and $x=3$ produce the sign chart

$$
(-\infty,-1/2)\cup(3,\infty).
$$

The pole $x=3$ is excluded. A point such as $x=0$ makes the argument negative, so it is out. A point such as $x=-1$ lies in $(-\infty,-1/2)$ and is in.

**Absolute values.** $\log|h(x)|$ needs $h(x)\neq 0$. The absolute value does not remove other restrictions coming from $h$, but it does allow negative $h$ values.

**Square roots inside logs.** $\log\sqrt{g(x)}$ needs $g(x)>0$ (so the root is positive; at $g=0$ the root is $0$ and the log fails). Equivalently $\frac12\log g(x)$ on that same set.

**Example 7.** Domain of $\log_{3}(x-1)+\log_{3}(x-3)$ requires $x-1>0$ and $x-3>0$, hence $x>3$. Combining into $\log_{3}\bigl((x-1)(x-3)\bigr)=1$ without the separate checks invites extraneous roots. (Equation solving of this type is rehearsed in Chapter 4.4; the domain discipline is the same here.)

### Log-log elasticity and ratio scales

Demand of the constant-elasticity form

$$
Q=A P^{-\beta}\qquad(A>0,\,\beta>0)
$$

becomes linear after logs:

$$
\ln Q=\ln A-\beta\ln P.
$$

**Elasticity** in the course sense is the log-log slope

$$
E=\frac{d\ln Q}{d\ln P}=-\beta.
$$

It is constant at every price $P>0$. If $\beta>1$, then $|E|>1$ and demand is called elastic.

Richter-style or pH-style letter claims compare magnitudes through logs. A difference of one log unit (base $10$) corresponds to a factor $10$ in the underlying positive quantity. Ratio reasoning stays multiplicative:

$$
\log_{b} U-\log_{b} V=\log_{b}\frac{U}{V}.
$$

**Example 8.** If $\log_{10} U-\log_{10} V=2$, then $U/V=100$. The claim “$U$ exceeds $V$ by $2$” as an additive level statement is False.

### Solving exponential and log equations (bridge to Chapter 4)

Chapter 10 focuses on functions, graphs, domains, and applied force. The algebraic equation toolkit still appears inside True/False claims:

- same-base cancellation: $b^{f}=b^{g}\Leftrightarrow f=g$;
- conversion: $\log_{b} A=c\Leftrightarrow A=b^{c}$ with $A>0$;
- substitution $u=b^{x}>0$ for equations that become quadratic in $u$;
- substitution $u=\log_{b} x$ when a quadratic in the log appears.

Always return to the domain after solving. A positive root of an auxiliary quadratic in $u=b^{x}$ is admissible; a nonpositive root is not.

## 10.3 Exam-Style

The following stems match the style of the chapter’s harder tasks. Work every letter. Answers and short reasons follow each stem.

### Example A — Continuous force versus discrete compounding

A stock of capital follows $P(t)=P_0 e^{kt}$ with $P_0>0$ and force $k>0$. A rival discrete model uses the same initial level but annual factor $(1+r)$ with $r>0$, namely $Q(t)=P_0(1+r)^{t}$. Assume throughout that $1+r\neq e^{k}$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** For every $t>0$, the continuous one-year multiplier equals $1+k$.

**B.** The paths $P$ and $Q$ coincide for every $t\ge 0$.

**C.** If $r=k$, then $Q(t)>P(t)$ for every $t>0$.

**D.** The continuous doubling time is $T=\dfrac{\ln 2}{k}$, independent of $P_0$.

**E.** Replacing $e^{kt}$ by $(1+k)^{t}$ leaves every level $P(t)$ unchanged.

**Solutions.**

**A.** False. The continuous one-year multiplier is $e^{k}$, not $1+k$.

**B.** False. The stem keeps $1+r\neq e^{k}$, so the paths diverge for $t>0$ (they only share the start).

**C.** False. For $k>0$ one has $e^{k}>1+k$, so $P(t)>Q(t)$ when $r=k$.

**D.** True. Solve $e^{kT}=2$.

**E.** False. Those are different functions for $k\neq 0$.

### Example B — Piecewise force and a false constant-rate shortcut

A fund starts at $A=1000$, grows at continuous force $\alpha=0.05$ until $t=\tau$ with $\alpha\tau=\ln 2$, then at force $\beta=0.025$. The target level is $M=4A$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** The target is reached strictly after the rate switch.

**B.** The path-average force $\bar k=\ln(M/A)/t_{\mathrm{hit}}$ reproduces $f(t_{\mathrm{hit}})=M$.

**C.** The hitting time equals $\ln(M/A)/\alpha$ (ignoring the switch).

**D.** The late force $\beta$ is strictly larger than the early force $\alpha$.

**E.** At the switch the fund already meets or exceeds the target $M$.

**Solutions.**

At the switch,

$$
f(\tau)=A e^{\alpha\tau}=A e^{\ln 2}=2A.
$$

The target $4A$ still lies above $2A$, so the hit finishes under the late force:

$$
t_{\mathrm{hit}}=\tau+\frac{\ln 2}{\beta}=\frac{\ln 2}{\alpha}+\frac{\ln 2}{\beta}.
$$

**A.** True: $f(\tau)=2A<4A$.

**B.** True by definition of $\bar k$.

**C.** False: $\ln(M/A)/\alpha=\ln 4/\alpha=2\ln 2/\alpha$ ignores the slower second piece and understates the hitting time.

**D.** False: $\beta=0.025<0.05=\alpha$.

**E.** False: the switch level is only $2A$.

### Example C — Crossing populations and the log meeting clock

Two populations follow $A(t)=A_0 e^{k_A t}$ and $B(t)=B_0 e^{k_B t}$ with $A_0=1000$, $k_A=0.03$, $B_0=1500$, $k_B=0.01$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** The populations meet at some strictly positive time before $t=40$.

**B.** At $t=40$, population $A$ exceeds population $B$.

**C.** The continuous force of $A$ exceeds that of $B$.

**D.** The meeting time equals $\ln(A_0/B_0)/(k_A-k_B)$.

**E.** Over $[0,40]$, $\ln(A/A_0)$ equals $\ln(B/B_0)$.

**Solutions.**

$$
t^{*}=\frac{\ln(B_0/A_0)}{k_A-k_B}=\frac{\ln 1.5}{0.02}\approx 20.27\in(0,40).
$$

**A.** True.

**B.** True: $A(40)=1000 e^{1.2}\approx 3320$ and $B(40)=1500 e^{0.4}\approx 2238$.

**C.** True: $0.03>0.01$.

**D.** False: that formula has the wrong numerator. The correct numerator is $\ln(B_0/A_0)$.

**E.** False: $\ln(A/A_0)=k_A t$ and $\ln(B/B_0)=k_B t$ agree for all $t$ in the interval only if $k_A=k_B$.

### Example D — Rational log domain versus the quotient law

Consider

$$
m(x)=\log\left(\frac{2x+1}{x-3}\right)
$$

with base $>1$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** The argument is positive on $(-\infty,-1/2)\cup(3,\infty)$.

**B.** $x=3$ lies in the domain.

**C.** $x=0$ is excluded because the argument is negative.

**D.** $x=-1$ lies in the domain.

**E.** On $(3,\infty)$ one may write $m(x)=\log(2x+1)-\log(x-3)$.

**Solutions.**

**A.** True by the sign chart of $(2x+1)/(x-3)$.

**B.** False: $x=3$ is a pole of the argument.

**C.** True: at $x=0$ the argument equals $-1/3<0$.

**D.** True: $-1\in(-\infty,-1/2)$.

**E.** True: both factors are positive on $(3,\infty)$.

### Example E — GDP per capita force

Aggregate real output and population follow $Y(t)=Y_0 e^{gt}$ and $N(t)=N_0 e^{pt}$ with $Y_0,N_0>0$. Per capita output is $y(t)=Y(t)/N(t)$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** The continuous force on $y$ is exactly $g-p$.

**B.** The continuous force on $y$ is exactly $g+p$.

**C.** $y(t)=y_0 e^{(g+p)t}$ with $y_0=Y_0/N_0$.

**D.** $\dfrac{d}{dt}\ln y(t)=g-p$ for every $t$.

**E.** If $g>0$ and $p>0$, then necessarily $y$ is strictly increasing.

**Solutions.**

$$
y(t)=y_0 e^{(g-p)t}.
$$

**A.** True.

**B.** False: addition of forces would describe a product $YN$, not a ratio.

**C.** False: the exponent must carry $g-p$.

**D.** True: $\ln y(t)=\ln y_0+(g-p)t$.

**E.** False: $y$ increases precisely when $g>p$. If population grows faster than output, per capita output falls.

### Example F — Path-average force versus arithmetic mean

A fund starts at $P_0=900$, grows at force $k_1=0.07$ until $t=2$, then at force $k_2=0.03$ through horizon $t=8$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** $\ln(f(8)/P_0)$ equals $k_1 T+k_2(t-T)$ exactly.

**B.** The path-average force equals $(k_1+k_2)/2=0.05$.

**C.** The late force is strictly larger than the early force.

**D.** At $t=8$ the fund lies strictly below $P_0$.

**E.** Doubling time from $P_0$ equals $\ln 2/k_1$ even after the switch.

**Solutions.**

$$
\ln\frac{f(8)}{900}=0.07\cdot 2+0.03\cdot 6=0.32,
\qquad
\bar k=\frac{0.32}{8}=0.04,
\qquad
f(8)=900 e^{0.32}>900.
$$

**A.** True.

**B.** False: the true average is $0.04$, not $0.05$.

**C.** False: $0.03<0.07$.

**D.** False: the fund is above $P_0$.

**E.** False: after the switch the pure-$k_1$ doubling clock no longer describes the path.

### Example G — Log-log elasticity

Demand is $Q=A P^{-\beta}$ with $A>0$ and $\beta>0$. Elasticity means $E=\dfrac{d\ln Q}{d\ln P}$. Evaluate each statement.

**A.** Taking logs yields $\ln Q=\ln A-\beta\ln P$.

**B.** The elasticity equals $-\beta$ at every price $P>0$.

**C.** With $a=32$, $b=4$ and base $2$, both $\log(a/b)$ and $\log a-\log b$ equal $3$.

**D.** If $\beta>1$, then $|E|>1$ (elastic demand).

**E.** With base $b=7$, both $\log_{b} b$ and $1$ agree.

**Solutions.**

**A.** True.

**B.** True: the log-log slope is the constant $-\beta$.

**C.** True: $\log_{2}(32/4)=\log_{2} 8=3$, and $\log_{2} 32-\log_{2} 4=5-2=3$.

**D.** True.

**E.** True by definition of logarithm.

## Summary reference

| Task | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Identify an exponential | Variable in the exponent: $a\cdot b^{x}$ or $P_0 e^{kt}$. |
| One-year continuous factor | $e^{k}$, not $1+k$. |
| Match discrete $1+r$ to force $k$ | Set $1+r=e^{k}$, i.e. $r=e^{k}-1$. |
| Rewrite $a^{t}$ | $a^{t}=e^{t\ln a}$. |
| Doubling time | $T=(\ln 2)/k$ for $k>0$. |
| Half-life | $H=(\ln 2)/\lambda$ for $P_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. |
| Recover force from two points | $k=\ln\bigl(P(t_2)/P(t_1)\bigr)/(t_2-t_1)$. |
| Semi-log slope | Slope of $\ln P$ against $t$ is $k$. |
| Piecewise path | Match levels at the switch; do not invent a jump in $P$. |
| Path-average force | $\bar k=(1/t)\ln(P(t)/P_0)$; not $(k_1+k_2)/2$. |
| Hitting time (pure path) | $t=\ln(M/P_0)/k$ on the active piece. |
| Crossing time | $t^{*}=\ln(B_0/A_0)/(k_A-k_B)$. |
| Per capita force | $g-p$ for $Y_0 e^{gt}/N_0 e^{pt}$. |
| Definition of log | $y=\log_{b} x\Leftrightarrow b^{y}=x$, $x>0$. |
| Log laws | Product, quotient, power; each factor positive when split. |
| Change of base | $\log_{b} x=\ln x/\ln b$. |
| Inverse identities | $\log_{b}(b^{t})=t$, $b^{\log_{b} x}=x$ ($x>0$). |
| Domain of a log expression | Argument $>0$, plus inner restrictions, plus nested positivity. |
| Log-log elasticity | For $Q=AP^{-\beta}$, $E=-\beta$. |

The central formulas are

$$
P(t)=P_0 e^{kt},
$$

$$
\frac{P(t+h)}{P(t)}=e^{kh},
$$

$$
T_{\mathrm{double}}=\frac{\ln 2}{k}\quad(k>0),
$$

$$
k=\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\ln\frac{P(t_2)}{P(t_1)},
$$

$$
y=\log_{b} x\ \Longleftrightarrow\ b^{y}=x\ (x>0),
$$

$$
\log_{b}(xy)=\log_{b} x+\log_{b} y,
\qquad
\log_{b} x=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b},
$$

$$
\log_{b}(b^{t})=t,
\qquad
b^{\log_{b} x}=x\ (x>0).
$$

**Working order.** Name the model (continuous, discrete, or log). Write the formula and the domain. Translate the claim into an identity about multipliers, forces, or arguments. For piecewise stories, locate the switch before using a hitting clock. For logs, impose argument positivity before applying a law. Keep letter forces symbolic until a comparison needs a sign or an ordering.

Why is a piecewise exponential continuous at the switch even when the force jumps? Why is the path-average force a weighted log-average rather than $(k_1+k_2)/2$? What is the per-capita force when output grows at $g$ and population at $p$? Why does $\log(xy)=\log x+\log y$ fail when $x$ and $y$ are both negative? How do you change base? What extra condition does a nested logarithm impose? And why does constant-elasticity demand become a straight line in log-log coordinates?
