# Chapter 13 — Binomial distribution

Counting successes in a fixed number of yes/no trials is one of the most common exam patterns in WU BBE mathematics. This chapter builds the binomial model from Bernoulli trials through the probability mass function, mean and variance, tails and complements, and the approximations you meet on harder tasks.

## Learning objectives

- Recognise a **Bernoulli trial** and the four conditions of a **binomial setting**.
- Compute $P(X = k)$ with the binomial PMF and interpret $\binom{n}{k}$.
- Use $E[X] = np$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)$ (and the standard deviation) without mixing them up.
- Translate English phrases such as “at least”, “at most”, “exactly”, and “all / none” into the correct sums or single terms.
- Compare two binomial counts (means, variances, and probability ratios) carefully.
- Apply **Poisson** and **normal** approximations when the task asks for them, and know when a sum of binomials is *not* itself binomial.

## 13.1 Bernoulli trials

A **Bernoulli trial** is a single experiment with exactly two outcomes:

- **success**, with probability $p$
- **failure**, with probability $q = 1 - p$

Typical labels: correct/incorrect, germinate/not, convert/not, defect/ok. The name “success” is only a label for the outcome you are counting — it need not be desirable.

If $Y$ is the indicator of success on one trial, then

$$
E[Y] = p, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y) = p(1-p).
$$

Variance $p(1-p)$ is largest when $p = 1/2$ and shrinks toward $0$ as $p$ approaches $0$ or $1$. That single fact explains many true/false traps about which of two people has the more variable score.

## 13.2 The binomial setting

Let $X$ be the **number of successes** in $n$ trials. We write

$$
X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)
$$

when all of the following hold:

1. **Fixed $n$** — the number of trials is set in advance.
2. **Two outcomes** each time — success or failure.
3. **Constant $p$** — the success probability does not change from trial to trial.
4. **Independence** — one trial’s result does not change the others.

If any condition fails (for example, sampling without replacement from a tiny population so that $p$ drifts, or two production lines with different defect rates forced into one “pooled” binomial), the plain $\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ model is the wrong story.

> **Exam habit**  
> Before computing, name $n$, $p$, and what counts as a success. Many false statements quietly change one of these.

## 13.3 Probability mass function

For $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n$,

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k} (1-p)^{n-k}.
$$

Two pieces multiply:

- $\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ counts how many sequences of length $n$ have exactly $k$ successes.
- $p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of any **one** such sequence under independence.

Special cases worth memorising:

| Event | Formula | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All successes | $P(X = n) = p^{n}$ | $\binom{n}{n} = 1$ and $(1-p)^{0} = 1$ |
| All failures | $P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}$ | $\binom{n}{0} = 1$ and $p^{0} = 1$ |
| Exactly one success | $P(X = 1) = n\, p\, (1-p)^{n-1}$ | $n$ positions for the single success |

Raising a probability below $1/2$ to a large power shrinks it extremely fast — perfect-run claims near a round percentage are often designed as close calls.

## 13.4 Mean, variance, and standard deviation

Write $X = Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n$ as a sum of independent Bernoulli indicators with the same $p$. Linearity and independence give

$$
E[X] = np,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p),
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}.
$$

Useful comparisons when two sides share the same $n$:

- Mean difference: $E[B] - E[A] = n(p_B - p_A)$.
- Mean ratio: $\dfrac{E[B]}{E[A]} = \dfrac{p_B}{p_A}$ (the $n$ cancels).
- Which variance is larger? Compare distance of each $p$ from $1/2$ — closer to $1/2$ means larger $p(1-p)$.

> **Trap**  
> A higher success rate does **not** automatically mean higher variance. An expert with $p = 0.90$ is typically *less* variable than a guesser with $p = 0.25$.

## 13.5 Cumulative probabilities and English tails

Exam statements almost never stop at “exactly $k$”. Translate the English carefully:

| Phrase | Event | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| exactly $k$ | $X = k$ | single PMF term |
| at least $k$ | $X \ge k$ | $\sum_{x=k}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| at most $k$ | $X \le k$ | $\sum_{x=0}^{k} P(X = x)$ |
| more than $k$ | $X > k$ | $\sum_{x=k+1}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| fewer than $k$ | $X < k$ | $\sum_{x=0}^{k-1} P(X = x)$ |
| at least one | $X \ge 1$ | $1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - (1-p)^{n}$ |

The outcomes $X = k, k+1, \ldots, n$ are mutually exclusive, so an “at least” probability is literally a **sum** of binomial terms — not one term and not $p$ itself.

Complement shortcuts save time on long tails:

$$
P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1),
$$

$$
P(X \le k) = 1 - P(X \ge k+1).
$$

## 13.6 Comparing two binomial sides

Many BBE-style tasks put two people, machines, or batches side by side with the same $n$ but different $p$ (or occasionally different $n$). Typical claim types:

1. **Perfect run** — check $p^{n}$ against a percentage cutoff; compute precisely near round thresholds.
2. **Tail wording** — verify that “at least $k$” is written as the sum from $k$ to $n$ with the stated $n$ and $p$.
3. **Likelihood ratios** — compute both tails, then divide. Do **not** replace the ratio of probabilities by the ratio of the $p$ values, and do not round $8.24$ up to “about $9$”.
4. **Variance / SD** — use $np(1-p)$ and $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$; remember the $p \approx 1/2$ rule.
5. **Means vs thresholds** — comparing $np$ to a cutoff needs no probability calculation.

> **Ratio discipline**  
> “$B$ is at least $300$ times as likely as $A$ to pass” means $\dfrac{P_B(\text{pass})}{P_A(\text{pass})} \ge 300$. Compute both pass probabilities first.

## 13.7 When a sum is not binomial

If $X_1 \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_1, p_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_2, p_2)$ are independent, then

$$
X_1 + X_2 \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_1 + n_2, p)
$$

**only when** $p_1 = p_2 = p$.

If $p_1 \neq p_2$, the total is a **Poisson-binomial** count (independent Bernoullis with unequal success probabilities). You may still use linearity:

$$
E[X_1 + X_2] = n_1 p_1 + n_2 p_2,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = n_1 p_1(1-p_1) + n_2 p_2(1-p_2).
$$

A “pooled” rate $\bar{p} = \dfrac{n_1 p_1 + n_2 p_2}{n_1 + n_2}$ matches the mean of $\mathrm{Bin}(n_1+n_2, \bar{p})$, but the variances generally differ — equal means do not make the distributions equal.

## 13.8 Poisson approximation

When $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $\lambda = np$ stays moderate,

$$
P(X = k) \approx e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}, \qquad X \approx \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda).
$$

Use this for rare events (defects, mutations, server errors) when a task invokes the Poisson model. Check that $p$ is small and $n$ is large before trusting the approximation on an exam claim.

## 13.9 Normal approximation

For large $n$ with both $np$ and $n(1-p)$ at least about $5$,

$$
X \approx \mathcal{N}\big(np,\, np(1-p)\big).
$$

Because $X$ is discrete and the normal is continuous, tail comparisons usually need a **continuity correction**, for example

$$
P(X \ge k) \approx P\big(Y \ge k - \tfrac{1}{2}\big)
$$

for $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(np, np(1-p))$. Always verify the $np$ and $n(1-p)$ rule of thumb on each side before accepting a normal-approximation claim.

## 13.10 Skewness (shape)

Binomial skewness is

$$
\gamma_1 = \frac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
$$

- If $p < 1/2$, the distribution is skewed right (long right tail).
- If $p > 1/2$, skewed left.
- If $p = 1/2$, skewness is $0$ (symmetric).

As $n$ grows with $p$ fixed away from the extremes, skewness shrinks and the shape becomes more bell-like — consistent with the normal approximation.

## 13.11 Calculator and exam workflow

On TI-style calculators used in BBE practice:

- **binompdf**($n$, $p$, $k$) → $P(X = k)$
- **binomcdf**($n$, $p$, $k$) → $P(X \le k)$

Then build other tails from complements, for example $P(X \ge k) = 1 - \texttt{binomcdf}(n,p,k-1)$.

A reliable attack order for true/false statements:

1. Confirm the binomial setting ($n$, $p$, independence, two outcomes).
2. Translate the English event into $=$, $\le$, or $\ge$.
3. Decide whether you need a single term, a sum, a mean/variance identity, or an approximation.
4. Near a round cutoff (20%, 300×, “more than 4 times”), compute exactly — do not eyeball.

## 13.12 Quick formula sheet

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p), \qquad \mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

$$
P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (1-p)^{n}, \qquad P(X = n) = p^{n}, \qquad P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}
$$

$$
\text{Poisson: } \lambda = np; \qquad \text{Normal: } X \approx \mathcal{N}(np, np(1-p))
$$

When you can state the setting, write the PMF, move fluently between English and sums, and handle means, variances, and approximations without mixing the tools, you are ready for the practice tasks in this chapter.
