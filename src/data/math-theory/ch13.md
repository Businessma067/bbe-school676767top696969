# Chapter 12: Binomial distribution

The binomial distribution is used when the same kind of trial is repeated and we count how many times one chosen outcome occurs. A trial might be one question, one free throw, one inspected product, one customer response, or one computer request. The situations look different, but the mathematics underneath them is the same.

This chapter starts with ordinary probability and builds the model one piece at a time. It then explains exact probabilities, ranges such as “at least” and “at most”, the mean and spread, comparisons between two cases, and linear scoring rules. The later examples combine several ideas because the difficult questions rarely tell you which formula to use.

## Learning objectives

- Understand what a probability, trial, outcome and random variable mean.
- Decide what will count as a success in a given situation.
- Check the four conditions required for a binomial model.
- Read and write the notation $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$.
- Understand where every part of the binomial formula comes from.
- Calculate exact, cumulative and complementary probabilities.
- Translate percentage cutoffs into possible whole-number counts.
- Interpret and compare the mean, variance and standard deviation.
- Compare two distributions using differences, ratios and percentage changes.
- Recognise when two binomial counts can and cannot be combined.
- Convert a scoring rule such as $+5$ and $-2$ into an expected score.

## 12.1 Definition

### Start with one trial

A **trial** is one repetition of an experiment. One free throw is a trial. One inspected item is a trial. One customer being asked a yes-or-no question is a trial.

Each trial has an **outcome**, meaning the result that happened. In a binomial model we sort all possible results into two groups:

- the outcome we want to count, called a **success**;
- every other outcome, called a **failure**.

The words success and failure are only labels. They do not mean good and bad.

Suppose a factory counts defective products. The result being counted is “defective”, so a defective product is called a success in the calculation. If the defect probability is $0.03$, then

$$
p = 0.03
$$

is the probability of success, while

$$
1-p = 1-0.03 = 0.97
$$

is the probability of failure, meaning a product is not defective.

This choice matters. If you silently change success from “defective” to “not defective”, then $p$ changes from $0.03$ to $0.97$, and the random variable starts counting something different.

### From one trial to many trials

Now repeat the same kind of trial $n$ times. The letter $n$ is the fixed number of trials.

We use a random variable $X$ to count the number of successes among those $n$ trials. A **random variable** is a numerical value determined by the random outcomes. Here it is simply a count.

If a player takes $8$ free throws and $X$ counts made shots, then

$$
X \in \{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8\}.
$$

The player cannot make $4.5$ shots, because $X$ is a count. The player also cannot make $9$ shots, because only $8$ were attempted. This is why the binomial distribution is called **discrete**. Its possible values are separate whole numbers.

### The notation

When the required conditions hold, we write

$$
X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p).
$$

This is read as:

> $X$ follows a binomial distribution with $n$ trials and probability $p$ of success on each trial.

The notation contains three different ideas:

| Symbol | Meaning | Example with 8 free throws |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $n$ | Number of trials | $8$ shots |
| $p$ | Success probability on one trial | Probability of making one shot |
| $X$ | Total number of successes | Number of made shots out of 8 |

The number $k$ is used when we ask about one particular value of $X$. For example, $P(X=6)$ means the probability of exactly $6$ successes.

Do not confuse $p$ and $P(X=k)$. The value $p$ describes one trial. The expression $P(X=k)$ describes the result of the whole group of $n$ trials.

**Example 1.** A student guesses every answer on a 10-question multiple-choice test. Each question has four choices and one correct answer.

One question is one trial. “Correct answer” is the success. Random guessing gives

$$
p = \frac14 = 0.25.
$$

There are $10$ questions, so $n=10$. If $X$ counts correct answers, then

$$
X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(10,0.25).
$$

The possible values are $0,1,\ldots,10$.

**Example 2.** A component fails independently in each of 2,500 tests with probability $0.0012$. Let $X$ count failures.

The success in the mathematical model is a failure of the component because that is what $X$ counts. Therefore

$$
n=2500,\qquad p=0.0012,\qquad X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2500,0.0012).
$$

The word “failure” in the story does not stop it from being called a success in the formula.

## 12.2 Required conditions

Seeing repeated yes-or-no outcomes is not enough. Four conditions must hold before one binomial model can be used.

| Condition | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed number of trials | The value of $n$ is known before the experiment begins. |
| Two outcome categories | Every trial is classified as success or failure. |
| Constant probability | The same success probability $p$ applies to every trial. |
| Independence | One result does not change the probabilities for later trials. |

### Condition 1: the number of trials is fixed

You must know in advance how many trials will be performed.

“Inspect 20 products” has fixed $n=20$.

“Keep inspecting products until the first defect is found” does not have a fixed number of trials. The inspection may stop after one item, after ten items, or much later. That situation is not binomial.

The difference is not a small wording detail. A binomial random variable counts successes inside a fixed number of places. If the number of places is itself random, the binomial formula no longer describes the experiment.

### Condition 2: each result belongs to one of two categories

The original situation may have more than two named outcomes, but it can still be binomial if the question groups them into success and failure.

Suppose a customer rates a service as poor, acceptable, good or excellent. If $X$ counts ratings of good or excellent, then:

- success means good or excellent;
- failure means poor or acceptable.

There are now two categories for the purpose of the question.

This condition fails when the problem wants separate counts for several categories at once. One binomial variable cannot simultaneously count poor, acceptable, good and excellent as four separate outcomes.

### Condition 3: the probability stays constant

The same value of $p$ must apply to every trial.

If a player has probability $0.8$ of making every free throw, then the probability is constant. If fatigue causes the probability to fall after each shot, there is no single $p$ that describes all shots.

Do not average different probabilities just to create one value. Suppose five easy questions have success probability $0.9$ and five difficult questions have success probability $0.4$. The average probability is $0.65$, but the ten questions are not ten identical Bernoulli trials with $p=0.65$. A model $\mathrm{Bin}(10,0.65)$ would generally give the wrong variance and wrong probabilities.

### Condition 4: the trials are independent

Independence means that learning one result does not change the probability of another result.

Separate free throws are often modelled as independent when the question says the success probability stays fixed. Drawing cards without replacement from a small deck is different. If an ace is removed, both the number of aces and the total number of cards left have changed. The next probability is no longer the same.

Independence is what allows probabilities to be multiplied. If three specific trials have to succeed, their joint probability is $p^3$ only when those successes are independent.

### A full check

**Example 1.** An inspector classifies 20 items. Each classification is correct independently with probability $0.75$. Let $X$ be the number classified correctly.

1. There are exactly $20$ classifications, so $n$ is fixed.
2. Each result is classified as correct or incorrect.
3. The probability of a correct classification is $0.75$ every time.
4. The word independently states the last condition directly.

All four conditions hold, so

$$
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(20,0.75).
$$

**Example 2.** Five cards are drawn from a standard deck without replacement, and $X$ counts aces.

The number of draws is fixed and each card is either an ace or not an ace. However, the first ace probability is $4/52$. If the first card is an ace, the next ace probability becomes $3/51$. If it is not an ace, the next probability becomes $4/51$.

The probability changes and the draws are dependent. The ordinary binomial model is not exact here.

**Example 3.** A device contains three independent components. In one cycle, the device fails if at least one component fails. The individual failure probabilities are $p_1$, $p_2$ and $p_3$. The device then runs for 2,500 independent cycles under the same conditions.

Before modelling the number of failed cycles, first find the failure probability for one cycle. It is easier to calculate the opposite event.

The device has no failure only if all three components survive:

$$
P(\text{no device failure})
=(1-p_1)(1-p_2)(1-p_3).
$$

Therefore

$$
p=P(\text{device failure})
=1-(1-p_1)(1-p_2)(1-p_3).
$$

If that value stays constant and the cycles are independent, then the number of failed cycles is

$$
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2500,p).
$$

This example has two layers. First find the probability of success on one trial. Only then build the binomial model across many trials.

## 12.3 Probability mass function

### What “exactly $k$” means

Suppose $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$. We want the probability of exactly $k$ successes.

If there are $k$ successes, there must be

$$
n-k
$$

failures. The probability of one particular ordering with $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures is

$$
p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.
$$

That is not yet the whole answer because the successes can occupy different positions.

### Why the binomial coefficient is needed

Take three trials and ask for exactly two successes. The possible orderings are:

1. success, success, failure;
2. success, failure, success;
3. failure, success, success.

Each ordering has probability

$$
p^2(1-p).
$$

The orderings cannot happen together, so their probabilities add:

$$
3p^2(1-p).
$$

The number $3$ is the number of ways to choose two success positions from three positions:

$$
\binom{3}{2}=3.
$$

In general, the number of ways to place $k$ successes among $n$ trials is

$$
\binom{n}{k}
=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

The exclamation mark means factorial:

$$
5!=5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1=120.
$$

We define $0!=1$. This makes the formula work naturally when there are zero successes or zero failures.

### The full formula

Combining the number of orderings with the probability of each ordering gives

$$
P(X=k)
=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k},
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n.
$$

Every factor has a job:

| Factor | Job |
| --- | --- |
| $\binom{n}{k}$ | Counts where the $k$ successes can occur |
| $p^k$ | Gives the probability of the $k$ successes |
| $(1-p)^{n-k}$ | Gives the probability of the remaining failures |

If you forget the coefficient, you calculate only one ordering. If you use $k$ as the failure exponent, you accidentally describe $k$ successes and $k$ failures. Always ask how many trials are left after the $k$ successes. The answer is $n-k$.

### A complete exact calculation

**Example 1.** A student guesses on 10 four-choice questions. Find the probability of exactly 3 correct answers.

First identify the model:

$$
n=10,\qquad p=0.25,\qquad k=3.
$$

Exactly three answers are correct, so seven are incorrect:

$$
n-k=10-3=7.
$$

Insert the values into the formula:

$$
P(X=3)
=\binom{10}{3}(0.25)^3(0.75)^7.
$$

Now evaluate each factor:

$$
\binom{10}{3}
=\frac{10!}{3!7!}
=\frac{10\cdot9\cdot8}{3\cdot2\cdot1}
=120,
$$

$$
(0.25)^3=0.015625,
$$

$$
(0.75)^7\approx0.133484.
$$

Therefore

$$
P(X=3)
\approx120(0.015625)(0.133484)
\approx0.2503.
$$

As a percentage:

$$
0.2503\times100\%\approx25.03\%.
$$

The coefficient $120$ says there are 120 different choices of which three questions are correct. The powers describe the probability attached to each of those choices.

### Useful edge cases

The general formula becomes shorter at the edges of the distribution.

All $n$ trials succeed:

$$
P(X=n)=p^n.
$$

There is only one ordering, so $\binom{n}{n}=1$, and there are no failures, so $(1-p)^0=1$.

No trial succeeds:

$$
P(X=0)=(1-p)^n.
$$

Again there is only one ordering, and $p^0=1$.

Exactly one trial succeeds:

$$
P(X=1)
=\binom{n}{1}p(1-p)^{n-1}
=np(1-p)^{n-1}.
$$

The one success can occur in any of the $n$ positions.

**Example 2.** A player makes each of 10 free throws independently with probability $0.85$. Find the probability of a perfect run.

A perfect run means $X=10$, not $X=1$. Every shot must succeed:

$$
P(X=10)=(0.85)^{10}\approx0.196874.
$$

Converting to a percentage gives about $19.69\%$. Therefore a statement saying “more than $20\%$” is false. The result is close to $20\%$, so rounding too early could change the verdict.

**Example 3.** A component fails independently on each of 2,500 tests with probability $0.0012$. Find the probability of no failures.

Here a failure is the counted success, so $p=0.0012$. Zero counted failures means every test produces the opposite outcome:

$$
P(X=0)
=(1-0.0012)^{2500}
=(0.9988)^{2500}
\approx0.049697.
$$

The result is about $4.97\%$. A very small one-trial probability does not mean the chance of at least one event across thousands of trials is small.

## 12.4 Mean and variance

The full probability formula tells us the chance of each possible count. The mean, variance and standard deviation summarize the overall location and spread.

### Mean

For $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$,

$$
E[X]=np.
$$

The notation $E[X]$ means the expected value or mean of $X$.

To understand the formula, imagine each trial contributes either $1$ success or $0$ successes. One trial contributes an average of $p$. With $n$ trials, the expected total contribution is $np$.

The mean is a long-run average across many repetitions of the whole experiment. It does not have to be a possible value of $X$.

**Example 1.** For 10 guessed questions with $p=0.25$,

$$
E[X]=10(0.25)=2.5.
$$

No single test produces $2.5$ correct answers. The meaning is that over many comparable 10-question tests, the average score approaches $2.5$.

The expected value is not a guarantee. A mean of $2.5$ does not say that $2$ or $3$ must be the most common result, and it certainly does not say every student will score near $2.5$.

### Variance

For a binomial random variable,

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p).
$$

Variance measures spread around the mean. A larger variance means the count changes more from one repetition to another.

The factor $p(1-p)$ explains why high probability does not automatically mean high variance. If $p$ is near $1$, success is almost certain and the count is predictable. If $p$ is near $0$, failure is almost certain and the count is also predictable. The greatest uncertainty occurs near $p=0.5$, where either outcome is equally plausible.

For a fixed $n$:

$$
0.5(1-0.5)=0.25,
$$

$$
0.8(1-0.8)=0.16,
$$

$$
0.95(1-0.95)=0.0475.
$$

The product gets smaller as $p$ moves away from $0.5$ in either direction.

There is also symmetry:

$$
p(1-p)=(1-p)p.
$$

So probabilities $p=0.2$ and $p=0.8$ give the same variance when $n$ is the same.

### Standard deviation

Variance is measured in squared units. The standard deviation returns to the original unit of the count:

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X)
=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}
=\sqrt{np(1-p)}.
$$

If $X$ counts correct answers, the standard deviation is measured in correct answers. It gives a rough scale for how far results tend to sit from the mean.

### Compare two students carefully

**Example 2.** Two students answer 20 questions. Student A has $p_A=0.50$ and Student B has $p_B=0.80$.

The means are

$$
E[A]=20(0.50)=10,
$$

$$
E[B]=20(0.80)=16.
$$

Student B has the higher expected score.

Now calculate the variances:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(A)
=20(0.50)(0.50)
=5,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(B)
=20(0.80)(0.20)
=3.2.
$$

Student A has the larger variance even though A has the lower mean. Student A’s $p=0.5$ is at the point of greatest uncertainty. Student B is more likely to succeed and also more predictable.

The standard deviations are

$$
\mathrm{SD}(A)=\sqrt5\approx2.236,
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(B)=\sqrt{3.2}\approx1.789.
$$

Do not compare a variance directly with a standard deviation. They are different measures. If a statement mentions standard deviation, take the square roots before comparing.

### Different values of $n$

The shortcut “closer to $0.5$ means greater variance” only settles the comparison when $n$ is the same. When the numbers of trials differ, calculate both full expressions.

**Example 3.** Side A has $n_A=18$, $p_A=0.72$. Side B has $n_B=24$, $p_B=0.91$.

Although $0.72$ is closer to $0.5$, Side B has more trials. Calculate:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(A)
=18(0.72)(0.28)
=3.6288,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(B)
=24(0.91)(0.09)
=1.9656.
$$

Side A still has the larger variance, but that conclusion comes from the calculation, not from looking only at the probabilities.

### Equal means do not imply equal distributions

**Example 4.** Compare

$$
X_A\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2500,0.0012)
$$

and

$$
X_B\sim\mathrm{Bin}(6000,0.0005).
$$

Both have mean

$$
np=3.
$$

Their variances are

$$
\mathrm{Var}(A)
=2500(0.0012)(0.9988)
=2.9964,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(B)
=6000(0.0005)(0.9995)
=2.9985.
$$

The means are exactly equal, but the variances are slightly different. Their exact probabilities are also slightly different. Knowing $np$ alone does not determine the entire binomial distribution because the values of $n$ and $p$ still matter separately.

## 12.5 Cumulative probabilities and exam wording

Most difficult errors in this chapter happen before any arithmetic. The words describe a set of possible counts, and you must translate that set correctly.

| Wording | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| exactly $k$ | $X=k$ |
| at least $k$ | $X\ge k$ |
| more than $k$ | $X\ge k+1$ |
| at most $k$ | $X\le k$ |
| fewer than $k$ | $X\le k-1$ |
| between $a$ and $b$, inclusive | $a\le X\le b$ |

### Why ranges become sums

Suppose a student passes with at least $7$ correct answers out of $10$. Passing can happen through four different counts:

$$
X=7,\quad X=8,\quad X=9,\quad X=10.
$$

Only one count can happen on a single test. These outcomes are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add:

$$
P(X\ge7)
=P(X=7)+P(X=8)+P(X=9)+P(X=10).
$$

Using summation notation:

$$
P(X\ge7)
=\sum_{x=7}^{10}
\binom{10}{x}p^x(1-p)^{10-x}.
$$

The letter $x$ inside the sum takes the values $7,8,9,10$ one by one.

Writing only $P(X=7)$ answers “exactly 7”, not “at least 7”. The missing values can hold most of the probability, especially when $p$ is high.

### Complements

The complement of an event is everything outside it. An event and its complement cover all possibilities, so their probabilities add to $1$.

For “at least $k$”:

$$
P(X\ge k)=1-P(X\le k-1).
$$

The complement stops at $k-1$ because the value $k$ belongs to the original event.

For “more than $k$”:

$$
P(X>k)=1-P(X\le k).
$$

For “at least one”, the complement is especially simple:

$$
P(X\ge1)
=1-P(X=0)
=1-(1-p)^n.
$$

This is one of the most useful shortcuts in probability. Adding $P(X=1)$ through $P(X=n)$ works, but calculating one zero-success term is much shorter.

**Example 1.** In five independent trials with $p=0.2$, find the probability of at least one success.

Direct addition would require five terms. Use the complement:

$$
P(X\ge1)
=1-P(X=0)
=1-(0.8)^5
=1-0.32768
=0.67232.
$$

So the probability is about $67.23\%$.

### Percentage cutoffs must become whole counts

A binomial variable cannot take decimal values. If a pass rule is written as a percentage, first convert that percentage into the smallest or largest whole count that satisfies it.

**Example 2.** A batch contains 18 units and passes if at least $90\%$ are acceptable.

Calculate the raw threshold:

$$
0.90(18)=16.2.
$$

The possible counts near this value are $16$ and $17$. Check them:

$$
\frac{16}{18}\approx88.89\%,
$$

$$
\frac{17}{18}\approx94.44\%.
$$

Sixteen does not reach $90\%$. Seventeen does. Therefore

$$
\text{at least }90\%
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X\ge17.
$$

We round upward because the wording is “at least”.

For a batch of 24:

$$
0.90(24)=21.6,
$$

so at least $90\%$ means $X\ge22$.

Do not round to the nearest integer automatically. The direction comes from the inequality:

- “at least” uses the smallest whole number above the raw cutoff;
- “at most” uses the largest whole number below the raw cutoff.

### A complete upper-tail calculation

**Example 3.** A student answers 10 questions independently with probability $0.9$ of being correct. Find the probability of at least 7 correct answers.

First translate the wording:

$$
P(X\ge7)
=P(X=7)+P(X=8)+P(X=9)+P(X=10).
$$

Now calculate each term:

$$
P(X=7)
=\binom{10}{7}(0.9)^7(0.1)^3
\approx0.0574,
$$

$$
P(X=8)
=\binom{10}{8}(0.9)^8(0.1)^2
\approx0.1937,
$$

$$
P(X=9)
=\binom{10}{9}(0.9)^9(0.1)
\approx0.3874,
$$

$$
P(X=10)
=(0.9)^{10}
\approx0.3487.
$$

Add the mutually exclusive outcomes:

$$
P(X\ge7)
\approx0.0574+0.1937+0.3874+0.3487
\approx0.9872.
$$

The pass probability is about $98.72\%$.

Notice how small the term for exactly $7$ is compared with the full result. Using only $P(X=7)$ would give $5.74\%$ instead of $98.72\%$.

### A middle interval

**Example 4.** Let $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(20,0.4)$. Find the probability that the count is between $8$ and $12$, inclusive.

The word inclusive says that both endpoints belong:

$$
P(8\le X\le12)
=\sum_{x=8}^{12}
\binom{20}{x}(0.4)^x(0.6)^{20-x}.
$$

The five terms correspond to $X=8,9,10,11,12$. Starting at $9$ or stopping at $11$ would change the event.

## 12.6 Comparing two distributions

Many questions place two binomial situations side by side. Before calculating, identify exactly what the claim compares.

### Difference

“B exceeds A by $d$” asks for subtraction:

$$
B-A.
$$

For means:

$$
E[B]-E[A]
=n_Bp_B-n_Ap_A.
$$

If both have the same $n$, this becomes

$$
E[B]-E[A]
=n(p_B-p_A).
$$

**Example 1.** Two players each take 10 shots. Player A has $p_A=0.55$ and Player B has $p_B=0.85$.

$$
E[A]=10(0.55)=5.5,
$$

$$
E[B]=10(0.85)=8.5.
$$

The expected difference is

$$
8.5-5.5=3.
$$

The shortcut gives the same result:

$$
10(0.85-0.55)=3.
$$

### Ratio

“B is $r$ times A” asks for division:

$$
\frac{B}{A}.
$$

The order matters. “B compared with A” places B in the numerator. Reversing the order gives the reciprocal.

For means with equal $n$:

$$
\frac{E[B]}{E[A]}
=\frac{np_B}{np_A}
=\frac{p_B}{p_A}.
$$

This shortcut applies to the means, not to arbitrary tail probabilities.

In general,

$$
\frac{P_B(X\ge k)}{P_A(X\ge k)}
\ne
\frac{p_B}{p_A}.
$$

The tail probabilities contain powers, coefficients and several added terms. They can be hundreds or millions of times apart even when $p_B/p_A$ is only two or three.

### Percentage increase

“B is $r\%$ higher than A” does not mean the same thing as “B is $r\%$ of A”.

The percentage increase of B over A is

$$
\frac{B-A}{A}\times100\%.
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\left(\frac BA-1\right)\times100\%.
$$

If $B/A=1.6$, then B is $160\%$ of A but only $60\%$ higher than A. The subtraction of $1$ removes the original $100\%$ represented by A.

**Example 2.** An experienced inspector has expected count $23.25$, while a new inspector has expected count $17.5$.

The increase is

$$
\left(\frac{23.25}{17.5}-1\right)100\%
\approx32.86\%.
$$

A claim of “more than $35\%$ higher” is false.

### Comparing tail probabilities

**Example 3.** Two people take the same 10-question test. Person A guesses with $p_A=0.25$. Person B has $p_B=0.9$. Passing requires at least 7 correct answers.

For A:

$$
P_A(X\ge7)
=\sum_{x=7}^{10}
\binom{10}{x}(0.25)^x(0.75)^{10-x}
\approx0.0035.
$$

For B:

$$
P_B(X\ge7)
=\sum_{x=7}^{10}
\binom{10}{x}(0.9)^x(0.1)^{10-x}
\approx0.9872.
$$

Now form the ratio in the stated order:

$$
\frac{P_B(\text{pass})}{P_A(\text{pass})}
\approx
\frac{0.9872}{0.0035}
\approx282.
$$

Person B is about 282 times as likely to pass. The one-trial probability ratio is only

$$
\frac{0.9}{0.25}=3.6,
$$

which is nowhere near the ratio of pass probabilities.

This is a good example of why the whole event must be calculated before comparing.

### A close threshold

**Example 4.** A player makes each of 10 free throws with probability $0.85$. A statement claims the probability of making all 10 is more than $20\%$.

Calculate without early rounding:

$$
(0.85)^{10}
\approx0.196874.
$$

As a percentage:

$$
0.196874\times100\%
\approx19.6874\%.
$$

The statement is false. Writing $0.196874\approx0.20$ first and then saying “20%” loses the information needed to compare with a strict “more than 20%” boundary.

### Comparing spreads

If a claim compares variances, calculate

$$
\frac{\mathrm{Var}(A)}{\mathrm{Var}(B)}
=
\frac{n_Ap_A(1-p_A)}
{n_Bp_B(1-p_B)}.
$$

If it compares standard deviations, either calculate both square roots or use

$$
\frac{\mathrm{SD}(A)}{\mathrm{SD}(B)}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{\mathrm{Var}(A)}
{\mathrm{Var}(B)}
}.
$$

A variance ratio of $4$ gives a standard-deviation ratio of $2$, not $4$.

**Example 5.** For $n=20$, compare $p_A=0.75$ with $p_B=0.95$.

$$
\mathrm{Var}(A)=20(0.75)(0.25)=3.75,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(B)=20(0.95)(0.05)=0.95.
$$

The variance ratio is

$$
\frac{3.75}{0.95}\approx3.947.
$$

So A’s variance is less than four times B’s variance, even though it is very close. A true-or-false claim saying “more than four times” is false.

## 12.7 Common errors

### Error 1: defining success carelessly

Write one sentence before the model:

> A success means ...

If $X$ counts defective items, use the defect probability. If $X$ counts acceptable items, use the acceptable-item probability. The values are complements, but the events $X=k$ are not interchangeable unless the count is also transformed.

### Error 2: forcing a binomial model when a condition fails

A fixed total, two categories, constant $p$ and independence are requirements, not optional details. Drawing without replacement from a small population, changing difficulty, fatigue, learning and stopping after success can all break the model.

### Error 3: using the one-trial probability as the final answer

The value $p$ is the chance of one success. It is not the chance of exactly $k$ successes, passing a threshold or getting at least one success.

For example:

$$
p=0.2
$$

does not imply

$$
P(X\ge1)=0.2.
$$

With five trials:

$$
P(X\ge1)=1-(0.8)^5=0.67232.
$$

### Error 4: forgetting the binomial coefficient

The expression

$$
p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

describes one particular ordering. Unless $k=0$ or $k=n$, there are usually many orderings. Multiply by $\binom nk$.

### Error 5: confusing exactly with at least

Exactly $k$ is one term:

$$
P(X=k).
$$

At least $k$ is a tail:

$$
P(X\ge k)
=P(X=k)+P(X=k+1)+\cdots+P(X=n).
$$

### Error 6: using the wrong endpoint in a complement

The opposite of $X\ge k$ is $X\le k-1$.

The opposite of $X>k$ is $X\le k$.

Draw the possible whole-number values if the boundary feels confusing.

### Error 7: rounding a percentage threshold in the wrong direction

If at least $90\%$ of 18 must succeed, the raw value is $16.2$. Sixteen is too low, so the count must be at least $17$. Ordinary “round to the nearest integer” is not the rule.

### Error 8: assuming the larger mean has the larger variance

Mean and variance answer different questions:

$$
E[X]=np,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p).
$$

With equal $n$, a value of $p$ closer to $0.5$ gives greater variance. A very strong performer may have a higher mean and a lower variance because strong performance is also more predictable.

### Error 9: confusing variance and standard deviation

Standard deviation is the square root of variance:

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}.
$$

If variance A is four times variance B, standard deviation A is twice standard deviation B.

### Error 10: treating the ratio of $p$ values as a ratio of event probabilities

The shortcut $p_B/p_A$ works for a ratio of means when $n$ is shared:

$$
\frac{np_B}{np_A}=\frac{p_B}{p_A}.
$$

It does not work for probabilities of passing, perfect runs or other multi-trial events.

### Error 11: interpreting “$60\%$ higher” as “$160\%$ higher”

A ratio of $1.6$ means B is $160\%$ of A. The increase is only the extra part:

$$
(1.6-1)100\%=60\%.
$$

### Error 12: rounding before a comparison

Keep extra digits until the claim has been tested. Values such as $19.6874\%$ versus $20\%$, $3.947$ versus $4$, and $8.03$ versus $8$ are deliberately close. Early rounding can reverse a true-or-false answer.

### Error 13: combining different binomial variables incorrectly

Suppose

$$
X_A\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n_A,p_A),
\qquad
X_B\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n_B,p_B),
$$

and the two counts are independent.

If $p_A=p_B=p$, then

$$
X_A+X_B
\sim
\mathrm{Bin}(n_A+n_B,p).
$$

All trials share the same probability, so they can be viewed as one larger binomial experiment.

If $p_A\ne p_B$, their sum is not generally binomial. You may still add means:

$$
E[X_A+X_B]
=n_Ap_A+n_Bp_B.
$$

For independent variables, you may add variances:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X_A+X_B)
=n_Ap_A(1-p_A)
+n_Bp_B(1-p_B).
$$

But using an averaged probability can give a different spread and different event probabilities.

**Example 1.** Line A has $n_A=10$, $p_A=0.9$. Line B has $n_B=10$, $p_B=0.6$.

The combined mean is

$$
10(0.9)+10(0.6)=15.
$$

An averaged probability would be

$$
\bar p=\frac{15}{20}=0.75.
$$

The true variance is

$$
10(0.9)(0.1)+10(0.6)(0.4)
=0.9+2.4
=3.3.
$$

A pooled model $\mathrm{Bin}(20,0.75)$ would have variance

$$
20(0.75)(0.25)=3.75.
$$

The means match, but the variances do not. Therefore the pooled binomial model is not the same distribution.

### Error 14: adding component failure probabilities as an exact rule

If a device fails when at least one independent component fails, the exact probability is found through the complement. For three independent components with failure probabilities $p_1$, $p_2$ and $p_3$:

$$
P(\text{device failure})
=1-(1-p_1)(1-p_2)(1-p_3).
$$

With more components the same idea applies: multiply the individual survival probabilities, then subtract from $1$. For very small probabilities, $p_1+p_2+\cdots$ may be close, but it is an approximation because it double-counts cycles where several components fail together.

### Error 15: assuming equal means give equal probabilities

Two binomial distributions can share $np$ and still have different values of $n$, $p$, variance and exact probabilities.

For example:

$$
(0.9988)^{2500}
\ne
(0.9995)^{6000},
$$

even though both models have mean $np=3$.

## 12.8 Linear scoring rules

Some statements attach points to each trial. A common scheme is:

- $+5$ points for each success;
- $-2$ points for each failure.

If there are $n$ trials and $X$ successes, then there are $n-X$ failures. The total score is

$$
S
=5X+(-2)(n-X)
=5X-2n+2X
=7X-2n.
$$

The score is a linear function of $X$:

$$
S=aX+b
$$

with $a=7$ and $b=-2n$.

Expectation is linear, so

$$
E[S]
=aE[X]+b
=7np-2n
=n(7p-2).
$$

This is the formula needed when a claim compares expected scores rather than expected counts.

**Example 1.** A screener reviews $n=28$ bags. Screener A has $p_A=0.42$ and Screener B has $p_B=0.91$. Scoring awards $+5$ for a correct flag and $-2$ for a miss.

First find the expected counts:

$$
E[A]=28(0.42)=11.76,
$$

$$
E[B]=28(0.91)=25.48.
$$

Now convert to expected scores:

$$
E[S_A]
=7(11.76)-2(28)
=82.32-56
=26.32,
$$

$$
E[S_B]
=7(25.48)-2(28)
=178.36-56
=122.36.
$$

The expected-score gap is

$$
122.36-26.32=96.04.
$$

A claim that B’s expected score exceeds A’s by more than $95$ points is therefore true.

Notice how different this is from the raw count gap:

$$
E[B]-E[A]=13.72.
$$

If you forget to convert the scoring rule into $7X-2n$, you will compare the wrong numbers and may reverse the verdict.

### General pattern

Whenever each success is worth $c$ points and each failure is worth $d$ points,

$$
S
=cX+d(n-X)
=(c-d)X+dn,
$$

$$
E[S]
=(c-d)np+dn.
$$

Write the algebra once, then substitute the given values. Do not invent a new shortcut for every story.

## 12.9 Summary reference

| Question | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Is the model binomial? | Check fixed $n$, two categories, constant $p$, independence. |
| What does success mean? | Name the outcome being counted before choosing $p$. |
| Exactly $k$ successes | Use $\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. |
| No successes | Use $(1-p)^n$. |
| All successes | Use $p^n$. |
| At least one success | Use $1-(1-p)^n$. |
| At least $k$ | Add from $k$ through $n$, or use $1-P(X\le k-1)$. |
| At most $k$ | Add from $0$ through $k$. |
| Percentage cutoff | Convert it to a valid whole count in the direction required by the wording. |
| Mean | Use $E[X]=np$. |
| Variance | Use $\mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p)$. |
| Standard deviation | Use $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$. |
| “How many times” | Divide in the order named by the claim. |
| Percentage increase | Use $\left(\frac{\text{new}}{\text{old}}-1\right)100\%$. |
| Add independent counts | Means always add; variances add when independent. The sum is binomial only when all trials share one $p$. |
| Scoring $+c$ / $-d$ | Rewrite as a linear function of $X$, then take $E[S]$. |

The central formulas are

$$
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p),
$$

$$
P(X=k)
=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k},
$$

$$
E[X]=np,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p),
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X)=\sqrt{np(1-p)}.
$$


For the scoring rule $+5$ per success and $-2$ per failure:

$$
S=7X-2n,
\qquad
E[S]=7np-2n.
$$

**Working order.** Start by writing what one trial is and what counts as success. Identify $n$, $p$ and the random variable $X$. Check the four binomial conditions. Translate the words into an event before calculating. Decide whether the question needs one exact term, several terms, a complement, a mean, a spread, a comparison or a score. Keep extra digits until the final comparison.

**Self-check.** Can you explain why a defective item may be called a success? Can you give an example where the number of trials is not fixed? Why does the factor $\binom nk$ appear in the exact probability formula? What is the difference between $p$ and $P(X=k)$? Why is “at least 7” a sum rather than one term? Why does “at least $90\%$ of 18” start at 17? How can a distribution with a higher mean have a lower variance? Why does a ratio of one-trial probabilities not equal a ratio of pass probabilities? When can two binomial variables be combined into one binomial variable? And why does a $+5$/$-2$ scoring rule become $7X-2n$?
