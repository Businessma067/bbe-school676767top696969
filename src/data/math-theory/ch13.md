# Chapter 13 — Binomial distribution

This guide covers the binomial probability distribution as used in the BBE exam format: a fixed number of independent trials, each with the same probability of success. Everything is explained from the beginning, assuming no prior knowledge of probability distributions. It presents the required conditions, the probability mass function, the mean and variance, the translation of exam wording into cumulative sums, the standard comparisons made between two distributions, common sources of error, and the normal approximation used for large samples.

## Learning objectives

- Define a binomial count and identify success versus failure.
- Check the four required conditions before applying the formula.
- Compute $P(X = k)$ with the probability mass function.
- Use the mean and variance, including the link between variance and distance from $p = 0.5$.
- Translate exam wording into the correct summation range.
- Compare two binomial distributions (ratio, difference, percentage increase, spread).
- Avoid the common errors listed in this chapter, and apply the normal approximation when both rule-of-thumb conditions hold.

## 13.1 Definition

### The situation being described

Many exam questions describe the same setup: something is repeated a fixed number of times, each repetition can turn out in only one of two ways, and we count how often the outcome we care about occurred. Each single repetition is a **trial** — one quiz question, one free throw, one inspected item, one customer decision.

The binomial distribution describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials, where each trial has only two possible outcomes and the same probability of success. It is a discrete distribution: the random variable $X$, the number of successes, takes only integer values from $0$ to $n$. Discrete means $X$ is a count: out of 10 questions the score can be $0, 1, \ldots, 10$, never $3.5$ and never $11$.

Three symbols are used throughout: $n$ is the number of trials, fixed in advance; $p$ is the probability of success on a single trial; $X$ is the total number of successes. The standard notation is

$$
X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p),
$$

read as “$X$ follows a binomial distribution with $n$ trials and success probability $p$.”

### Success and failure

**Success** — The outcome being counted, with fixed probability $p$ on each trial — for example, a correctly answered question, a made shot, or a correctly identified defect.

**Failure** — Any other outcome, with probability $1 - p$ on each trial. This is not evaluative; it denotes only the complement of the outcome being counted.

“Success” is a label, not a judgement: if the question asks how many items are defective, a defective item is the success. Since every trial ends in one of the two categories, $p + (1-p) = 1$. Decide explicitly what counts as a success before writing anything down — choosing the wrong outcome invalidates an otherwise correct calculation.

**Example 1.** A multiple-choice exam has 10 questions, each with 4 answer choices and exactly one correct choice. A student guesses randomly on every question.

- Trial: one question  
- “Success”: answering correctly  
- $n = 10$  
- $p = 0.25$  
- Possible values of $X$: $0$ through $10$

Here $p = 1/4 = 0.25$ because one of four choices is correct and the student picks at random, so failure has probability $0.75$.

## 13.2 Required conditions

A scenario must satisfy the following four conditions before the binomial formula can be applied.

| Condition | Requirement | Example 1 check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed $n$ | The number of trials is determined in advance. | $n = 10$ questions, fixed before the exam is taken. |
| Binary outcome | Each trial results in exactly one of two outcomes. | Each question is either correct or incorrect. |
| Constant $p$ | The probability of success is identical on every trial. | $p = 0.25$ on every question, since each has 4 choices. |
| Independence | The outcome of one trial does not affect another. | Guessing on question 3 does not affect the odds on question 4. |

What each condition rules out:

- **Fixed $n$** — “answer all 10 questions” gives $n = 10$; “keep shooting until the first success” does not fix the number of trials and is not binomial.
- **Binary outcome** — three categories such as correct, partially correct, incorrect must first be merged into two.
- **Constant $p$** — if the first half of a quiz is easy and the second half hard, the trials do not share one common $p$.
- **Independence** — knowing one result must not change the probabilities of the others; this typically fails when objects are drawn from a small population without replacement.

If any condition fails, do not force a $\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ model. Sampling without replacement from a small deck makes $p$ drift and breaks independence. Two processes with different success probabilities cannot be treated as one binomial with a single pooled $p$ (see Common errors).

**Example 1.** The quiz in §13.1 satisfies all four conditions, so $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(10, 0.25)$ is appropriate.

**Example 2.** Drawing 5 cards without replacement and counting aces fails constant $p$ and independence — not binomial in the usual fixed-$p$ sense. The first card is an ace with probability $4/52$, but once a card is removed and not returned, both the aces remaining and the cards remaining have changed, so $p$ drifts from trial to trial.

**Example 3.** An inspector classifies 20 items correctly with probability $0.75$ each, independently. Then $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$. If skill improves mid-batch so later items have a higher $p$, the constant-$p$ condition breaks.

## 13.3 Probability mass function

The probability of obtaining exactly $k$ successes in $n$ trials is

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n.
$$

The formula is a product of three factors, each answering a separate question. If exactly $k$ of the $n$ trials succeed, the remaining $n - k$ must fail, so $p^{k}$ covers the $k$ successes, $(1-p)^{n-k}$ covers the $n-k$ failures, and $\binom{n}{k}$ covers the fact that those successes can occur in many different positions.

$\binom{n}{k}$ (also written $C(n,k)$, “$n$ choose $k$”) equals the number of ways to select $k$ successes among $n$ trials:

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

The exclamation mark is a factorial, the product of all whole numbers up to that value: $5! = 5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120$, and $0! = 1$.

It does not depend on which specific trials succeed, only on the count. The factor $p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of any one specific ordering: $k$ independent successes and $n-k$ independent failures.

A small case makes the counting factor concrete. With $3$ trials and $2$ successes the orderings are

```text
success, success, failure
success, failure, success
failure, success, success
```

Three orderings, matching $\binom{3}{2} = 3$, each with the same probability $p^{2}(1-p)$ — so the total is $3\,p^{2}(1-p)$. The counting factor multiplies the single-ordering probability rather than replacing it.

Useful shortcuts:

- All successes: $P(X = n) = p^{n}$
- All failures: $P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}$
- Exactly one success: $P(X = 1) = n\, p\, (1-p)^{n-1}$

They follow from the general formula: $\binom{n}{n} = \binom{n}{0} = 1$, since there is one way for everything to succeed and one way for everything to fail, while $\binom{n}{1} = n$, since the single success can sit in any of $n$ positions.

**Example 1.** Using $n = 10$, $p = 0.25$, compute $P(X = 3)$:

$$
\binom{10}{3} = 120, \quad 0.25^{3} = 0.015625, \quad 0.75^{7} \approx 0.133484,
$$

$$
P(X = 3) = 120 \times 0.015625 \times 0.133484 \approx 0.2503 \approx 25.03\%.
$$

The exponent $7$ appears because $n - k = 10 - 3$ questions must be answered incorrectly.

**Example 2.** Three fair coin flips, exactly two heads: $P(X = 2) = \binom{3}{2}(1/2)^{3} = 3/8$. Since $p = 1-p = 1/2$, the factor $p^{2}(1-p)$ collapses to $(1/2)^{3}$.

**Example 3.** A perfect run of 10 guesses at $p = 0.25$ is $(0.25)^{10} \approx 9.54 \times 10^{-7}$. Near a round percentage cutoff, compute $p^{n}$ exactly rather than estimating by eye — high powers shrink a probability far faster than intuition suggests.

## 13.4 Mean and variance

The mean, variance, and standard deviation summarize the distribution without listing every individual probability.

$$
E[X] = np
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

The **mean** $E[X]$, also called the expected value, is the average number of successes over many repetitions of the whole experiment; it need not be a value a single experiment could produce, so a mean of $2.5$ out of $10$ is a long-run average rather than a possible score. The formula is intuitive: each trial contributes on average $p$ successes, and there are $n$ of them.

The **variance** measures spread — how far results typically fall from the mean. It is in squared units, which makes it awkward to read directly; the **standard deviation** is its square root and is in the same units as $X$, answering how many successes a typical result differs from the mean by.

Variance can also be written from the general discrete definition $\sum (x - \mu)^{2} P(X = x)$; for a binomial variable that expression simplifies to $np(1-p)$. In practice you use the simplified form.

**Key relationship.** For a fixed $n$, the product $p(1-p)$ is maximized at $p = 0.5$ and decreases as $p$ moves toward $0$ or $1$. Consequently, variance is largest when $p$ is closest to $0.5$, regardless of whether $p$ is otherwise “high” or “low.” A probability near $0$ or $1$ makes each trial nearly predictable, leaving little room for variation, while $p = 0.5$ makes both outcomes equally plausible: numerically $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$ against $0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.09$.

When two sides share the same $n$:

- Mean difference: $E[B] - E[A] = n(p_B - p_A)$
- Mean ratio: $E[B]/E[A] = p_B/p_A$

In the ratio the shared $n$ cancels, so this shortcut is valid only when both sides truly have the same number of trials.

**Example 1.** Two students each answer 20 questions. Student A has $p = 0.50$; Student B has $p = 0.80$.

- Mean A: $20 \times 0.50 = 10$; mean B: $20 \times 0.80 = 16$
- Variance A: $20 \times 0.50 \times 0.50 = 5.0$; variance B: $20 \times 0.80 \times 0.20 = 3.2$

Student A’s scores vary more, despite the lower mean: $p = 0.50$ sits exactly at maximum variance, while $0.80$ is further from $0.5$ and therefore more predictable.

**Example 2.** On a 10-question quiz with $p = 0.25$, $E[X] = 2.5$. With $p = 0.90$, $E[X] = 9$. The gap is $n(0.90 - 0.25) = 6.5$.

**Example 3.** An inspector with $n = 20$, $p = 0.75$ has mean $15$. If certification needs at least 18 correct, the average lies below the bar — a comparison that uses only $np$ and no probability calculation at all.

## 13.5 Cumulative probabilities and exam wording

Statements in this exam format frequently describe a range of outcomes in words rather than in mathematical notation. The translation depends only on the wording, not on the values of $n$ or $p$.

| Wording | Notation |
| --- | --- |
| “at least $k$” | $\sum_{x=k}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| “at most $k$” | $\sum_{x=0}^{k} P(X = x)$ |
| “more than $k$” | $\sum_{x=k+1}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| “fewer than $k$” | $\sum_{x=0}^{k-1} P(X = x)$ |
| “between $a$ and $b$, inclusive” | $\sum_{x=a}^{b} P(X = x)$ |
| “exactly $k$” | $P(X = k)$, a single term |

These are sums because only one value of $X$ can actually occur — the count cannot be both $7$ and $8$ — and probabilities of mutually exclusive outcomes add. So “at least 7 out of 10” is the combined probability of $7$, $8$, $9$ and $10$, never a single term.

“At least $k$” includes $k$; “more than $k$” starts at $k+1$. Complements help on long tails: $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1)$.

The complement rule works because all values together carry probability $1$. Watch where the opposite event stops: the opposite of $X \ge k$ is $X \le k-1$, not $X \le k$, because $k$ belongs to the original event. The same idea gives the most common shortcut in the chapter, $P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (1-p)^{n}$.

**Percentage thresholds.** When a threshold is expressed as a percentage of $n$, it frequently does not correspond to a whole number. For example, 90% of 18 units is $16.2$. Since the count must be an integer, “at least 90%” requires rounding $16.2$ **up** to $17$ (the smallest integer that still meets the requirement). Conversely, “at most” thresholds round **down**. Rounding in the wrong direction changes the summation range. Note that $16$ units is only $88.9\%$ of $18$ and fails the requirement, so rounding to the nearest integer would give the wrong range.

**Example 1.** Using $n = 10$, $p = 0.90$, the probability of at least 7 correct:

$$
\begin{align*}
P(X=7) &\approx 0.0574 \\
P(X=8) &\approx 0.1937 \\
P(X=9) &\approx 0.3874 \\
P(X=10) &\approx 0.3487 \\
P(X \ge 7) &\approx 0.9872 \approx 98.72\%.
\end{align*}
$$

Each line is one application of the formula from §13.3; the last line adds them because the outcomes are mutually exclusive.

**Example 2.** With $n = 5$, $p = 0.2$: $P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (0.8)^{5} \approx 0.672$. Summing the terms for $1$ through $5$ gives the same value far more slowly.

**Example 3.** For $n = 10$, “more than 8” means $X \ge 9$, while “at least 8” means $X \ge 8$ — a difference of one whole term, $P(X = 8)$, often large enough to flip a true/false verdict.

## 13.6 Comparing two distributions

Many exam questions present two entities with a shared binomial structure but different values of $p$, and ask for a comparison. The comparison generally takes one of the following four forms.

**Ratio** — “$B$ is $X$ times as likely as $A$.” Compute both probabilities in full, then divide. Do not round to the nearest whole number before comparing to the stated threshold. The order matters: the entity named first goes in the numerator, and reversing it gives the reciprocal and the opposite verdict.

**Difference** — “$B$ exceeds $A$ by more than $X$.” Subtract directly. For two means with the same $n$, the difference simplifies to $n(p_B - p_A)$.

**Percentage increase** — “$B$ is $X\%$ higher than $A$.” Compute the ratio of the two means, subtract $1$, and multiply by $100$. When $n$ is shared, this equals the ratio of the two $p$-values. Subtracting $1$ is what turns a ratio into an increase: a ratio of $1.6$ is a $60\%$ increase, not $160\%$.

**Variance or standard deviation ratio** — Compute $np(1-p)$ separately for each entity before dividing. Take the square root only if the comparison concerns standard deviation rather than variance.

**Example 1.** Using the students from §13.4 ($n = 20$), the percentage by which B’s mean exceeds A’s mean is $(16/10 - 1) \times 100 = 60\%$.

**Example 2.** Free throws, $n = 10$, $p_A = 0.55$, $p_B = 0.85$. Perfect run for B: $(0.85)^{10} \approx 0.1969$ (just under 20%). Mean gap: $10(0.85 - 0.55) = 3$. Rookie SD is larger because $0.55$ is closer to $0.5$. Since $0.1969$ sits just below the round threshold $0.20$, a claim of “more than a 20% chance of a perfect run” is false by a narrow margin — exactly the case estimation cannot settle.

**Example 3.** If $P_A(\text{pass}) \approx 0.35\%$ and $P_B(\text{pass}) \approx 98.72\%$, then $P_B/P_A \approx 282$. A claim “at least 300 times as likely” is false — compute the ratio; do not round it up, and never substitute $p_B/p_A$ for a ratio of probabilities.

## 13.7 Common errors

The following errors account for the majority of incorrect answers on this exam format.

1. **Estimating ratio thresholds instead of computing them.** A claim such as “more than 20 times as likely” is frequently placed close to the true ratio (for example, $19.7$ or $20.3$). Estimating rather than computing produces the wrong answer.

2. **Assuming a higher mean implies a higher variance.** The entity with the higher success probability (further from $p = 0.5$) frequently has the lower variance. Variance depends on distance from $0.5$, not on which value of $p$ corresponds to better performance.

3. **Overlooking the symmetry between $p$ and $1-p$.** A binomial with parameter $p$ has the same variance as one with parameter $1-p$ for the same $n$, because $p(1-p)$ is unchanged when the two swap places. Additionally, $P(X = 0)$ at probability $p$ equals $P(X = n)$ at probability $1-p$, since relabelling the success turns “none” into “all”.

4. **Treating the sum of two different binomial variables as binomial.** If two independent processes have different success probabilities, their combined count does not follow a binomial with a pooled probability, even though the means still add. The true combined variance will generally not equal the variance of a pooled binomial with the same mean. Two counts do combine into one binomial when $p$ is identical on both sides, since then all $n_1 + n_2$ trials share a single $p$; with different probabilities there is no common $p$ to put in the formula.

5. **Checking only one side of the normal-approximation rule of thumb.** The rule requires both $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$. A large value on one side does not compensate for a small value on the other.

**Example 1.** Line A: $n_1 = 10$, $p_1 = 0.9$. Line B: $n_2 = 10$, $p_2 = 0.6$. Combined mean $15$, pooled $\bar{p} = 0.75$. True variance $3.3$; pooled $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$ variance $3.75$. The combined count is not $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$. The true figure adds the separate variances $10(0.9)(0.1) = 0.9$ and $10(0.6)(0.4) = 2.4$; identical means do not imply identical distributions.

**Example 2.** Same $n$, $p = 0.2$ and $p = 0.8$ share the same variance $np(1-p)$, illustrating the $p$ / $1-p$ symmetry.

**Example 3.** A ratio claim with a round multiplier near the true value is designed to punish estimation — always divide the two computed probabilities.

## 13.8 Normal approximation

For large $n$, computing the exact binomial probability term by term becomes impractical. The normal approximation is used, subject to a validity condition:

$$
X \approx \mathcal{N}\big(np,\, np(1-p)\big)
\quad\text{when}\quad np \ge 5 \text{ and } n(1-p) \ge 5.
$$

A cumulative probability such as $P(X \ge 25)$ with $n = 50$ needs $26$ separate terms. When $n$ is large and both conditions hold, the binomial shape is close enough to a bell curve to use a normal distribution with the same mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$. The conditions ensure the distribution is not squeezed against either end of its range: a very small $np$ piles probability near $0$ successes, a very small $n(1-p)$ does the same at the top, and each side must be checked separately.

**Continuity correction:** for “at most $k$,” use $k + 0.5$; for “at least $k$,” use $k - 0.5$, before standardizing to a $z$-score.

The correction exists because $X$ is a count while the normal curve is continuous, so a single whole number has no width of its own and is treated as the interval from $k - 0.5$ to $k + 0.5$. Extending the boundary by $0.5$ in the direction that keeps $k$ inside the event is what matches the count being described. Standardizing converts the boundary into a $z$-score, the number of standard deviations from the mean:

$$
z = \frac{\text{corrected boundary} - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
$$

**Example 1.** For $n = 40$, $p = 0.3$: $np = 12$ and $n(1-p) = 28$ — both $\ge 5$, so the approximation is reasonable. For $n = 20$, $p = 0.05$: $np = 1$ — do not use the normal approximation.

**Example 2.** For $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(50, 0.4)$ and $P(X \ge 25)$, mean $20$, variance $12$. With continuity correction use $P(Y \ge 24.5)$ and $z = (24.5 - 20)/\sqrt{12} \approx 1.30$. The boundary is $24.5$ rather than $25.5$ because “at least 25” includes the value $25$.

**Example 3.** A statement that applies the normal approximation after checking only $np \ge 5$ (and ignoring $n(1-p)$) is using the rule incorrectly.

## 13.9 Summary reference

| Task | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Verify the distribution is binomial | Confirm fixed $n$, binary outcome, constant $p$, independence. |
| “Exactly $k$” | $\binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ |
| “At least / at most / between” | Convert to a summation range per §13.5. |
| Percentage threshold given | Convert to a whole-number count; round up for “at least,” round down for “at most.” |
| “How many times as likely” | Compute both probabilities in full, then divide. |
| Which distribution has greater spread | The $p$-value closer to $0.5$ yields the greater variance, for equal $n$. |
| Large $n$, approximation needed | Normal approximation if $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$. |

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p), \qquad \mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

**Working order for a statement.** State what counts as a success and write down $n$ and $p$; check the four conditions from §13.2; translate the wording into $=$, $\le$ or $\ge$ using the table in §13.5; decide whether the statement needs a single term, a sum, a mean, a variance, a ratio, or an approximation; compute without rounding intermediate values, especially against a round threshold; then compare with the exact claim before answering.

**Self-check.** Can you explain what $n$, $p$ and $X$ represent and why the success must be defined first; which four conditions must hold; why $\binom{n}{k}$ belongs in the formula; the difference between “at least $k$” and “more than $k$”; why $P(X \ge 1)$ is easier as $1 - (1-p)^{n}$; how the higher mean can carry the lower variance; why percentage thresholds round up for “at least” and down for “at most”; and why both rule-of-thumb conditions and the continuity correction are needed?
