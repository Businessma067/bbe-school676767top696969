# Chapter 13 — Binomial distribution

Counting successes in a fixed number of yes/no trials is one of the most common exam patterns in WU BBE mathematics. This chapter builds the binomial model from Bernoulli trials through the probability mass function, mean and variance, tails and complements, comparisons of two sides, and the approximations you meet on harder tasks — with worked examples at each step so the formulas become habits rather than slogans.

## Learning objectives

- Recognise a **Bernoulli trial** and the four conditions of a **binomial setting**.
- Compute $P(X = k)$ with the binomial PMF and interpret $\binom{n}{k}$.
- Use $E[X] = np$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)$ (and the standard deviation) without mixing them up.
- Translate English phrases such as “at least”, “at most”, “exactly”, and “all / none” into the correct sums or single terms.
- Compare two binomial counts (means, variances, and probability ratios) carefully.
- Apply **Poisson** and **normal** approximations when the task asks for them, and know when a sum of binomials is *not* itself binomial.

---

## 13.1 Bernoulli trials

A **Bernoulli trial** is a single experiment with exactly two outcomes:

- **success**, with probability $p$
- **failure**, with probability $q = 1 - p$

Typical labels: correct/incorrect, germinate/not, convert/not, defect/ok. The name “success” is only a label for the outcome you are counting — it need not be desirable. A “success” can be a defective item if that is what $X$ is counting.

If $Y$ is the indicator of success on one trial ($Y = 1$ on success, $Y = 0$ on failure), then

$$
E[Y] = p, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y) = p(1-p).
$$

Variance $p(1-p)$ is largest when $p = 1/2$ and shrinks toward $0$ as $p$ approaches $0$ or $1$. That single fact explains many true/false traps about which of two people has the more variable score.

> **Example — one free throw**  
> A player makes a free throw with probability $p = 0.8$. One shot is a Bernoulli trial with success = “make”.  
> $E[Y] = 0.8$, $\mathrm{Var}(Y) = 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$, $\mathrm{SD}(Y) = 0.4$.  
> If a weaker player has $p = 0.5$, then $\mathrm{Var} = 0.25$ — **larger** variance despite a lower make rate.

> **Example — variance peaks at $1/2$**  
> Compare $p(1-p)$ for a few values:  
> $p = 0.1$ → $0.09$; $p = 0.25$ → $0.1875$; $p = 0.5$ → $0.25$; $p = 0.9$ → $0.09$.  
> So a guesser near $p = 0.25$ is often *more* variable than an expert near $p = 0.9$.

## 13.2 The binomial setting

Let $X$ be the **number of successes** in $n$ trials. We write

$$
X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)
$$

when all of the following hold:

1. **Fixed $n$** — the number of trials is set in advance.
2. **Two outcomes** each time — success or failure.
3. **Constant $p$** — the success probability does not change from trial to trial.
4. **Independence** — one trial’s result does not change the others.

If any condition fails (for example, sampling without replacement from a tiny population so that $p$ drifts, or two production lines with different defect rates forced into one “pooled” binomial), the plain $\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ model is the wrong story.

> **Exam habit**  
> Before computing, name $n$, $p$, and what counts as a success. Many false statements quietly change one of these.

> **Example — multiple-choice exam**  
> A quiz has $n = 10$ questions, each with 4 choices and one correct answer. A student who guesses every question has $p = 1/4 = 0.25$ per question. If guesses are independent,  
> $X =$ number correct $\sim \mathrm{Bin}(10, 0.25)$.  
> Success = “correct”; failure = “wrong”. The setting fits: fixed $n$, two outcomes, constant $p$, independence.

> **Example — when it fails**  
> Drawing 5 cards from a 52-card deck **without replacement** and counting aces is **not** binomial with constant $p$: after each draw the deck composition changes, so $p$ is not the same on every trial (and trials are dependent). A large-population “with replacement” story, or an independent-shot story, is needed for $\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$.

## 13.3 Probability mass function

For $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n$,

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k} (1-p)^{n-k}.
$$

Two pieces multiply:

- $\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ counts how many sequences of length $n$ have exactly $k$ successes.
- $p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of any **one** such sequence under independence.

Special cases worth memorising:

| Event | Formula | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All successes | $P(X = n) = p^{n}$ | $\binom{n}{n} = 1$ and $(1-p)^{0} = 1$ |
| All failures | $P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}$ | $\binom{n}{0} = 1$ and $p^{0} = 1$ |
| Exactly one success | $P(X = 1) = n\, p\, (1-p)^{n-1}$ | $n$ positions for the single success |

Raising a probability below $1/2$ to a large power shrinks it extremely fast — perfect-run claims near a round percentage are often designed as close calls.

> **Example — three coin flips, exactly two heads**  
> Fair coin: $n = 3$, $p = 1/2$, success = heads.  
> $$P(X = 2) = \binom{3}{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{1} = 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} = \tfrac{3}{8}.$$  
> The three sequences are HHT, HTH, THH — each with probability $1/8$.

> **Example — guesser gets all 10 correct**  
> $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(10, 0.25)$. A perfect paper is  
> $$P(X = 10) = (0.25)^{10} \approx 9.54 \times 10^{-7} \approx 0.000095\%,$$  
> far below $0.01\%$. Near a round cutoff, compute $p^{n}$ exactly — do not eyeball.

> **Example — building $\binom{n}{k}$**  
> $\binom{10}{3} = \dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!} = \dfrac{10 \times 9 \times 8}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = 120$.  
> So there are 120 ways to choose which 3 of 10 trials succeed; multiply by $p^{3}(1-p)^{7}$ for the probability of exactly three successes.

## 13.4 Mean, variance, and standard deviation

Write $X = Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n$ as a sum of independent Bernoulli indicators with the same $p$. Linearity and independence give

$$
E[X] = np,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p),
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}.
$$

Useful comparisons when two sides share the same $n$:

- Mean difference: $E[B] - E[A] = n(p_B - p_A)$.
- Mean ratio: $\dfrac{E[B]}{E[A]} = \dfrac{p_B}{p_A}$ (the $n$ cancels).
- Which variance is larger? Compare distance of each $p$ from $1/2$ — closer to $1/2$ means larger $p(1-p)$.

> **Trap**  
> A higher success rate does **not** automatically mean higher variance. An expert with $p = 0.90$ is typically *less* variable than a guesser with $p = 0.25$.

> **Example — expected correct answers**  
> Guesser: $n = 10$, $p = 0.25$ → $E[X] = 2.5$.  
> Prepared student: $p = 0.90$ → $E[X] = 9$.  
> Same $n$, different means; the gap is $n(0.90 - 0.25) = 6.5$.

> **Example — who is more variable?**  
> Same exam, $n = 10$:  
> Guesser $p = 0.25$: $\mathrm{Var} = 10 \times 0.25 \times 0.75 = 1.875$.  
> Expert $p = 0.90$: $\mathrm{Var} = 10 \times 0.90 \times 0.10 = 0.90$.  
> The guesser’s score spreads more. Statement “the expert has higher variance” is false.

> **Example — mean vs a pass bar**  
> Inspector with $n = 20$, $p = 0.75$: $E[X] = 15$.  
> Certification needs at least 18 correct. On average the inspector is **below** the bar ($15 < 18$). That comparison uses only $np$ — no PMF required.

## 13.5 Cumulative probabilities and English tails

Exam statements almost never stop at “exactly $k$”. Translate the English carefully:

| Phrase | Event | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| exactly $k$ | $X = k$ | single PMF term |
| at least $k$ | $X \ge k$ | $\sum_{x=k}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| at most $k$ | $X \le k$ | $\sum_{x=0}^{k} P(X = x)$ |
| more than $k$ | $X > k$ | $\sum_{x=k+1}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| fewer than $k$ | $X < k$ | $\sum_{x=0}^{k-1} P(X = x)$ |
| at least one | $X \ge 1$ | $1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - (1-p)^{n}$ |

The outcomes $X = k, k+1, \ldots, n$ are mutually exclusive, so an “at least” probability is literally a **sum** of binomial terms — not one term and not $p$ itself.

Complement shortcuts save time on long tails:

$$
P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1),
$$

$$
P(X \le k) = 1 - P(X \ge k+1).
$$

> **Example — “at least 7 out of 10”**  
> Pass if $X \ge 7$ with $n = 10$:  
> $$P(X \ge 7) = P(X=7) + P(X=8) + P(X=9) + P(X=10) = \sum_{x=7}^{10} \binom{10}{x} p^{x}(1-p)^{10-x}.$$  
> A single term $P(X = 7)$ would undercount. On true/false items, check that the written sum starts at the threshold and ends at $n$.

> **Example — at least one success**  
> $n = 5$, $p = 0.2$:  
> $$P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (0.8)^{5} = 1 - 0.32768 = 0.67232.$$  
> Computing $P(1)+\cdots+P(5)$ works but is slower; the complement is the exam shortcut.

> **Example — “more than 8” vs “at least 8”**  
> For $n = 10$: “more than 8” is $X \ge 9$ (i.e. $X = 9$ or $10$), while “at least 8” is $X \ge 8$. One word changes the starting index of the sum — read carefully.

## 13.6 Comparing two binomial sides

Many BBE-style tasks put two people, machines, or batches side by side with the same $n$ but different $p$ (or occasionally different $n$). Typical claim types:

1. **Perfect run** — check $p^{n}$ against a percentage cutoff; compute precisely near round thresholds.
2. **Tail wording** — verify that “at least $k$” is written as the sum from $k$ to $n$ with the stated $n$ and $p$.
3. **Likelihood ratios** — compute both tails, then divide. Do **not** replace the ratio of probabilities by the ratio of the $p$ values, and do not round $8.24$ up to “about $9$”.
4. **Variance / SD** — use $np(1-p)$ and $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$; remember the $p \approx 1/2$ rule.
5. **Means vs thresholds** — comparing $np$ to a cutoff needs no probability calculation.

> **Ratio discipline**  
> “$B$ is at least $300$ times as likely as $A$ to pass” means $\dfrac{P_B(\text{pass})}{P_A(\text{pass})} \ge 300$. Compute both pass probabilities first.

> **Example — free throws, clutch bonus**  
> $n = 10$ shots. Rookie $p_A = 0.55$, all-star $p_B = 0.85$. Clutch = at least 8 makes.  
> Perfect run for B: $P(X_B = 10) = (0.85)^{10} \approx 0.1969$ (just **under** 20%).  
> Mean gap: $E[B] - E[A] = 10(0.85 - 0.55) = 3$ exactly.  
> SD: $\mathrm{SD}(A) = \sqrt{10 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} \approx 1.57 > \mathrm{SD}(B) \approx 1.13$, so the rookie is more variable.

> **Example — ratio trap**  
> Suppose $P_A(\text{pass}) \approx 0.35\%$ and $P_B(\text{pass}) \approx 98.72\%$.  
> $$\frac{P_B}{P_A} \approx \frac{98.72}{0.35} \approx 282.$$  
> A claim “at least 300 times as likely” is **false** even though 282 is “near” 300. Do not round the ratio up to the exam’s round number.

> **Example — same wording, different $p$**  
> Statement: “$P(A \text{ certifies}) = \sum_{x=18}^{20} \binom{20}{x}(0.75)^{x}(0.25)^{20-x}$.”  
> If the scenario is $n = 20$, $p_A = 0.75$, threshold “at least 18”, the sum bounds and parameters match — the statement is about **notation**, not about computing the decimal value.

## 13.7 When a sum is not binomial

If $X_1 \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_1, p_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_2, p_2)$ are independent, then

$$
X_1 + X_2 \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_1 + n_2, p)
$$

**only when** $p_1 = p_2 = p$.

If $p_1 \neq p_2$, the total is a **Poisson-binomial** count (independent Bernoullis with unequal success probabilities). You may still use linearity:

$$
E[X_1 + X_2] = n_1 p_1 + n_2 p_2,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = n_1 p_1(1-p_1) + n_2 p_2(1-p_2).
$$

A “pooled” rate $\bar{p} = \dfrac{n_1 p_1 + n_2 p_2}{n_1 + n_2}$ matches the mean of $\mathrm{Bin}(n_1+n_2, \bar{p})$, but the variances generally differ — equal means do not make the distributions equal.

> **Example — two production lines**  
> Line A: $n_1 = 10$, $p_1 = 0.9$. Line B: $n_2 = 10$, $p_2 = 0.6$. Independent.  
> Total successes $S = X_A + X_B$:  
> $E[S] = 9 + 6 = 15$, so $\bar{p} = 15/20 = 0.75$.  
> True variance: $10 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 + 10 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.9 + 2.4 = 3.3$.  
> Pooled binomial $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$ has variance $20 \times 0.75 \times 0.25 = 3.75 \neq 3.3$.  
> So $S$ is **not** $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$, even though the means match.

> **Example — when pooling is fine**  
> Two identical machines, each $\mathrm{Bin}(8, 0.7)$ and independent → total $\mathrm{Bin}(16, 0.7)$. Same $p$ is the key.

## 13.8 Poisson approximation

When $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $\lambda = np$ stays moderate,

$$
P(X = k) \approx e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}, \qquad X \approx \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda).
$$

Use this for rare events (defects, mutations, server errors) when a task invokes the Poisson model. Check that $p$ is small and $n$ is large before trusting the approximation on an exam claim.

> **Example — rare defects**  
> $n = 200$ items, defect probability $p = 0.01$ → $\lambda = np = 2$.  
> $$P(X = 0) \approx e^{-2} \approx 0.1353, \qquad P(X = 1) \approx e^{-2}\cdot 2 \approx 0.2707.$$  
> Exact binomial values are close; the Poisson formula is much faster by hand.

> **Example — when not to use Poisson**  
> $n = 10$, $p = 0.4$ → $\lambda = 4$, but $p$ is not small and $n$ is not large. Prefer the exact binomial (or a calculator), not Poisson.

## 13.9 Normal approximation

For large $n$ with both $np$ and $n(1-p)$ at least about $5$,

$$
X \approx \mathcal{N}\big(np,\, np(1-p)\big).
$$

Because $X$ is discrete and the normal is continuous, tail comparisons usually need a **continuity correction**, for example

$$
P(X \ge k) \approx P\big(Y \ge k - \tfrac{1}{2}\big)
$$

for $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(np, np(1-p))$. Always verify the $np$ and $n(1-p)$ rule of thumb on each side before accepting a normal-approximation claim.

> **Example — rule of thumb check**  
> $n = 40$, $p = 0.3$: $np = 12$, $n(1-p) = 28$ — both $\ge 5$, so a normal approximation is reasonable.  
> $n = 20$, $p = 0.05$: $np = 1$ — too small; do **not** trust the normal rule of thumb here (Poisson may fit better if $p$ is tiny).

> **Example — continuity correction sketch**  
> $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(50, 0.4)$, want $P(X \ge 25)$.  
> Mean $np = 20$, variance $np(1-p) = 12$, $\mathrm{SD} = \sqrt{12} \approx 3.46$.  
> With continuity correction: $P(X \ge 25) \approx P(Y \ge 24.5)$ for $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(20, 12)$,  
> $$z = \frac{24.5 - 20}{\sqrt{12}} \approx 1.30.$$  
> Then use a standard normal table or calculator for $P(Z \ge 1.30)$.

## 13.10 Skewness (shape)

Binomial skewness is

$$
\gamma_1 = \frac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
$$

- If $p < 1/2$, the distribution is skewed right (long right tail).
- If $p > 1/2$, skewed left.
- If $p = 1/2$, skewness is $0$ (symmetric).

As $n$ grows with $p$ fixed away from the extremes, skewness shrinks and the shape becomes more bell-like — consistent with the normal approximation.

> **Example — shape from $p$**  
> $n = 20$, $p = 0.2$: $\gamma_1 = \dfrac{1 - 0.4}{\sqrt{20 \times 0.2 \times 0.8}} = \dfrac{0.6}{\sqrt{3.2}} \approx 0.335$ (skewed right).  
> $n = 20$, $p = 0.8$: sign flips — skewed left, mirror image of the $p = 0.2$ case.  
> $n = 20$, $p = 0.5$: $\gamma_1 = 0$ — symmetric about the mean $10$.

## 13.11 Calculator and exam workflow

On TI-style calculators used in BBE practice:

- **binompdf**($n$, $p$, $k$) → $P(X = k)$
- **binomcdf**($n$, $p$, $k$) → $P(X \le k)$

Then build other tails from complements, for example $P(X \ge k) = 1 - \texttt{binomcdf}(n,p,k-1)$.

> **Example — calculator path for a pass probability**  
> $n = 15$, $p = 0.6$, need $P(X \ge 13)$:  
> $$P(X \ge 13) = 1 - P(X \le 12) = 1 - \texttt{binomcdf}(15, 0.6, 12).$$  
> Or sum $\texttt{binompdf}$ at $13$, $14$, and $15$ — same answer, more keystrokes.

A reliable attack order for true/false statements:

1. Confirm the binomial setting ($n$, $p$, independence, two outcomes).
2. Translate the English event into $=$, $\le$, or $\ge$.
3. Decide whether you need a single term, a sum, a mean/variance identity, or an approximation.
4. Near a round cutoff (20%, 300×, “more than 4 times”), compute exactly — do not eyeball.

> **Example — full mini-case**  
> Agent A: $n = 15$, $p = 0.60$. Agent B: $p = 0.88$. Bonus if at least 13 first-contact resolutions.  
> - Perfect run B: $(0.88)^{15} \approx 0.147$ — just under 15%, so “$> 15\%$” is false.  
> - Tail wording for A: sum from $x = 13$ to $15$ with $p = 0.60$ — true if written that way.  
> - Mean B: $15 \times 0.88 = 13.2 > 13$ — on average B clears the bar.  
> - SD A: $\sqrt{15 \times 0.6 \times 0.4} \approx 1.90$ — “SD $> 2$” is false.

## 13.12 Quick formula sheet

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p), \qquad \mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

$$
P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (1-p)^{n}, \qquad P(X = n) = p^{n}, \qquad P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}
$$

$$
\text{Poisson: } \lambda = np; \qquad \text{Normal: } X \approx \mathcal{N}(np, np(1-p))
$$

$$
\gamma_1 = \frac{1-2p}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
$$

When you can state the setting, write the PMF, move fluently between English and sums, work a short numerical example for means/variances/tails, and handle approximations without mixing the tools, you are ready for the practice tasks in this chapter.
