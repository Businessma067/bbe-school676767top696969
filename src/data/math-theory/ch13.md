# Chapter 13 — Binomial distribution

This guide covers the binomial distribution the way the BBE exam uses it: a fixed number of independent tries, each with the same chance of success. It starts from zero, so no earlier knowledge of probability distributions is needed. You will meet the four conditions, the main formula, the mean and variance, the wording used in exam statements, the usual comparisons between two cases, the mistakes that cost the most points, and the normal approximation for large samples.

## Learning objectives

- Define a binomial count and identify success versus failure.
- Check the four required conditions before applying the formula.
- Compute $P(X = k)$ with the probability mass function.
- Use the mean and variance, including the link between variance and distance from $p = 0.5$.
- Translate exam wording into the correct summation range.
- Compare two binomial distributions (ratio, difference, percentage increase, spread).
- Avoid the common errors listed in this chapter, and apply the normal approximation when both rule-of-thumb conditions hold.

## 13.1 Definition

### The situation being described

A lot of exam questions describe the same picture. Something is repeated a fixed number of times, each repetition can end in only one of two ways, and we count how often the outcome we care about happened. One repetition is called a **trial**. A trial can be one quiz question, one free throw, one inspected item, or one customer decision.

The binomial distribution describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials, where each trial has only two possible outcomes and the same probability of success. It is a discrete distribution: the random variable $X$, the number of successes, takes only whole values from $0$ to $n$. Discrete simply means $X$ counts things. Out of 10 questions the score can be $0, 1, \ldots, 10$. It cannot be $3.5$ and it cannot be $11$.

Three symbols do all the work. $n$ is the number of trials, fixed before we start. $p$ is the probability of success on one trial. $X$ is the total number of successes. The standard notation is

$$
X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p),
$$

read as “$X$ follows a binomial distribution with $n$ trials and success probability $p$.”

### Success and failure

**Success** is the outcome being counted, with fixed probability $p$ on each trial. It might be a correctly answered question, a made shot, or a correctly identified defect.

**Failure** is any other outcome, with probability $1 - p$ on each trial. The word carries no judgement. It only means the opposite of the outcome being counted.

“Success” is a label, not praise. If the question asks how many items are defective, then a defective item is the success. Every trial ends in one of the two categories, so $p + (1-p) = 1$. Write down what counts as a success before you calculate anything, because picking the wrong outcome ruins a calculation that is otherwise correct.

**Example 1.** A multiple-choice exam has 10 questions, each with 4 answer choices and exactly one correct choice. A student guesses randomly on every question.

- Trial: one question  
- “Success”: answering correctly  
- $n = 10$  
- $p = 0.25$  
- Possible values of $X$: $0$ through $10$

The value $p = 1/4 = 0.25$ comes from one correct choice out of four with random guessing, which leaves probability $0.75$ for failure.

## 13.2 Required conditions

A scenario must satisfy the following four conditions before the binomial formula can be applied.

| Condition | Requirement | Example 1 check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed $n$ | The number of trials is determined in advance. | $n = 10$ questions, fixed before the exam is taken. |
| Binary outcome | Each trial results in exactly one of two outcomes. | Each question is either correct or incorrect. |
| Constant $p$ | The probability of success is identical on every trial. | $p = 0.25$ on every question, since each has 4 choices. |
| Independence | The outcome of one trial does not affect another. | Guessing on question 3 does not affect the odds on question 4. |

What each condition rules out:

- **Fixed $n$.** “Answer all 10 questions” gives $n = 10$. “Keep shooting until the first success” never fixes the number of trials, so it is not binomial.
- **Binary outcome.** Three categories such as correct, partially correct and incorrect have to be merged into two first.
- **Constant $p$.** If the first half of a quiz is easy and the second half is hard, the trials do not share one common $p$.
- **Independence.** Knowing one result must not change the odds for the others. This usually breaks when objects are drawn from a small population without replacement.

If any condition fails, do not force a $\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ model. Sampling without replacement from a small deck makes $p$ drift and breaks independence. Two processes with different success probabilities cannot be treated as one binomial with a single pooled $p$ (see Common errors).

**Example 1.** The quiz in §13.1 satisfies all four conditions, so $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(10, 0.25)$ is appropriate.

**Example 2.** Drawing 5 cards without replacement and counting aces fails constant $p$ and independence, so it is not binomial in the usual fixed-$p$ sense. The first card is an ace with probability $4/52$. Once that card is removed and not returned, both the aces left and the cards left have changed, so $p$ drifts from trial to trial.

**Example 3.** An inspector classifies 20 items correctly with probability $0.75$ each, independently. Then $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$. If skill improves mid-batch so later items have a higher $p$, the constant-$p$ condition breaks.

## 13.3 Probability mass function

The probability of obtaining exactly $k$ successes in $n$ trials is

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n.
$$

The formula multiplies three factors, and each one answers a separate question. If exactly $k$ of the $n$ trials succeed, the other $n - k$ must fail. So $p^{k}$ takes care of the $k$ successes, $(1-p)^{n-k}$ takes care of the $n-k$ failures, and $\binom{n}{k}$ takes care of the many positions those successes can occupy.

$\binom{n}{k}$ (also written $C(n,k)$, “$n$ choose $k$”) equals the number of ways to select $k$ successes among $n$ trials:

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

The exclamation mark is a factorial, the product of all whole numbers up to that value. For instance $5! = 5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120$, and $0! = 1$.

The binomial coefficient does not care which particular trials succeed, only how many. The factor $p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of one specific ordering: $k$ independent successes and $n-k$ independent failures.

A small case makes the counting factor concrete. With $3$ trials and $2$ successes, the possible orderings are:

1. success, success, failure;
2. success, failure, success;
3. failure, success, success.

Three orderings, which matches $\binom{3}{2} = 3$, and each has the same probability $p^{2}(1-p)$. The total is therefore $3\,p^{2}(1-p)$. The counting factor multiplies the single-ordering probability instead of replacing it.

Useful shortcuts:

- All successes: $P(X = n) = p^{n}$
- All failures: $P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}$
- Exactly one success: $P(X = 1) = n\, p\, (1-p)^{n-1}$

They follow straight from the general formula. Both $\binom{n}{n}$ and $\binom{n}{0}$ equal $1$, since there is one way for everything to succeed and one way for everything to fail, while $\binom{n}{1} = n$, since the single success can sit in any of $n$ positions.

**Example 1.** Using $n = 10$, $p = 0.25$, compute $P(X = 3)$:

$$
\binom{10}{3} = 120, \quad 0.25^{3} = 0.015625, \quad 0.75^{7} \approx 0.133484,
$$

$$
P(X = 3) = 120 \times 0.015625 \times 0.133484 \approx 0.2503 \approx 25.03\%.
$$

The exponent $7$ shows up because $n - k = 10 - 3$ questions have to be answered incorrectly.

**Example 2.** Three fair coin flips, exactly two heads: $P(X = 2) = \binom{3}{2}(1/2)^{3} = 3/8$. Since $p = 1-p = 1/2$ here, the factor $p^{2}(1-p)$ collapses to $(1/2)^{3}$.

**Example 3.** A perfect run of 10 guesses at $p = 0.25$ is $(0.25)^{10} \approx 9.54 \times 10^{-7}$. Near a round percentage cutoff, compute $p^{n}$ exactly instead of estimating by eye, because high powers shrink a probability much faster than intuition suggests.

## 13.4 Mean and variance

The mean, variance, and standard deviation summarize the distribution without listing every individual probability.

$$
E[X] = np
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

The **mean** $E[X]$, also called the expected value, is the average number of successes over many repetitions of the whole experiment. It does not have to be a value one experiment could produce, so a mean of $2.5$ out of $10$ is a long-run average rather than a possible score. The formula makes sense once you notice that each trial contributes on average $p$ successes and there are $n$ trials.

The **variance** measures spread, meaning how far results usually fall from the mean. It comes in squared units, which is awkward to read, so we often prefer the **standard deviation**. That is the square root of the variance and it uses the same units as $X$, so it answers how many successes a typical result sits away from the mean.

Variance can also be written from the general discrete definition $\sum (x - \mu)^{2} P(X = x)$. For a binomial variable that expression simplifies to $np(1-p)$, and in practice you use the simplified form.

**Key relationship.** For a fixed $n$, the product $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$ and gets smaller as $p$ moves toward $0$ or $1$. So variance is largest when $p$ is closest to $0.5$, no matter whether that $p$ counts as “high” or “low.” A probability near $0$ or $1$ makes each trial almost predictable, which leaves little room for variation, while $p = 0.5$ makes both outcomes equally plausible. In numbers, $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$ against $0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.09$.

When two sides share the same $n$:

- Mean difference: $E[B] - E[A] = n(p_B - p_A)$
- Mean ratio: $E[B]/E[A] = p_B/p_A$

The shared $n$ cancels in the ratio, so that shortcut only works when both sides really have the same number of trials.

**Example 1.** Two students each answer 20 questions. Student A has $p = 0.50$; Student B has $p = 0.80$.

- Mean A: $20 \times 0.50 = 10$; mean B: $20 \times 0.80 = 16$
- Variance A: $20 \times 0.50 \times 0.50 = 5.0$; variance B: $20 \times 0.80 \times 0.20 = 3.2$

Student A’s scores vary more even though the mean is lower, because $p = 0.50$ sits exactly at maximum variance while $0.80$ is further from $0.5$ and therefore more predictable.

**Example 2.** On a 10-question quiz with $p = 0.25$, $E[X] = 2.5$. With $p = 0.90$, $E[X] = 9$. The gap is $n(0.90 - 0.25) = 6.5$.

**Example 3.** An inspector with $n = 20$, $p = 0.75$ has mean $15$. If certification needs at least 18 correct, the average sits below the bar. That comparison uses only $np$ and needs no probability calculation.

## 13.5 Cumulative probabilities and exam wording

Statements in this exam format usually describe a range of outcomes in words rather than in notation. The translation depends only on the wording, not on the values of $n$ or $p$.

| Wording | Notation |
| --- | --- |
| “at least $k$” | $\sum_{x=k}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| “at most $k$” | $\sum_{x=0}^{k} P(X = x)$ |
| “more than $k$” | $\sum_{x=k+1}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| “fewer than $k$” | $\sum_{x=0}^{k-1} P(X = x)$ |
| “between $a$ and $b$, inclusive” | $\sum_{x=a}^{b} P(X = x)$ |
| “exactly $k$” | $P(X = k)$, a single term |

These are sums because only one value of $X$ can actually happen. The count cannot be both $7$ and $8$, and probabilities of outcomes that exclude each other add up. So “at least 7 out of 10” is the combined probability of $7$, $8$, $9$ and $10$, never a single term.

“At least $k$” includes $k$, while “more than $k$” starts at $k+1$. Complements help on long tails: $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1)$.

That rule works because all values together carry probability $1$. Watch where the opposite event stops. The opposite of $X \ge k$ is $X \le k-1$, not $X \le k$, because $k$ belongs to the original event. The same idea gives the most common shortcut in the chapter, $P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (1-p)^{n}$.

**Percentage thresholds.** When a threshold is given as a percentage of $n$, it often lands between whole numbers. For example, 90% of 18 units is $16.2$. The count has to be an integer, so “at least 90%” means rounding $16.2$ **up** to $17$, the smallest integer that still meets the requirement. “At most” thresholds round **down**. Rounding the wrong way changes the summation range. Notice that $16$ units is only $88.9\%$ of $18$ and fails the requirement, so rounding to the nearest integer would give the wrong range.

**Example 1.** Using $n = 10$, $p = 0.90$, the probability of at least 7 correct:

$$
\begin{align*}
P(X=7) &\approx 0.0574 \\
P(X=8) &\approx 0.1937 \\
P(X=9) &\approx 0.3874 \\
P(X=10) &\approx 0.3487 \\
P(X \ge 7) &\approx 0.9872 \approx 98.72\%.
\end{align*}
$$

Each line is one application of the formula from §13.3, and the last line adds them because the outcomes exclude each other.

**Example 2.** With $n = 5$, $p = 0.2$: $P(X \ge 1) = 1 - (0.8)^{5} \approx 0.672$. Summing the terms for $1$ through $5$ gives the same value much more slowly.

**Example 3.** For $n = 10$, “more than 8” means $X \ge 9$, while “at least 8” means $X \ge 8$. The gap is one whole term, $P(X = 8)$, which is often large enough to flip a true/false verdict.

## 13.6 Comparing two distributions

Many exam questions put two cases side by side with the same binomial structure but different values of $p$, then ask for a comparison. The comparison usually takes one of these four forms.

**Ratio.** “$B$ is $X$ times as likely as $A$.” Compute both probabilities in full, then divide. Do not round to the nearest whole number before comparing with the stated threshold. Order matters, since the case named first goes in the numerator, and swapping them gives the reciprocal and the opposite verdict.

**Difference.** “$B$ exceeds $A$ by more than $X$.” Subtract directly. For two means with the same $n$, the difference simplifies to $n(p_B - p_A)$.

**Percentage increase.** “$B$ is $X\%$ higher than $A$.” Compute the ratio of the two means, subtract $1$, and multiply by $100$. When $n$ is shared, this equals the ratio of the two $p$-values. Subtracting $1$ is what turns a ratio into an increase, so a ratio of $1.6$ is a $60\%$ increase and not $160\%$.

**Variance or standard deviation ratio.** Compute $np(1-p)$ separately for each case before dividing. Take the square root only if the comparison is about standard deviation rather than variance.

**Example 1.** Using the students from §13.4 ($n = 20$), the percentage by which B’s mean exceeds A’s mean is $(16/10 - 1) \times 100 = 60\%$.

**Example 2.** Free throws, $n = 10$, $p_A = 0.55$, $p_B = 0.85$. Perfect run for B: $(0.85)^{10} \approx 0.1969$, just under 20%. Mean gap: $10(0.85 - 0.55) = 3$. Rookie SD is larger because $0.55$ is closer to $0.5$. Since $0.1969$ sits just below the round threshold $0.20$, a claim of “more than a 20% chance of a perfect run” is false by a narrow margin, and that margin is exactly what estimation cannot settle.

**Example 3.** If $P_A(\text{pass}) \approx 0.35\%$ and $P_B(\text{pass}) \approx 98.72\%$, then $P_B/P_A \approx 282$. A claim of “at least 300 times as likely” is false. Compute the ratio, do not round it up, and never replace a ratio of probabilities by $p_B/p_A$.

## 13.7 Common errors

These mistakes account for most of the wrong answers on this exam format.

1. **Estimating ratio thresholds instead of computing them.** A claim such as “more than 20 times as likely” is usually placed close to the true ratio, for example at $19.7$ or $20.3$. Estimating instead of computing gives the wrong answer.

2. **Assuming a higher mean implies a higher variance.** The case with the higher success probability, further from $p = 0.5$, often has the lower variance. Variance depends on distance from $0.5$, not on which value of $p$ looks like better performance.

3. **Overlooking the symmetry between $p$ and $1-p$.** For the same $n$, a binomial with parameter $p$ has the same variance as one with parameter $1-p$, because $p(1-p)$ does not change when the two swap places. On top of that, $P(X = 0)$ at probability $p$ equals $P(X = n)$ at probability $1-p$, since relabelling the success turns “none” into “all”.

4. **Treating the sum of two different binomial variables as binomial.** If two independent processes have different success probabilities, their combined count does not follow a binomial with a pooled probability, even though the means still add. The true combined variance will usually differ from the variance of a pooled binomial with the same mean. Two counts do combine into one binomial when $p$ is identical on both sides, since then all $n_1 + n_2$ trials share a single $p$. With different probabilities there is no common $p$ to put in the formula.

5. **Checking only one side of the normal-approximation rule of thumb.** The rule needs both $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$. A large value on one side does not make up for a small value on the other.

**Example 1.** Line A: $n_1 = 10$, $p_1 = 0.9$. Line B: $n_2 = 10$, $p_2 = 0.6$. Combined mean $15$, pooled $\bar{p} = 0.75$. True variance $3.3$, while pooled $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$ gives $3.75$. So the combined count is not $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$. The true figure adds the separate variances $10(0.9)(0.1) = 0.9$ and $10(0.6)(0.4) = 2.4$, which shows that identical means do not make distributions identical.

**Example 2.** Same $n$, $p = 0.2$ and $p = 0.8$ share the same variance $np(1-p)$, which illustrates the $p$ and $1-p$ symmetry.

**Example 3.** A ratio claim with a round multiplier near the true value is built to punish estimation, so always divide the two computed probabilities.

## 13.8 Normal approximation

For large $n$, computing the exact binomial probability term by term becomes impractical. The normal approximation is used, subject to a validity condition:

$$
X \approx \mathcal{N}\big(np,\, np(1-p)\big)
\quad\text{when}\quad np \ge 5 \text{ and } n(1-p) \ge 5.
$$

A cumulative probability such as $P(X \ge 25)$ with $n = 50$ would need $26$ separate terms. When $n$ is large and both conditions hold, the binomial shape is close enough to a bell curve that we can switch to a normal distribution with the same mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$. The conditions keep the distribution from being squeezed against either end of its range. A very small $np$ piles probability near $0$ successes, a very small $n(1-p)$ does the same at the top, and each side has to be checked separately.

**Continuity correction:** for “at most $k$,” use $k + 0.5$; for “at least $k$,” use $k - 0.5$, before standardizing to a $z$-score.

The correction exists because $X$ counts while the normal curve is continuous. A single whole number has no width of its own, so it is treated as the interval from $k - 0.5$ to $k + 0.5$. Moving the boundary by $0.5$ in the direction that keeps $k$ inside the event is what matches the count being described. Standardizing then turns the boundary into a $z$-score, the number of standard deviations from the mean:

$$
z = \frac{\text{corrected boundary} - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
$$

**Example 1.** For $n = 40$, $p = 0.3$: $np = 12$ and $n(1-p) = 28$, both at least $5$, so the approximation is reasonable. For $n = 20$, $p = 0.05$: $np = 1$, so the normal approximation should not be used.

**Example 2.** For $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(50, 0.4)$ and $P(X \ge 25)$, mean $20$, variance $12$. With continuity correction use $P(Y \ge 24.5)$ and $z = (24.5 - 20)/\sqrt{12} \approx 1.30$. The boundary is $24.5$ rather than $25.5$ because “at least 25” includes the value $25$.

**Example 3.** A statement that applies the normal approximation after checking only $np \ge 5$, ignoring $n(1-p)$, is using the rule incorrectly.

## 13.9 Summary reference

| Task | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Verify the distribution is binomial | Confirm fixed $n$, binary outcome, constant $p$, independence. |
| “Exactly $k$” | $\binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ |
| “At least / at most / between” | Convert to a summation range per §13.5. |
| Percentage threshold given | Convert to a whole-number count; round up for “at least,” round down for “at most.” |
| “How many times as likely” | Compute both probabilities in full, then divide. |
| Which distribution has greater spread | The $p$-value closer to $0.5$ yields the greater variance, for equal $n$. |
| Large $n$, approximation needed | Normal approximation if $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$. |

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p), \qquad \mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

**Working order for a statement.** Say what counts as a success and write down $n$ and $p$. Check the four conditions from §13.2. Translate the wording into $=$, $\le$ or $\ge$ with the table in §13.5. Decide whether the statement needs a single term, a sum, a mean, a variance, a ratio, or an approximation. Compute without rounding along the way, especially against a round threshold. Then compare your value with the exact claim before answering.
