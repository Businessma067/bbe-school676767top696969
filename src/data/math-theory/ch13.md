# Chapter 13 — Binomial distribution

This guide covers the binomial probability distribution as used in the BBE exam format: a fixed number of independent trials, each with the same probability of success. It presents the required conditions, the probability mass function, the mean and variance, the translation of exam wording into cumulative sums, the standard comparisons made between two distributions, common sources of error, and the normal approximation used for large samples.

## Learning objectives

- Define a binomial count and identify success versus failure.
- Check the four required conditions before applying the formula.
- Compute $P(X = k)$ with the probability mass function.
- Use the mean and variance, including the link between variance and distance from $p = 0.5$.
- Translate exam wording into the correct summation range.
- Compare two binomial distributions (ratio, difference, percentage increase, spread).
- Avoid the common errors listed in this chapter, and apply the normal approximation when both rule-of-thumb conditions hold.

---

## 13.1 Definition

The binomial distribution describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials, where each trial has only two possible outcomes and the same probability of success. It is a discrete distribution: the random variable $X$, the number of successes, takes only integer values from $0$ to $n$.

**Success** — The outcome being counted, with fixed probability $p$ on each trial — for example, a correctly answered question, a made shot, or a correctly identified defect.

**Failure** — Any other outcome, with probability $1 - p$ on each trial. This is not evaluative; it denotes only the complement of the outcome being counted.

**Example.** Lena is preparing for a campus entrance quiz. The practice paper has 10 multiple-choice questions, each with 4 options and exactly one correct answer. She has not studied this topic yet, so on every question she picks an option at random.

Here the **trial** is one question. **Success** means she answers that question correctly; **failure** means she does not. There are $n = 10$ trials, and on each trial $p = 1/4 = 0.25$. The random variable $X$ is “how many of the 10 questions she gets right,” so $X$ can only be $0, 1, 2, \ldots, 10$. That integer count — not the list of which questions she got right — is what the binomial distribution describes.

Notice that “success” is just a label for the event we count. If a factory later counts defective lamps, “success” might mean “defective.” The math is the same; only the story behind $p$ changes.

## 13.2 Required conditions

A scenario must satisfy the following four conditions before the binomial formula can be applied.

| Condition | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed $n$ | The number of trials is determined in advance. |
| Binary outcome | Each trial results in exactly one of two outcomes. |
| Constant $p$ | The probability of success is identical on every trial. |
| Independence | The outcome of one trial does not affect another. |

If any condition fails, do not force a $\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ model. Sampling without replacement from a small deck makes $p$ drift and breaks independence. Two processes with different success probabilities cannot be treated as one binomial with a single pooled $p$ (see Common errors).

**Example.** A bike-share depot checks each returned bike for a working front light before putting it back on the rack. Tonight the depot plans to check exactly 20 bikes. For this fleet, historical data say that a randomly chosen bike has a working light with probability $0.85$, and one bike’s light does not change another’s.

Check the four conditions in that story:

1. **Fixed $n$** — they decided in advance to inspect 20 bikes.  
2. **Binary outcome** — each bike either has a working light or it does not.  
3. **Constant $p$** — every bike is treated as having the same $0.85$ chance.  
4. **Independence** — knowing bike 3’s light status does not change bike 4’s chance.

So if $X$ is the number of bikes with a working light among the 20, then $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.85)$ is a fair model.

Contrast that with drawing 5 cards from a shuffled deck and counting aces **without replacement**: after you draw an ace, fewer aces remain, so $p$ changes and draws are dependent. That story fails the binomial conditions even though each card is still “ace or not.”

## 13.3 Probability mass function

The probability of obtaining exactly $k$ successes in $n$ trials is

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n.
$$

$\binom{n}{k}$ (also written $C(n,k)$, “$n$ choose $k$”) equals the number of ways to select $k$ successes among $n$ trials:

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

It does not depend on which specific trials succeed, only on the count. The factor $p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of any one specific ordering: $k$ independent successes and $n-k$ independent failures.

Useful shortcuts:

- All successes: $P(X = n) = p^{n}$
- All failures: $P(X = 0) = (1-p)^{n}$
- Exactly one success: $P(X = 1) = n\, p\, (1-p)^{n-1}$

**Example.** Back to Lena’s 10-question guessing paper ($n = 10$, $p = 0.25$). She wonders: “What is the chance I get **exactly three** questions right?”

That is $P(X = 3)$. First count how many patterns have three correct and seven wrong:

$$
\binom{10}{3} = \frac{10 \times 9 \times 8}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = 120.
$$

One specific pattern with three correct answers has probability

$$
(0.25)^{3}(0.75)^{7} = 0.015625 \times 0.75^{7} \approx 0.015625 \times 0.133484 \approx 0.002086.
$$

Multiply by the 120 patterns:

$$
P(X = 3) = 120 \times 0.002086 \approx 0.2503 \approx 25.03\%.
$$

So about one guessing attempt in four yields exactly three correct answers. The same method answers “exactly 0,” “exactly 10,” or any other single $k$ — only the numbers inside the formula change.

## 13.4 Mean and variance

The mean, variance, and standard deviation summarize the distribution without listing every individual probability.

$$
E[X] = np
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)
$$

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$

Variance can also be written from the general discrete definition $\sum (x - \mu)^{2} P(X = x)$; for a binomial variable that expression simplifies to $np(1-p)$. In practice you use the simplified form.

**Key relationship.** For a fixed $n$, the product $p(1-p)$ is maximized at $p = 0.5$ and decreases as $p$ moves toward $0$ or $1$. Consequently, variance is largest when $p$ is closest to $0.5$, regardless of whether $p$ is otherwise “high” or “low.”

When two sides share the same $n$:

- Mean difference: $E[B] - E[A] = n(p_B - p_A)$
- Mean ratio: $E[B]/E[A] = p_B/p_A$

**Example.** A café runs a loyalty stamp card: each visit, a customer either pays with the loyalty app (success) or with cash/card (failure). On a busy Saturday the café expects $n = 20$ customers. Mira, a new barista, sees that about half the customers use the app ($p = 0.50$). Omar, who works evenings in a student neighbourhood, sees about $80\%$ app payments ($p = 0.80$).

For Mira’s shift:

$$
E[X_M] = 20 \times 0.50 = 10, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_M) = 20 \times 0.50 \times 0.50 = 5.0.
$$

For Omar’s shift:

$$
E[X_O] = 20 \times 0.80 = 16, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_O) = 20 \times 0.80 \times 0.20 = 3.2.
$$

Omar’s typical count is higher (16 vs 10), but Mira’s count **spreads more**. That matches the key relationship: $p = 0.50$ is right at the peak of $p(1-p)$, while $p = 0.80$ is farther from $0.5$, so Omar’s evenings are steadier even though more customers use the app.

The manager does not need every probability $P(X = k)$ to see this — only the mean and variance formulas.

## 13.5 Cumulative probabilities and exam wording

Statements in this exam format frequently describe a range of outcomes in words rather than in mathematical notation. The translation depends only on the wording, not on the values of $n$ or $p$.

| Wording | Notation |
| --- | --- |
| “at least $k$” | $\sum_{x=k}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| “at most $k$” | $\sum_{x=0}^{k} P(X = x)$ |
| “more than $k$” | $\sum_{x=k+1}^{n} P(X = x)$ |
| “fewer than $k$” | $\sum_{x=0}^{k-1} P(X = x)$ |
| “between $a$ and $b$, inclusive” | $\sum_{x=a}^{b} P(X = x)$ |
| “exactly $k$” | $P(X = k)$, a single term |

“At least $k$” includes $k$; “more than $k$” starts at $k+1$. Complements help on long tails: $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1)$.

**Percentage thresholds.** When a threshold is expressed as a percentage of $n$, it frequently does not correspond to a whole number. For example, 90% of 18 units is $16.2$. Since the count must be an integer, “at least 90%” requires rounding $16.2$ **up** to $17$ (the smallest integer that still meets the requirement). Conversely, “at most” thresholds round **down**. Rounding in the wrong direction changes the summation range.

**Example.** After a week of studying, Lena retakes a similar 10-question paper. Now she estimates $p = 0.90$ of knowing each question. The pass mark is **at least 7 out of 10**.

“At least 7” is not one outcome — it is the mutually exclusive counts $X = 7, 8, 9, 10$. So

$$
P(X \ge 7) = P(7) + P(8) + P(9) + P(10).
$$

Using the binomial PMF with $n = 10$, $p = 0.90$:

$$
\begin{align*}
P(X=7) &\approx 0.0574, \\
P(X=8) &\approx 0.1937, \\
P(X=9) &\approx 0.3874, \\
P(X=10) &\approx 0.3487.
\end{align*}
$$

Adding them gives $P(X \ge 7) \approx 0.9872$, about a **98.72%** chance of passing. A single term such as $P(X = 7)$ would badly understate her pass chance, because scoring 8, 9, or 10 also counts as passing.

If the rule had been “more than 7,” the sum would start at $8$ instead — the English boundary matters as much as the algebra.

## 13.6 Comparing two distributions

Many exam questions present two entities with a shared binomial structure but different values of $p$, and ask for a comparison. The comparison generally takes one of the following four forms.

**Ratio** — “$B$ is $X$ times as likely as $A$.” Compute both probabilities in full, then divide. Do not round to the nearest whole number before comparing to the stated threshold.

**Difference** — “$B$ exceeds $A$ by more than $X$.” Subtract directly. For two means with the same $n$, the difference simplifies to $n(p_B - p_A)$.

**Percentage increase** — “$B$ is $X\%$ higher than $A$.” Compute the ratio of the two means, subtract $1$, and multiply by $100$. When $n$ is shared, this equals the ratio of the two $p$-values.

**Variance or standard deviation ratio** — Compute $np(1-p)$ separately for each entity before dividing. Take the square root only if the comparison concerns standard deviation rather than variance.

**Example.** Two friends play a free-throw challenge: each takes $n = 10$ shots. Alex is a weekend player with make probability $p_A = 0.55$. Blake is a club player with $p_B = 0.85$. A “clutch” bonus goes to anyone who makes **at least 8** of the 10.

Several comparisons fall out of the same story:

- **Perfect run.** Blake’s chance of making all 10 is $(0.85)^{10} \approx 0.1969$, just under 20%. A claim that Blake has “more than a 20% chance of a perfect 10” is false — you must compute $p^{n}$, not round by feel.  
- **Mean difference.** $E[B] - E[A] = 10(0.85 - 0.55) = 3$, so Blake expects exactly three more makes than Alex.  
- **Spread.** $\mathrm{SD}(A) = \sqrt{10 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} \approx 1.57$ while $\mathrm{SD}(B) \approx 1.13$. Alex’s results bounce around more because $0.55$ sits closer to $0.5$ than $0.85$ does.  
- **Ratio of pass chances.** If $P_A(\text{clutch}) \approx 9.96\%$ and $P_B(\text{clutch}) \approx 82.02\%$, then $P_B/P_A \approx 8.24$. A claim “Blake is more than 9 times as likely to earn the clutch bonus” is false, even though 8.24 feels “about 9.”

One real situation therefore trains all four comparison types the exam likes to mix.

## 13.7 Common errors

The following errors account for the majority of incorrect answers on this exam format.

1. **Estimating ratio thresholds instead of computing them.** A claim such as “more than 20 times as likely” is frequently placed close to the true ratio (for example, $19.7$ or $20.3$). Estimating rather than computing produces the wrong answer.

2. **Assuming a higher mean implies a higher variance.** The entity with the higher success probability (further from $p = 0.5$) frequently has the lower variance. Variance depends on distance from $0.5$, not on which value of $p$ corresponds to better performance.

3. **Overlooking the symmetry between $p$ and $1-p$.** A binomial with parameter $p$ has the same variance as one with parameter $1-p$ for the same $n$. Additionally, $P(X = 0)$ at probability $p$ equals $P(X = n)$ at probability $1-p$.

4. **Treating the sum of two different binomial variables as binomial.** If two independent processes have different success probabilities, their combined count does not follow a binomial with a pooled probability, even though the means still add. The true combined variance will generally not equal the variance of a pooled binomial with the same mean.

5. **Checking only one side of the normal-approximation rule of thumb.** The rule requires both $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$. A large value on one side does not compensate for a small value on the other.

**Example.** A small electronics shop runs two repair benches in parallel. Bench A finishes a phone screen repair on time with probability $p_A = 0.90$; Bench B, staffed by a trainee, has $p_B = 0.60$. Each bench completes $n = 10$ booked jobs today, independently. The owner wants the **total** number of on-time repairs $S = X_A + X_B$.

The means still add: $E[S] = 10 \times 0.90 + 10 \times 0.60 = 15$. Someone might invent a pooled rate $\bar{p} = 15/20 = 0.75$ and claim $S \sim \mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$. That is error 4.

True variance (sum of the two line variances):

$$
\mathrm{Var}(S) = 10 \times 0.90 \times 0.10 + 10 \times 0.60 \times 0.40 = 0.9 + 2.4 = 3.3.
$$

A fake pooled binomial $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$ would have variance $20 \times 0.75 \times 0.25 = 3.75$. Same mean, different spread — so $S$ is **not** binomial with the pooled $p$. The owner can still use $E[S] = 15$ for staffing, but must not treat the combined count as $\mathrm{Bin}(20, 0.75)$ on an exam-style claim.

## 13.8 Normal approximation

For large $n$, computing the exact binomial probability term by term becomes impractical. The normal approximation is used, subject to a validity condition:

$$
X \approx \mathcal{N}\big(np,\, np(1-p)\big)
\quad\text{when}\quad np \ge 5 \text{ and } n(1-p) \ge 5.
$$

**Continuity correction:** for “at most $k$,” use $k + 0.5$; for “at least $k$,” use $k - 0.5$, before standardizing to a $z$-score.

**Example.** A warehouse ships $n = 50$ online orders in an hour. Historically, about $40\%$ of orders need a handwritten customs note ($p = 0.40$). Let $X$ be the number of orders that need a note. The supervisor asks whether it is reasonable that **at least 25** notes will be needed.

First check the rule of thumb: $np = 20$ and $n(1-p) = 30$, both at least $5$, so a normal approximation is allowed. Mean $20$, variance $np(1-p) = 12$, $\mathrm{SD} = \sqrt{12} \approx 3.46$.

Because $X$ is discrete, apply the continuity correction for “at least 25”:

$$
P(X \ge 25) \approx P(Y \ge 24.5), \qquad Y \sim \mathcal{N}(20, 12),
$$

$$
z = \frac{24.5 - 20}{\sqrt{12}} \approx 1.30.
$$

From a standard normal table or calculator, $P(Z \ge 1.30)$ is the approximate chance. The point of the story is the workflow: check both $np$ and $n(1-p)$, then correct the boundary, then standardize — skipping the first check is exactly common error 5.

If instead only $n = 20$ packages had $p = 0.05$ of needing a rare sticker, then $np = 1 < 5$ and the normal shortcut would be inappropriate for that quieter warehouse hour.

## 13.9 Summary reference

| Task | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Verify the distribution is binomial | Confirm fixed $n$, binary outcome, constant $p$, independence. |
| “Exactly $k$” | $\binom{n}{k} p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ |
| “At least / at most / between” | Convert to a summation range per §13.5. |
| Percentage threshold given | Convert to a whole-number count; round up for “at least,” round down for “at most.” |
| “How many times as likely” | Compute both probabilities in full, then divide. |
| Which distribution has greater spread | The $p$-value closer to $0.5$ yields the greater variance, for equal $n$. |
| Large $n$, approximation needed | Normal approximation if $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$. |

$$
E[X] = np, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p), \qquad \mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}
$$
